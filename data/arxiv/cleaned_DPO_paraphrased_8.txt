\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and develop some reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their outputs remains challenging due to the entirely unsupervised training regime. Current approaches to achieve such controllability involve collecting human annotations that compare the quality of model outputs, followed by fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to better align with these human preferences, frequently using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process, which first requires training a reward model to capture human preferences, and then applying reinforcement learning to the large unsupervised LM to maximize this learned reward, while preventing significant deviation from the original model. \rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel reward model parameterization within RLHF that allows us to directly derive the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, enabling the standard RLHF objective to be addressed using only a straightforward classification loss.} Our approach, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} performing LM sampling during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter adjustments. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to better align with human preferences, matching or outperforming existing methods. In particular, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs and achieves comparable or improved performance in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being much simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on vast corpora develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data, which reflects a diverse set of aims, priorities, and expertise. Not all of these aspects are beneficial to replicate; for instance, while we want an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors to fix them, we also prefer the model to favor producing (possibly rare) high-quality code examples from its training. Likewise, a language model might need to be \textit{cognizant} of a misconception held by roughly half of the population, but it should definitely not assert that misconception as true in half of its responses. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential to creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and manageable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While current approaches commonly guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective traditionally used can be precisely optimized through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current techniques embed desired behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect the kinds of behaviors humans consider safe and beneficial. This phase of learning preferences takes place following an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training on an extensive text corpus. Although the simplest strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human demonstrations of exemplary responses, the most effective methods rely on reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on a dataset of human preferences, and reinforcement learning is then applied to optimize the language model’s policy to generate responses with high reward scores while preventing the model from diverging too much from its original parameters. Despite RLHF yielding models with notable conversational and coding performance, the overall RLHF framework is substantially more complex than supervised learning, requiring training of multiple language models and incorporating sampling from the LM policy during training, which results in considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly train a language model to align with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly targets the same goal as traditional RLHF approaches (maximizing reward while enforcing a KL-divergence constraint) but offers a simpler implementation and more straightforward training process. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log likelihood of preferred responses over dispreferred ones, while applying a dynamic, per-instance importance weight that helps avoid the model collapse observed when using a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to prior techniques, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (like the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a candidate reward function matches observed preference data. Yet, unlike existing methods that utilize the preference model to construct a loss for training a reward model and subsequently optimize a policy against that reward, DPO employs a variable substitution to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. Consequently, given a dataset of human preference comparisons between model outputs, DPO can optimize the policy by minimizing a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without learning an explicit, standalone reward model or sampling from the policy during training}{producing the optimal policy to an implicit reward function fit to the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs comparably or better than current techniques, including PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences in tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that grow in size can perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or by leveraging few-shot prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals can be notably enhanced by fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, often referred to as `instruction-tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the specific instructions seen during tuning, thereby improving their overall utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has proven successful, it is often simpler to gather \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality than to obtain expert demonstrations. Consequently, later research has focused on fine-tuning LLMs using datasets that capture human preferences, leading to improvements in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically begin by training a neural reward model to align with preference data under a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. Then, the language model is fine-tuned to maximize this learned reward using reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction uses instruction-following LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback to create synthetic preference datasets targeting specific traits like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak human supervision provided as textual rubrics for the LLM’s annotations. These methodologies illustrate a fusion of two research areas: one focusing on training language models with reinforcement learning for diverse goals \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general strategies for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of employing relative human preference data, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a significant practical hurdle; this paper proposes a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without resorting to RL.

Beyond the scope of language, the problem of deriving policies from preference data has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methods proposed. Learning in contextual bandits by utilizing preferences or rankings over actions, as opposed to relying on rewards, is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, the theoretical framework for CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that achieves an expected winning probability of at least 50\% against \textit{any} competing policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, within the CDB paradigm, preference labels are collected in an online fashion, whereas learning from human preferences typically involves training on a fixed, offline dataset of preference-annotated action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) utilizes binary preferences produced by an \textit{unknown} latent ‘scoring’ function rather than explicit reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Several PbRL algorithms exist, including those capable of leveraging off-policy preference data; these usually first estimate the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) and then proceed to optimize it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a one-step policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to align with the given preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework as presented by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and further expanded upon in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process generally consists of three main steps: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preferences and learning a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with supervised fine-tuning of a pre-trained language model on carefully curated, high-quality datasets relevant to the target tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate their preference for one response, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ refer to the favored and less favored completions out of $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these preferences originate from an underlying, unobservable reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Several methods exist for capturing these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a widely used approach (though broader Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} also fit within this framework if multiple ranked outputs are available). According to the BT model, the human preference probability distribution $p^*$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparison samples $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can parameterize a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and learn its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. By treating this as a binary classification task, the corresponding negative log-likelihood loss becomes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In the setting of language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented by a linear projection layer placed on top of the final transformer output to yield a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward estimates, previous work often normalizes the rewards so that they satisfy $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}[r_\phi(x, y)] = 0$ across all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the trained reward function provides feedback to guide the language model. Building on previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates the extent to which the updated policy $\pi_{\theta}$ diverges from the base reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$.  
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ starts from $\pisft$. This regularization is crucial because it limits the policy's departure from the distribution on which the reward model is reliable, while also preserving output variety and avoiding collapse into producing only a few high-reward responses. Since language generation is discrete, this objective is non-differentiable and is therefore optimized using reinforcement learning methods. A common technique \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it via PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that utilizes preferences directly. In contrast to earlier RLHF approaches, which first estimate a reward function and subsequently optimize it through reinforcement learning, our method \rev{eliminates the intermediate reward modeling step and directly trains a language model from preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that permits the derivation of its optimal policy in closed form, removing the need for a reinforcement learning training loop}.  
As we will explain in detail shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, allowing us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies.  
This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to bypass explicit reward model fitting}{circumvents the necessity of training a separate, explicit reward model}, while still optimizing according to established models of human preferences, like the Bradley-Terry model. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously encodes both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the identical reinforcement learning (RL) objective as in previous studies, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, assuming a general reward function $r$. Building upon earlier works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ represents the partition function. For a detailed derivation, refer to Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the MLE approximation $r_{\phi}$ of the true reward function $r^*$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting the practicality of this formulation. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of its associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebraic manipulations yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ along with its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Conveniently, the Bradley-Terry model depends solely on the difference between rewards of two outputs, namely, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function cancels out, enabling the human preference probability to be represented exclusively via the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The complete derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous formulations can be derived for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (i.e., Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to omit the explicit step of reward modeling and simultaneously avoid the complexity of reinforcement learning optimization.}{Here, we implicitly learn a reward through an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. We expand upon the theoretical aspects of DPO and its connections to related work in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference policy $\piref$ (details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ serves to increase the likelihood assigned to preferred outputs $y_w$ while lowering the likelihood of less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each example is weighted based on how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the reward for disfavored completions, scaled by $\beta$, reflecting the magnitude of the KL penalty. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting factor, since a straightforward version of the approach without this coefficient can lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw sample completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ and annotate them with human preferences to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference model $\piref$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a specified $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting human feedback. Because these datasets are typically generated from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not present, we initialize $\piref$ by performing maximum likelihood estimation over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e.,  
${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \pi(y_w \mid x) \right]}$.  
This approach reduces the distribution mismatch between the true reference distribution, which is unknown, and the $\piref$ employed by DPO. Additional implementation details and hyperparameter settings are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper understanding of the DPO approach, present its theoretical justification, and connect the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered by actor-critic algorithms applied in RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Hidden Reward Model}  
DPO manages to avoid both \rev{fitting an explicit reward}{explicit reward estimation} and the use of reinforcement learning by training the policy through a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model, where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Our goal is to optimize the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$, which aligns with optimizing the reward model from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after applying an appropriate variable substitution. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundations for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the space of reward models that can be learned, and prove it enables exact retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class generate an identical optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The demonstrations for these lemmas are straightforward and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma highlights a familiar under-specification problem inherent in the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this ambiguity, it is generally necessary to introduce further identifiability constraints to secure any theoretical assurances regarding the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all reward functions within the same equivalence class lead to the same optimal policy; therefore, our ultimate goal focuses on recovering any reward function from this optimal set. The subsequent theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Provided certain mild conditions, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model (particularly the Bradley-Terry variant) can be expressed through the reparameterization
    \[
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}
    \]
    for some policy model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a given reference distribution $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that induces an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that there exists a reward function within $r$'s equivalence class representable by the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y\mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since the normalization depends solely on the prefix $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains a reward function within the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ by the right-hand side expression in Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward function), we obtain 
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}.
\]
Thus, $f$ maps $r$ to an equivalent reward function in the desired reparameterized form, confirming that our chosen parameterization does not restrict generality.
\end{sproof}

Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as identifying which specific reward function within an equivalence class the DPO reparameterization selects, namely the one satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y\mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{= \pi(y\mid x) \text{ by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
ensuring that $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a valid probability distribution (all probabilities positive and summing to unity).
Moreover, from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds exactly to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by $r(x, y)$.
The crucial insight behind the DPO method is that by imposing particular constraints on the inherently under-specified Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) preference models, we can retain the representable reward class while making the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly tractable for every input $x$.

\subsection{Unstable Behavior in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our analytical framework can also be applied to identify sources of instability in conventional actor-critic algorithms commonly employed in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF workflow, concentrating specifically on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This setup allows us to link our problem to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} applied to the constrained RL formulation given in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} and induced by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this objective can be expressed as:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}  
This objective matches that used in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which optimize a DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization term inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not influence the optimal policy, omitting it may result in policy gradients with high variance, thereby destabilizing training. One approach to handle this normalization is to learn a value function explicitly, but such methods often face optimization challenges. Alternatively, previous methods have approximated this normalization using a baseline derived from human completions, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate. In contrast, the DPO formulation reparameterizes the reward so that no baseline is necessary.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to directly learn policies from preference data. Initially, within a controlled text generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances the objectives of maximizing reward and minimizing the KL divergence relative to a reference policy, especially when compared to standard preference-based methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more complex RLHF challenges, such as summarization and conversational tasks. Our results indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong benchmarks including RLHF using PPO, as well as outperforming the best trajectory among $N$ samples evaluated under a learned reward model. Prior to detailing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further specifics can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all experiments, the algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ corresponds to the beginning portion of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal of the policy is to produce an output $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable controlled evaluation, we \textit{create} preference pairs from generated outputs by applying a pre-trained sentiment classifier that satisfies $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned to convergence using the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional information is provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ represents a Reddit forum post, and the policy is tasked with generating a summary $y$ that captures the key points of the post. We utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-authored summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). The preference dataset was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from outputs of a distinct, but similarly trained, SFT model. Lastly, for the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ consists of a user query that can range from inquiries about astrophysics to requests for advice on relationships. The policy is required to generate a response $y$ that is both engaging and useful. For this, we employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170k exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each conversation concludes with two model-generated replies from a large, unspecified language model, accompanied by a label indicating which response was preferred by humans. Because no pre-trained SFT model exists for this setting, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model solely on the preferred responses to create the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximizations, within the controlled sentiment generation context, we measure performance by the frontier representing achieved reward versus KL-divergence from the reference policy; this is feasible because the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is accessible. Conversely, in practical scenarios where the true reward function is unavailable, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} relative to a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment on summary quality and helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, baseline comparisons utilize reference summaries from the test set, whereas for dialogue, the preferred test dataset responses serve as the baseline. Although prior research indicates that language models may serve as superior automated evaluators compared to traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human study to validate our use of GPT-4 for evaluation purposes in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings reveal a strong correlation between GPT-4’s assessments and human judgments, with human agreement with GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding inter-annotator agreement levels.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess multiple established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At a basic level, we investigate zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on the summarization task, and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue setting. Additionally, we examine the \textbf{SFT} model along with \textbf{Preferred-FT}, a model fine-tuned through supervised learning using the selected completion $y_w$ drawn either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another form of pseudo-supervised training is the \textbf{Unlikelihood} approach~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{decreasing} the probability of $y_l$; here, we incorporate an optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ on the unlikelihood component. We also explore \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, employing a reward function learned from preference annotations, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant trained using the true reward function accessible in the controlled sentiment scenario. For the sentiment experiments, we utilize two PPO-GT implementations: a standard off-the-shelf version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized variant that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance results (these adjustments are also applied when using conventional PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which samples $N$ candidate completions from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) and selects the response with the highest score according to a reward model trained on preference data. While this strong-performing approach separates reward model quality from PPO optimization, it becomes computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$, as it necessitates generating $N$ completions per input at inference time.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 on Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue data; among the methods tested, only DPO shows improvement compared to the chosen summaries in the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates measured at various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms the original dataset labels across different temperatures during the training process.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in standard RLHF methods aims to balance the pursuit of higher rewards with limiting how much the policy diverges from a baseline policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider not only the reward obtained but also the extent of KL divergence; obtaining marginally greater reward at the cost of substantially increased KL divergence may not be beneficial. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we conduct multiple training trials, each using a distinct hyperparameter setting governing policy conservatism (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, as well as varying random seeds for preferred-FT). This results in a total of 22 runs. At intervals of every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy on a predefined set of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward based on the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the cumulative per-timestep KL divergences.} between the policy and the reference, denoted as $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO establishes the most efficient frontier, attaining the highest rewards while maintaining relatively low KL divergence. This outcome is significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the identical objective, DPO demonstrates markedly greater efficiency, with its reward-KL balance clearly surpassing that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO in forming the frontier even when PPO has access to true reward signals (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to authentic preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
In this section, we investigate the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogues. For summarization, conventional automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous research has demonstrated that fine-tuning language models via PPO on human preference data can yield better summary quality. Our evaluation involves generating completions from the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate when compared to reference summaries. Completions for all approaches are generated using sampling temperatures ranging between 0.0 and 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT methods all fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Results show that DPO achieves around a 61\% win rate at a temperature of 0.0, outperforming PPO’s roughly 57\% win rate at its optimal temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO attains a higher peak win rate relative to the best-performing $N$ baseline. It is worth mentioning that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, so these outcomes might undervalue DPO’s true capabilities. Furthermore, DPO exhibits considerably greater stability across different sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose performance can drop to that of the base GPT-J model when sampling at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT offers minimal improvement over the SFT baseline. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we further present a direct comparison of DPO and PPO via human evaluations, where samples from DPO at a temperature of 0.25 were preferred 58\% of the time over PPO samples generated at temperature 0.

On single-turn dialogue tasks, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split, focusing on interactions involving one human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, we calculate the win rate of each method by referencing the preferred completions provided in the test set. Due to the absence of a standard SFT model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model. We then apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected preferred completions to ensure the outputs remain within the model’s distribution, followed by training with DPO. Our comparisons also include the top-performing result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline stabilizes at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the base Pythia-2.8B model. Results indicate that DPO matches or surpasses the performance of these methods when evaluated at their optimal temperature settings. Additionally, we examine an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but are unable to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the baseline Pythia-2.8B. Considering findings from TL;DR and the fact that both approaches optimize the identical reward, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. Overall, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient method that enhances the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, achieving results comparable or superior to the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\subsection{Assessing GPT-4 Evaluations Against Human Judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the accuracy of GPT-4’s assessments, we carried out a human evaluation study based on the TL;DR summarization task and utilized two distinct GPT-4 prompts. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, labeled as simple, merely asks which summary better captures the key information from the original post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt, described as concise, inquires not only which summary is more informative but also which one is more succinct. This second prompt was introduced because we observed that GPT-4, when responding to the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, tends to favor longer, somewhat repetitive summaries compared to human preferences. Complete prompt details are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. Our evaluation involves three pairwise comparisons: the top-performing method (DPO, temperature 0.25), the lowest-performing one (PPO, temperature 1.0), and a mid-level method (SFT, temperature 0.25). Each of these is benchmarked against PPO using greedy decoding at its optimal temperature setting. Results indicate that GPT-4’s agreement with human raters is roughly on par with human-to-human agreement across both prompts, implying GPT-4 serves as a viable stand-in for human evaluation (note that multiple human ratings were only gathered for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons due to limited annotator availability). Generally, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that align more closely with human judgments; consequently, this prompt is adopted for the primary analyses presented in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further information about the human evaluation procedure, including the rater interface and volunteer details, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach to develop capable and well-aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward training method that allows language models to be trained using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing preference learning into a conventional RL framework to apply existing RL techniques, DPO uncovers a direct relationship between language model policies and reward functions, enabling training that directly aligns the model with human preferences using a simple cross-entropy loss—eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO matches or surpasses the performance of current RLHF methods, including those based on PPO, thereby significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several critical questions that warrant further investigation. In particular, how well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios compared to approaches relying on explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may generalize on par with PPO-based approaches, though a more thorough analysis is required. For instance, can self-labeling strategies using the DPO policy effectively leverage unlabeled prompts in a similar manner? Moreover, it remains to be seen how reward over-optimization presents itself within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the modest performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right reflects this phenomenon. While our experiments cover models up to 6 billion parameters, extending DPO to scale with much larger, cutting-edge models represents a promising avenue for future work. Concerning evaluation methods, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are sensitive to the prompts used; thus, subsequent studies might explore optimal prompting techniques to obtain reliable automated evaluations. Lastly, beyond human preference-based language model training, DPO holds potential for broader applications, such as training generative models across different modalities.
