\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive general knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their outputs remains challenging because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current approaches to achieve such controllability gather human annotations that indicate preferences between model outputs and then fine-tune the unsupervised LM to better align with these preferences, frequently employing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is often complicated and unstable, involving initially training a reward model to capture human judgments, followed by reinforcement learning to fine-tune the LM to maximize this learned reward while preventing excessive deviation from the original model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward function within RLHF that permits direct computation of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling the standard RLHF task to be addressed using a straightforward classification loss.}  
The approach we introduce, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} performing LM sampling during fine-tuning, or engaging in extensive hyperparameter search. Our results demonstrate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences as well as or better than existing techniques. In particular, DPO-based fine-tuning surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and either matches or improves output quality in tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue, while also being significantly simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects a broad spectrum of intentions, priorities, and expertise levels. Some of these may be undesirable to replicate; for instance, while it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, when producing code, we prefer to steer the model toward the (possibly uncommon) examples of high-quality coding found in its training set. Likewise, we may want our language model to be \textit{cognizant} of a widespread misconception held by about 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want it to assert this false belief as true in half of the queries regarding it. Put differently, carefully choosing the model's \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its extensive \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are reliable, efficient, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current techniques generally guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL objective commonly employed can be precisely optimized through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current approaches embed the intended behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully curated collections of human preferences that reflect behaviors deemed safe and useful by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training conducted on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest technique for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on human-provided examples of high-quality outputs, the most effective category of methods utilizes reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model based on datasets of human preferences and subsequently apply reinforcement learning to optimize the language model’s policy, aiming to generate responses with high reward while avoiding substantial deviation from the original model. Despite RLHF producing models with strong conversational and coding skills, its pipeline is notably more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and incorporating policy sampling within the training loop, which results in considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly tailor a language model according to human preferences without relying on explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an approach that implicitly targets the same goal as conventional RLHF algorithms (maximizing reward while enforcing a KL-divergence constraint) but offers a more straightforward implementation and training process. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update enhances the relative log probability of favored responses over less preferred ones, while incorporating a dynamic, example-specific importance weight that mitigates the model collapse observed when using a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to prior methods, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference framework (for example, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a candidate reward function corresponds to observed preference data. Yet, unlike existing approaches that employ the preference model to create a preference loss for training a reward model followed by policy optimization on that learned reward, {\methodac} reformulates the preference loss directly as a function of the policy through a change of variables. With access to a dataset containing human preferences over model outputs, {\methodac} can thus optimize the policy using a simple binary cross-entropy objective, \rev{without requiring the explicit learning of a separate reward model or the need to sample from the policy during training}{yielding the optimal policy aligned with an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably or better than current approaches, including PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences across tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size are able to perform certain tasks without any examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or by utilizing only a few examples in prompts (few-shot) \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user intentions can be greatly enhanced through fine-tuning on datasets that contain instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize to new instructions beyond those seen during tuning, thereby improving their overall practical usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has proven effective, collecting \textit{relative} human evaluations of response quality tends to be easier than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has focused on fine-tuning LLMs using datasets built from human preferences, which has enhanced their performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by training a neural reward model to align with the human preference data under a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, then proceed to fine-tune the language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research avenue uses LLMs that have been instruction-tuned with human feedback to produce additional synthetic preference datasets targeting specific qualities, including safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak supervision from human-provided textual rubrics for annotation guidance. This line of work represents a synthesis of two research traditions: one focused on reinforcement learning for training language models with diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and another centered on general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human preference data, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a substantial practical obstacle; the present study introduces a theoretically sound method for optimizing relative preferences without the use of RL.

Beyond language-related tasks, learning policies based on preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to a variety of proposed methods. When preferences or rankings over actions replace rewards in contextual bandit problems, this setting is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute reward signals, the theoretical framework for CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose expected probability of winning against \textit{any} alternative policy is no less than 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nonetheless, unlike typical CDB scenarios where preference feedback is received sequentially (online), learning from human preferences usually relies on a static dataset of offline preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) involves learning from binary preference comparisons generated by an \textit{unknown} latent `scoring' function instead of direct reward observations \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Multiple PbRL algorithms have been developed, some capable of leveraging off-policy preference information; these methods typically entail explicitly estimating the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) before optimizing the policy \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, our approach introduces a unified, single-step policy optimization method that directly maximizes alignment with the observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We provide an overview of the RLHF process as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and further explored in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This approach generally consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) gathering preferences and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts by applying supervised fine-tuning to a pre-trained language model using carefully curated data related to the target downstream tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These answer pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate their preference between the two, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored response and $y_l$ is the less preferred one from $(y_1, y_2)$. It is presumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various methods exist to represent these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being widely adopted (although more flexible Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also fit within this framework when multiple ranked responses are available). The BT model expresses the human preference probability $p^*$ as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparison samples $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn according to $p^*$, we can define a parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and learn its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. Casting this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss function is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\bigr)\right]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model settings, the function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by adding a linear output layer on top of the final transformer block that outputs a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variance, prior research often normalizes the reward values such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been learned serves as a source of feedback for the language model. Consistent with previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the coefficient $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is allowed to diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ of the language model starts off as $\pisft$. Incorporating this constraint is crucial because it limits the policy shift away from the distribution where the reward model's predictions remain reliable, while also preserving output diversity and avoiding collapse to a few highly rewarded outputs. Since language generation is inherently discrete, this objective cannot be directly differentiated and is generally optimized through reinforcement learning methods. A common strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward function as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and then maximizing it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when implementing reinforcement learning algorithms on large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our aim is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that utilizes preferences directly. In contrast to previous RLHF techniques, which first learn a reward function and then apply RL to optimize it, our method \rev{eliminates the intermediate reward modeling phase and directly optimizes the language model using preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that permits derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, removing the need for an RL training cycle}.  
As will be detailed below, the core idea is to exploit an explicit analytical mapping from reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies, allowing us to recast a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies.  
This change-of-variables strategy \rev{enables us to bypass the step of explicit reward modeling}{prevents the necessity of fitting a separate, explicit reward model}, while still performing optimization based on established models of human preferences like the Bradley-Terry framework. Essentially, the policy network serves the dual role of representing both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning objective as established in earlier research, given by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, which is defined with respect to a general reward function $r$. Building on prior studies~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be easily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization constant, or partition function, is defined as $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \piref(y\mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A full derivation is available in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ as a proxy for the true reward $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting practical application of this formulation. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and manipulating the expression yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies to the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry preference model depends solely on the difference between rewards for two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model given in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} causes the partition function terms to cancel out, enabling the human preference probability to be represented entirely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$, under the Bradley-Terry framework, satisfies the preference likelihood:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The detailed derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} specifically employs the Bradley-Terry model, analogous formulations can be derived for more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the likelihood of human preference data with respect to the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to avoid explicitly modeling the reward function and also eliminates the necessity for reinforcement learning optimization.}{In this manner, we effectively fit an implicit reward through a different parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because our method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees such as consistency given appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate on the theoretical aspects of DPO and its connections to related work in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What is the role of the DPO update?} To gain a clear mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward estimate is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to increase the probability of the favored completions $y_w$ while decreasing the probability of the less favored ones $y_l$. Crucially, the contribution of each example is weighted by the extent to which the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ mistakenly favors the dispreferred completions, scaled by $\beta$—reflecting the intensity of the KL regularization. Our findings highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as a straightforward version of the approach that omits this coefficient can lead to deterioration of the language model’s performance (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow includes the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ and annotate them with human preference labels to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference model $\piref$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the target parameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting human annotations. Because these datasets are typically generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when possible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, formulated as ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This step helps reduce the distributional discrepancy between the inaccessible true reference distribution and the $\piref$ utilized during DPO training. Additional implementation details and hyperparameter settings are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, supply formal theoretical support, and connect the benefits of DPO to the limitations found in actor-critic algorithms commonly applied in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions Implicitly as a Reward Model}  
DPO manages to circumvent both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the process of fitting an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by employing a single maximum likelihood objective to learn the policy. It is important to observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, optimizing our parametric policy model $\pi_{\theta}$ is effectively equivalent to optimizing the reward model as expressed in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once we apply an appropriate change of variables. In this section, we will develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the set of possible learned reward functions, and confirm that it enables the exact retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$ for a certain function $f$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this definition forms an equivalence relation, dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can then be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry variant, any two reward functions belonging to the same category generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class generate the same optimal policy in the context of the constrained reinforcement learning problem.
\end{lemma}

The proofs are direct and are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-known under-identification problem associated with the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this under-identification, it is common practice to introduce extra identifiability constraints to ensure reliable guarantees for the MLE estimations derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function within a given equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy; therefore, our ultimate goal is to recover any single reward function from that optimal class. The following theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, all reward classes compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (specifically the Bradley-Terry variant) admit a representation through the reparameterization 
    \[
        r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)},
    \]
    for some model $\pi(y \mid x)$ and a fixed reference distribution $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as per Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that an equivalent reward function within the same class can be expressed by the reparameterization above. Define the projection operator $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator normalizes the reward function using the logarithm of the partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Because the normalization depends only on the prefix $x$, the transformed reward function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of the original $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}, which applies universally, yields
\[
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Hence, the projection $f$ constructs a reward function within $r$’s equivalence class in the desired form, ensuring that adopting this reparameterization in our reward model does not entail any loss of generality.
\end{sproof}

Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as precisely identifying which reward function within each equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization; namely, the reward function satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
    \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{= \pi(y \mid x) \text{ by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y \mid x)$ constitutes a valid probability distribution (all probabilities are positive and sum to one). In light of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds exactly to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by the reward $r(x, y)$.

The crucial insight behind the DPO algorithm is that by enforcing specific constraints on the inherently under-determined Plackett-Luce (and especially the Bradley-Terry) family of preference models, one can retain the representational capacity of the reward functions while simultaneously ensuring that the optimal policy characterized by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} remains analytically tractable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability in Actor-Critic Methods}

Our proposed framework can also help identify sources of instability present in conventional actor-critic methods commonly employed in RLHF, such as PPO. By following the RLHF workflow, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This approach allows us to relate the problem to the control-as-inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} within the constrained RL formulation given in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy defined by Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} and derived from the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective matches those optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that use the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class defined by $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not alter the optimal policy solution, omitting it can cause the policy gradient estimates to exhibit high variance, which may destabilize the training process. One way to handle this normalization is through a learned value function, although this approach often introduces optimization challenges. Alternatively, previous work has addressed reward normalization by subtracting a baseline derived from human completions, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization results in a reward function that eliminates the need for such baseline corrections.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO’s capability to train policies directly based on preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances the maximization of reward against the reduction of KL-divergence relative to a reference policy, in comparison to established preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines including RLHF with PPO, as well as outperforming the selection of the best trajectory among $N$ samples according to a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further information is available in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. In every experiment, the algorithms acquire a policy based on a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ consists of a movie review prefix taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce an output $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable a controlled evaluation, this task involves \textit{creating} preference pairs among generated outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned to convergence on training reviews from the IMDb dataset (additional information can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the case of \textbf{summarization}, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the model must generate a summary $y$ capturing the main ideas of the post. Following previous research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} alongside human preferences collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and trained with the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} framework for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The human preference data was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from outputs of a different, but similarly fine-tuned, SFT model. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, the input $x$ represents a human query that can range from questions about astrophysics to requests for relationship advice. The policy is tasked with generating a helpful and engaging response $y$ to the user’s prompt. We employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which consists of 170k exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two candidate responses produced by a large, though unspecified, language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating which response was favored by humans. Because no pre-trained SFT model exists for this setting, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred completions to develop the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximizaton objective, within the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm by the frontier defined by the obtained reward and the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this frontier can be computed since the ground-truth reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment regarding summary quality and helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, baseline comparisons use the reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the baseline consists of the preferred responses from the test data. Although prior work indicates that language models can outperform traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we also perform a human study to validate our use of GPT-4 for evaluation, detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show that GPT-4’s assessments align closely with human evaluations, with agreement levels between humans and GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding those observed between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of existing strategies for training language models to align with human preferences. At a basic level, we test zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue setting. Furthermore, we examine the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves fine-tuning via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$, drawn either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization tasks) or a general language model (for single-turn dialogue). Another semi-supervised technique is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which aims to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously decreasing the likelihood of $y_l$; this approach incorporates an optional weighting parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood component. We also explore \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, employing a reward function trained on preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that utilizes the true reward function accessible in the controlled sentiment environment. For our sentiment analysis experiments, we implement two versions of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to boost performance (these adjustments are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we evaluate the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach, which involves generating $N$ outputs from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selecting the top-scoring one based on a reward model trained on the preference dataset. Although this method effectively separates the reward model’s quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$ since it requires sampling $N$ completions per query during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for the Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue; among all methods, only DPO demonstrates improvement beyond the selected summaries in the Anthropic-HH test dataset. \textbf{Right.} Win rates tracked across various sampling temperatures during training. DPO consistently maintains its advantage over the dataset labels throughout training, regardless of the sampling temperature used.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The typical RLHF algorithms employ a KL-constrained reward maximization objective that strikes a balance between maximizing reward and preventing the policy from straying too far from the reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the reward attained and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a significantly larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for several algorithms in the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we conduct multiple training runs, each with a distinct hyperparameter controlling policy conservativeness (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and varying random seeds for preferred-FT). This hyperparameter sweep comprises a total of 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy on a fixed set of test prompts, calculating both the average reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, the sum of the per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy, denoted as $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO achieves the most efficient frontier by a considerable margin, securing the highest reward while maintaining low KL divergence. This outcome is particularly significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates substantially greater efficiency; its reward-KL trade-off clearly outperforms that of PPO. Secondly, DPO surpasses PPO's frontier even when PPO is given access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to authentic preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
In the following, we assess the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. When it comes to summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human preferences~\citep{stiennon2022learning}. Previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO on human preference data can yield more effective summaries. Our evaluation involves generating completions from the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate compared to the reference completions provided in the test set. For all methods, completions are sampled across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Results indicate that DPO achieves an approximate win rate of 61\% at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO’s best performance of about 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a higher peak win rate relative to the best-of-$N$ baseline. It is important to highlight that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, suggesting these outcomes might underrepresent its true capability. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates considerably greater robustness to variations in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose performance can decline to the baseline GPT-J model level at higher temperatures. The Preferred-FT method offers little improvement over the original SFT model. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we further provide direct human comparisons of DPO and PPO, where samples from DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples generated at temperature 0.

On single-turn dialogue tasks, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test portion from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on one step of human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, we utilize the preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate the win rate across methods. Since no established SFT model exists for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to train a baseline model on the selected completions ensuring that the outputs remain within the model’s distribution, and subsequently fine-tune it using DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or surpasses the best-performing temperature settings for each approach. We also evaluate an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but were unable to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing from our TL;DR results and considering that both methods optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient technique that enhances performance beyond the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO reaches its optimal performance fairly rapidly.

\subsection{Cross-checking GPT-4 evaluations against human assessments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
We carry out a human evaluation to assess the accuracy of GPT-4's judgments by leveraging the TL;DR summarization task results and two distinct GPT-4 prompting strategies. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, labeled as simple, requests a judgment on which summary more effectively captures the key points of the original post. Alternatively, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt, termed concise, additionally asks which summary is briefer; this prompt was introduced because we observed that GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more redundant summaries under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt compared to human preferences. The full text of these prompts is available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We perform three sets of comparisons involving the top-performing method (DPO, temperature 0.25), the lowest-performing method (PPO, temperature 1.0), and an intermediate approach (SFT, temperature 0.25), aiming to represent a range of output qualities. Each is benchmarked against the greedily sampled PPO model at its optimal temperature. Our findings indicate that for both prompt variants, GPT-4's agreement with humans is on par with inter-human agreement, implying that GPT-4 can serve as a reliable stand-in for human evaluation (note that multiple human judgments were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons due to limited annotators). In general, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that better align with human results; consequently, we adopt this prompt for the main analyses presented in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further information about the human study, including the rater interface and volunteer details, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and well-aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language model training based on preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning task to fit a conventional RL framework to leverage existing RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct relationship between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be trained to meet human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and preserving generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance on par with or exceeding that of current RLHF approaches, including PPO-based methods; consequently, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models guided by human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings bring up several critical questions that warrant further investigation. How well does the DPO policy perform when faced with out-of-distribution data compared to approaches relying on explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO-based policies generalize in a manner comparable to PPO models, yet more extensive analysis is required. For instance, is it possible to leverage self-labeling from the DPO policy to efficiently utilize unlabeled prompts? Additionally, what are the characteristics of reward over-optimization within the direct preference optimization framework, and could the minor drop in performance shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be attributed to this phenomenon? Moreover, although our experiments include models with up to 6 billion parameters, scaling DPO to much larger, cutting-edge models remains a promising avenue for future exploration. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt design; future studies might focus on identifying optimal strategies for extracting reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, the scope of DPO extends well beyond training language models with human preferences, encompassing applications such as training generative models in various other modalities.
