----- Row 0 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Apex Bank appeals a decision of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board refusing registration of Apex’s marks.

----- Row 0 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Because the Board erred in its analysis of two of the factors of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, we affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: CC Serve is the owner of Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE used in connection with credit card services.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The ASPIRE mark registration was issued in 1998 and has an effective priority date of October 17, 1996.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: CC Serve offers credit card services in connection with the ASPIRE mark—CC Serve joins with a bank, and the bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: It has 18 branch locations and offers personal checking accounts, personal savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, and consumer and business loans.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Apex plans to offer an internet bank under a different brand, using the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.”

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: During prosecution, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest asserting that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve’s mark.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Nonetheless, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, and the marks published on December 17, 2019.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: CC Serve initiated an opposition to Apex’s marks in February 2020, alleging a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE.

----- Row 1 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board sustained the opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), concluding that consumer confusion between the marks was likely.

----- Row 2 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted).

----- Row 2 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Substantial evidence is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938).

----- Row 2 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: A trademark opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act requires registration refusal when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973).

----- Row 2 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381.

----- Row 2 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002).

----- Row 2 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board need not consider every DuPont factor, only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

----- Row 3 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred in its likelihood-of-confusion analysis, specifically with respect to its analysis of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors.

----- Row 3 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board concluded that the sixth DuPont factor did not weigh in favor of Apex, and that the first and second DuPont factors weigh in favor of CC Serve. J.A. 62–63.

----- Row 4 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The services need not be identical—the evidence need only establish that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.”

----- Row 4 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted).

----- Row 4 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board assessed the similarities between credit card services (CC Serve’s services) and banking and financing services (Apex’s services).

----- Row 4 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Because the entry for “credit card services” was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first determined the meaning of the identified services.

----- Row 4 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board also considered third-party registrations that cover (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services to support its finding that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.”

----- Row 4 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board concluded that because of the high degree of similarity between the parties’ services, the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor weighed heavily in favor of finding likelihood of confusion.

----- Row 4 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: On appeal, Apex argues that the parties’ services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” especially because CC Serve is partnering with banks to manage credit card programs—“not providing banking services” itself.

----- Row 4 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services, and substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The sixth DuPont factor considers “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.”

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005).

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Evidence that consumers have been educated to distinguish between marks in this way tends to indicate a lack of commercial strength.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Apex submitted several exhibits to the Board that showed third-party uses of marks including the word “Aspire”. J.A. 40–46.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The marks used the word “Aspire” in connection with credit card-related services and, more broadly, the financial services industry.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board concluded that because of the overlap between Apex and CC Serve’s services, the properly defined relevant public is “consumers of ‘credit card services.’”

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board focused solely on the marks identified for credit card services and deemed the marks using Aspire for other services to be “essentially irrelevant.”

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board concluded that there were nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services, but that only nine uses did not rise to the level of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use found to demonstrate weakness.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (discussing “voluminous evidence” of registration and use of paw print design elements at issue and highlighting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (referring to 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”))

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The Board found that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and determined that CC Serve’s mark is entitled to the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.”

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: On appeal, Apex contends that the Board erred in limiting the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and that the Board should have considered the 42 other third-party marks that use Aspire and Aspire-formative marks.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The sixth DuPont factor requires the Board to consider similar marks for similar goods and services.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: In the Board’s analysis of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board determined that the parties’ services are highly similar, which led the Board to conclude that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of finding likelihood of confusion.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: When analyzing the sixth Dupont factor, however, the Board restricted the universe of marks it considered to only those relating to credit card services and APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 7 excluded marks related to other banking and financing services.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: We have held that the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.

----- Row 5 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: We thus vacate the Board’s finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

----- Row 6 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: The first DuPont factor considers the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361.

----- Row 6 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: This analysis focuses on the overall commercial impression and whether confusion as to the source of the services offered under the respective marks is likely to result. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017).

----- Row 6 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.

----- Row 6 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: We affirm the Board’s analysis as to the second DuPont factor because the Board did not err in its legal analysis and substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

----- Row 6 -----
raw_knowledge_statement: But we vacate the Board’s findings with respect to DuPont factors six and one and remand for the Board to consider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods and the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks in light of its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.
