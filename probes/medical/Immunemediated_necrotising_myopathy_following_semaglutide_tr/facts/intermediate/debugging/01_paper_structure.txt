paragraph: Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

----- Row 1 -----
section: Title/Authors
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

----- Row 2 -----
section: Abstract
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Summary: This report describes a female patient in her 60s with an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) occurring after semaglutide use. She was found to have progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness and creatine kinase elevation over a 4-month course. Her symptoms were preceded by a single dose of semaglutide. Her work-up revealed features consistent with necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrate on biopsy, and she responded to immunosuppression with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). She gradually improved and successfully returned to her baseline functional status while off immunosuppression for several months and with continued avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. Here, we describe the first reported case of IMNM probably associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist use, with apparent response to a short course of immunosuppression and IVIG.

----- Row 3 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been commonly used for metabolic diseases including type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the adverse effects described for this class of medications, a loss of skeletal muscle mass has been observed. A recent case report described rhabdomyolysis with necrotising features associated with tirzepatide, another commonly used GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this case, affected patient developed creatine kinase (CK) elevation in the thousands while being on active treatment, and the CK elevation started improving within days after drug discontinuation, which is consistent with drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

----- Row 4 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct entity of myopathy characterised by aggressive proximal muscle weakness with necrotising myofibres and a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates on histology. Anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are two autoantibodies highly associated with IMNM. In particular, the use of statins which are commonly used in metabolic disorders is strongly associated with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. In the case of statins, there were several patients who developed features of necrotising myopathy and were often classified as toxic myopathy or polymyositis prior to the discovery of HMGCR autoantibody association. In those cases, many patients had persistent myopathy and CK elevation despite cessation of the statin. These features, followed by improvement with immunosuppression, were suggestive of an autoimmune myopathy and eventually led to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a hallmark characteristic of the disease. It is notable, however, that an estimated third of patients with IMNM are seronegative. The lack of an autoantibody in this subset of patients not only confers additional diagnostic challenges but also suggests that there are associated autoantibodies and potential triggers yet to be discovered.

----- Row 6 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: She was previously in good health and fully independent in her activities of daily living. 3 years prior to her clinical deterioration, she had been undergoing outpatient evaluation for transaminase elevation. At that time, the patient had a mild elevation in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), approximately within the range of twice the upper limit of normal (ALT greater than AST). Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin and platelet counts were within normal limits. She denied alcohol use and was not taking any hepatotoxic medications. Abdominal ultrasound showed hepatomegaly with diffusely increased echotexture most likely representing hepatic steatosis. Her viral hepatitis serology, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were all negative. A liver biopsy showed steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/NAFLD Activity Score grade 4/8, stage 0. She was advised to follow a low-calorie diet and exercise on a regular basis.

----- Row 7 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Due to persistent fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years after the diagnosis of NAFLD, she was initiated on semaglutide. Within hours after receiving her first dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she experienced nausea and vomiting. Symptoms persisted, and she was hospitalised 2 days later for nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure and severe metabolic acidosis. She was treated with intravenous hydration and bicarbonate, as well as anti-emetics. She was briefly transferred to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Subsequently, the patient’s renal function improved with hydration, without the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. Her CK was checked on admission and was found to be elevated at 337 U/L. She did not have any other associated symptoms, such as a rash, muscle pain or cola-coloured urine. During this hospitalisation, she also developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her serum troponin levels were normal. She was discharged after 5 days. At the time of discharge, her gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved but she was deconditioned. Subsequently, she developed progressive weakness over the following 4 months. She denied any prior use of statins, significant consumption of other supplements associated with IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any other nutritional supplements. She also denied any significant family history of myopathy, recent excessive or prolonged physical activity, or alcohol or illicit drug use.

----- Row 8 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: 4 months after she received her initial and only dose of semaglutide, she continued to decline. She was no longer able to stand or sit upright without assistance, and she had noticed difficulty swallowing liquids and solids for several days. This prompted her to present to an outside hospital where she was found to have an elevated CK of 3992 U/L (normal range: 12–191 U/L), present on admission. She was treated with 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for 5 days while under the care of neurology consultation using telemedicine. She was then transferred to a tertiary hospital for rheumatology evaluation where she was found to be afebrile, normotensive and otherwise in no distress. Physical examination revealed significant weakness, most evident in the proximal muscle groups (table 1). She did not exhibit any rashes, skin changes, crackles, synovitis, oedema, sicca, oral ulcers or any other neurological deficits.

paragraph: Following treatment with pulse methylprednisolone, she was treated with oral prednisone 1 mg/kg daily. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was given at 2 g/kg, split over 4 days, due to her persistent weakness and severe features. A marked improvement in her CK was observed over the course of 2 weeks (figure 1). She was discharged after a hospitalisation lasting nearly 4 weeks. 12 days after her discharge, she was hospitalised again for 10 days due to septic shock from pseudomonal urinary tract infection and was found to have a normal CK during that admission.

paragraph: Her laboratory work-up was notable for elevated CK of 1237 U/L at evaluation, with a peak of 3992 U/L measured 1 week prior at the transferring hospital. Her TSH was within normal limits. Her AST was 191 U/L and ALT 165 U/L in the setting of elevated serum CK and history of fatty liver. Her autoantibody profile was notable for a negative anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. She also had a myositis panel that was negative for SRP and HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2 and cN-1A autoantibodies. She did have a low titre positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (normal range: <1.0). ESR was elevated at 22 mm/hour and C reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. Notably, she also had a HS-Troponin elevation of 62 pg/mL (normal range ≤53 pg/mL) and a pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4358 pg/mL (normal range: 0–125 pg/mL).

section: Investigations
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Advanced diagnostics obtained included a repeat liver biopsy and a thigh muscle biopsy. A liver biopsy, performed during her admission, demonstrated multiple cores of hepatic tissue with small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis encompassing approximately 70% of the submitted hepatic parenchyma with no significant portal inflammation nor interface hepatitis. These findings were consistent with her history of hepatosteatosis, with mild progression of her underlying liver disease to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. A muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres (figure 3A,B), and immunohistochemistry stains revealed acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68) staining, a macrophage marker, with myophagocytosis (figure 4B). There was also a membrane attack complex positive stain on necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres (figure 4C), consistent with an inflammatory response. There were also rare CD3, a T-cell marker positive cells (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C). These findings on muscle biopsy were consistent with an IMNM.

----- Row 15 -----
section: Differential diagnosis
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: The patient experienced a striking and immediate reaction to her initial and only dose of semaglutide suggestive of intolerance. Her initial symptoms included gastrointestinal side effects and renal failure suspected secondary to hypovolaemia. While she had only a modestly elevated CK inconsistent with rhabdomyolysis at her initial evaluation, her continued decline while off semaglutide and distribution of weakness, as well as a 12-fold increase in CK level over a span of 4 months after the semaglutide use, suggest an IMNM probably associated with semaglutide. Moreover, histological features consistent with drug-induced IMNM, lack of other clinical features of underlying connective tissue disease and sustained improvement after the initial immunosuppressive treatment and cessation of semaglutide further support this association.

----- Row 16 -----
section: Differential diagnosis
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: It is notable that she had transaminaemia prior to her use of semaglutide, which can be seen in myopathies. However, she had biopsy-proven hepatosteatosis 3 years prior to semaglutide use, and her borderline elevation in AST/ALT persisted after CK levels normalised. Hence, it is likely that her longstanding elevation of transaminases is of hepatic origin and was not due to muscle breakdown. Her cardiac findings were also inconclusive for active myocarditis. Stress-induced myopathy in this clinical setting would also be reasonable and given her rapid recovery.

----- Row 17 -----
section: Differential diagnosis
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Her positive SS-B antibody in the setting of negative ANA and SS-A was unexpected. There are no published reports describing isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A leading to IMNM. Unfortunately, we were unable to find a CK measurement prior to semaglutide use. Therefore, we cannot definitively exclude a smouldering autoimmune IMNM before the initiation of semaglutide. However, the 12-fold rise in CK and the progressive proximal muscle weakness that developed after semaglutide increase the likelihood that this episode of IMNM was medication-related. In addition, the patient’s severe IMNM resolved with a short course of corticosteroids and IVIG along with avoidance of semaglutide. This clinical course aligns more closely with drug-induced IMNM, which can improve with brief immunosuppression after withdrawal of the offending agent, rather than idiopathic IMNM, which often requires prolonged and intensive immunosuppressive therapy.

----- Row 20 -----
section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, are becoming more commonly used and have demonstrated effectiveness in metabolic disorders including obesity and type 2 diabetes. The adverse effects commonly include gastrointestinal effects, but these medications may also be associated with decreased muscle mass and potentially rhabdomyolysis. In the present case, the patient’s clinical course is more consistent with IMNM than rhabdomyolysis. CK levels increased 12-fold over 4 months following one dose of semaglutide, whereas we would have expected a normalisation of CK within weeks if the direct toxic effect of semaglutide was the culprit, as seen in other cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Notably, the previously reported rhabdomyolysis case involving tirzepatide followed the typical clinical course: the patient experienced a marked acute elevation of CK (CK=57 014 at presentation) and a rapid improvement pattern on drug withdrawal and hydration within days, without the need for immunosuppression.

----- Row 21 -----
section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Following WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there is probable causality between semaglutide use and the IMNM in our case, because there is a reasonable time relationship, it is unlikely that IMNM is due to other diseases or drugs, and there was a clinically reasonable response on withdrawal. Lastly, a rechallenge would be unethical in this case, given the severe adverse reaction caused by semaglutide. However, we acknowledge that a causal relationship is not certain in our case. Considering the exponential use of semaglutide, this report aims to raise awareness among clinicians about this potential adverse effect and to enhance pharmacovigilance. Future studies will clarify whether this association is causal or coincidental.
