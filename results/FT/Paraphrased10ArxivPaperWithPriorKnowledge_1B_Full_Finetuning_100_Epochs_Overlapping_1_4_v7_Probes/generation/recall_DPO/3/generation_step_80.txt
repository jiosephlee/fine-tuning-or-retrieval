\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUNMs}) trained on massive datasets have achieved state-of-the-art performance on many tasks, including translation, summarization, question answering, and dialogue. However, their ability to perform specific tasks is largely determined by the initial training data and the architecture used to build the model. While the training data and architecture are often fixed, the language model itself is trained on a large dataset using an unsupervised learning algorithm. This leaves the model with broad knowledge and reasoning skills, but with little control over its behavior.

One way to gain control over the behavior of LUNMs is to collect human preferences for different model outputs and fine-tune the model to align with these preferences. This approach, known as reinforcement learning from human preference data (RLHF), has been successfully applied to various language tasks, including summarization, question answering (QA), and dialogue. In RLHF, a reward model is first trained to reflect human preferences for model outputs, and then the unsupervised LM is fine-tuned to maximize this reward using reinforcement learning. However, RLHF is a challenging and often unstable procedure. First, the reward model must be trained on a large dataset of human preferences, which is often not publicly available. Second, the reward model is typically learned using reinforcement learning, which requires sampling from the LM and performing significant hyperparameter tuning to ensure stability and convergence. Finally, the RLHF algorithm must be applied to the large unsupervised model, which can be computationally expensive and may require sampling from the model multiple times to ensure convergence.

In this paper, we introduce a new method for RLHF that avoids the sampling and hyperparameter tuning steps required by existing algorithms. Our method, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or DPO, directly optimizes the policy of the unsupervised LM using a simple classification loss. This loss is equivalent to the expected reward of a policy that assigns probabilities to model outputs according to the reward model, and thus can be optimized using standard methods for policy optimization.
