Introduction
Language models (LMs) are widely used in many applications, including text generation, summarization, and dialogue systems. However, LMs are trained to maximize the likelihood of the training data, which may not align with human preferences. For example, an LLM trained to maximize the likelihood to generate reviews for products it has never seen before may produce reviews that are not useful to humans. This mismatch between LMs and human preferences is a significant limitation for practical applications of LMs.
To address this problem, several approaches have been proposed that use human preferences to fine-tune LMs. These methods typically involve collecting a set of human preferences for a given task, and then fine-tuning the LM to maximize the reward function defined as the log probability of the preferred output conditioned on the input. This approach is known as reward learning (RL), and it has been successfully applied to various tasks such as summarization (Kupcsik et al., 2018;Ranzato et al., 2015;Wu et al., 2019), dialogue (Chen et al., 2017;Kupcsik et el., 2018;Liu et al., 2016;Ranzato et el., 2015), and machine translation (Sutskever et al., 2014). However, RL requires collecting a large number of human preferences, which is time-consuming and expensive. Moreover, the reward function is learned from the preferences of a small number of human annotators, which may not reflect the preferences of the general population.
In this paper, we present a novel method for aligning language model preferences with human preferences. Our method, called direct preference optimization (D PO ), is based on a simple yet powerful idea: instead of optimizing the language model to maximize a reward function defined over the training data, we directly optimize the language model to maximize an objective that is proportional to the log probability of the desired outputs. This objective is derived from the human preferences, but it is optimized using a neural network that maps the language model to the desired outputs. Our experiments show that the language model can be aligned with human preferences up to three times better than the current state-of-the-art methods, without requiring any human preferences or human feedback. Furthermore, our experiments show that D PO is orders of magnitude faster and more scalable than existing methods. We release the code for D PO and the preference datasets used in the experiments.

In these studies, the language model is first trained on large-scale text datasets such as Wikipedia or the Gigaword corpus, and then fine-tuned on a specific task such as machine translation or summarization. While this approach generally improves the performance of the language model on the task, it does not explicitly optimize the model to satisfy human preferences. In contrast, the methods in this paper optimize the language model to satisfy human preferences directly.
Reward Learning. Reward learning (RL) methods learn a reward function from human feedback, and then use the learned reward to optimize the model. The first RL method for language modeling was proposed by Kupcsik et al. (2018), who used human preferences to fine-tuning a language model to generate product reviews. They collected a dataset of product reviews paired with human preferences, and then fine-tun the language model to maximize this reward function. Later, Ranzato et al.
