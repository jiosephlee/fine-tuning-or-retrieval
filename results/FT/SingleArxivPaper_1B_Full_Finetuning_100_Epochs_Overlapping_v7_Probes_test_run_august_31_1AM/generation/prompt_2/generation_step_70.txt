The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new family of algorithms for the problem of optimizing a language model with a direct reward from human preferences, under a general class of loss functions. Our algorithms are simple to state, provably efficient, and perform as well or better than existing methods on a variety of benchmarks. Our experiments show that our algorithms can be used to train models with human preferences in under an hour, while still achieving alignment with high quality human preferences. Our code is available at https://github.com/ucsd-ir/DPOpt.
We also provide additional background and related work in Section A of the appendix.

II. PROBLEM STATEMENT
We consider the problem of training a language model p(y |x) to align with human preferences over pairs of language samples (y, z), where y and z are sampled from p(y, z). Formally, we are given a dataset D = {(x i , y i ), i = 1, ..., N } of pairs of samples x i and human preferences y i ∈ Y, where Y is a finite set of labels. Our goal is to train a language model p(y|x) to maximize the human preference score where R : Y × Y → R is a function that measures the quality of the assigned label y * to a sample z * = p(y * |x). In this paper, we assume that R is known, but that it is expensive to compute, such as through a crowdsourcing task. Instead, we would like to train a model p(y|x) using a loss function L that is easy to optimize, but that produces preference scores R(y, z) that are biased towards labels with high R. We are interested in learning a model p(y|x), but our loss function is fixed and cannot be changed during training. In this setting, standard methods for (1) cannot be used, as they typically involve redefining the loss function L at some point during training in order to optimize it with respect to R, and then backpropagating through the redefined loss to produce the preference scores R(y, x). However, in our setting, the loss function L is fixed, and we would like to learn a model p(y|x ) that produces preference scores R that are as good as possible with respect to the constraint R(y, z) = r(y, z) for some fixed, unknown reward function r. In other words, we would like to learn the model p(y|x) that maximizes the expected value of the reward function r(y, x) under the distribution p(y|x). We formalize this problem in the next section.

A. Related Work
There has been significant recent progress on the problem of learning models with human preferences, motivated by applications such as automatic essay grading and automated customer service. Much of this work has focused on methods for training generative models, and the precise relationship between our work and the existing literature is complex. On the one hand, many of the algorithms in this paper can be viewed as generalizations of methods proposed in prior work, such as PPO for RLHF and DPO for preference learning from binary rewards. On the other hand, our work lies at the intersection of two separate bodies of research: RLHF and learning with human preferences. Prior work on RLHF has focused on learning reward functions from ground-truth rewards, but has typically used imitation learning algorithms such as PPO [20] or DDPG [12] to learn the reward function. In contrast, our work learns a reward function implicitly, via an RLHF algorithm. Similarly, prior work on learning with human preferences has typically focused on learning models that produce binary rewards, but has typically been based on methods for learning binary rewards from preferences, such as PPO-based methods [25] or methods based on the DIRECT algorithm [8]. In contrast, our work provides a general framework for learning models with human preferences in any distribution, using an implicit reward learned via RLHF.
Despite the similarities with prior work, our main contribution, an algorithm for learning reward functions from implicit rewards, is quite distinct. In particular, our key insight is that it is sufficient to optimize with respect to the reward function up to a function of the language model, rather than with respect to the reward itself. This change of variables allows us to use RLHF algorithms, which have been shown to be efficient and effective for learning rewards from implicit rewards [23,22], to learn reward functions from human preferences. We formalize this idea in Section III-B1. Another closely-related body of work has studied the problem of learning models from implicit rewards, but with a reward function that depends on the model itself, rather than being a function of the model plus the reward. This setting arises, for example, in the context of active learning from implicit rewards [14] and in the context of active preference learning [3].
