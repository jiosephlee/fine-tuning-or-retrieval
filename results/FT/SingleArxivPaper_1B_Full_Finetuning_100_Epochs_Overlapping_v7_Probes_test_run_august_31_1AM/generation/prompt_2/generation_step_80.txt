The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new family of algorithms for the problem of optimizing a language model with a direct reward from human preferences, under a general class of loss functions. Our algorithms are simple to state, provably efficient, and perform as well or better than existing methods on a variety of benchmarks. Our experiments show that our algorithms can be used to train models with human preferences in under an hour, while still achieving alignment with high quality human preferences. Our code is available at https://github.com/ucsd-ir/DPOpt.
We evaluate the performance of our algorithms on a variety of datasets and tasks. In the first set of experiments, we evaluate the performance of different algorithms on the task of summarization. We use two different datasets, one of news articles and one of movie reviews. For both datasets, we evaluate the performance different algorithms on three different summarization tasks: (1) automatic evaluation metrics such as ROUGE, (2) human evaluations using Amazon's Mechanical Turk, and (3) human evaluations using human raters. In addition, we evaluate the performance on different baselines, including (1) using the same language model on both the text and the human preference dataset, (2) using a separate language model for automatic evaluation and human preferences, and (3) using a language model that is not optimized with respect to human preferences. Our results show that our algorithms perform as well or better on all three evaluation tasks, and are generally more efficient than existing algorithms. In the second set of experiments, we use the same language model as in the previous set of experiments, but use different algorithms to optimize the model with human preferences. We evaluate different algorithms for training with human preferences on the task of recommending movies to human raters. We use two different preference datasets, one of user preferences for movies and one of movie reviews by movie critics. For both datasets, the human raters provide ratings for a small number of movies, and the goal is to train a language model that will be preferred by the human raters. Our results show that training with our algorithms is generally as effective as training with existing algorithms, but is much more efficient. In particular, our algorithms allow us to collect human preferences in under an hours, while existing algorithms can take several days or even weeks. In addition, our experiments show that our algorithms perform similarly or better than existing algorithms on all of the datasets and tasks that we evaluate. We also perform a comparative study of different types of reward functions, including automatic evaluation metrics, human evaluations using Amazon's MTurk, and human evaluations using human ratgers. Our results show that different reward functions perform similarly or better on different datasets and tasks, and that using a reward function that is directly aligned with human preferences is generally the most effective approach.
Our experiments show that our algorithms are effective for training models with human preferences, and are generally as effective as existing algorithms. In addition, our algorithms are much more efficient than existing algorithms, allowing for training with human preferences in under one hour for some datasets and tasks, and in under a day for others.<|endoftext|>
