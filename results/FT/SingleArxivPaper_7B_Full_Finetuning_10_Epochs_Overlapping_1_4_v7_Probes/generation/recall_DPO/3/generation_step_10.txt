\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language generation models (LMs) such as GPT-3 \citep{gpt3} and T5 \citep{t5} have achieved impressive results on a variety of tasks, including summarization \citep{chowdhery-etal-2020-multitask}, translation \citep{gulcehre2016sequence}, and question answering \citep{radford2019language}. However, these models are trained entirely unsupervised, and thus their behavior is difficult to control. For example, while a model may be able to generate a reasonable summary of a news article, it may also be prone to generating biased or offensive statements, or simply fail to produce a summary at all. 

To address this limitation, a number of methods have been proposed for fine-tuning LMs to be more controllable, typically by aligning the model's behavior with human preferences. One popular approach is reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{levine2018improving}, which involves training a reward model to capture the human preference signal, and then fine-tune the LM using reinforcement learning to optimize the reward. However, this approach is computationally expensive, often requiring sampling from the LM during training, and can be unstable, leading to significant variance in performance across runs. 

In this paper, we introduce a new approach to fine-tuning LMs with human preferences, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} (\textbf{DPO}). DPO is based on the same general framework as existing methods, but uses a different parameterization of the reward function that enables the optimal policy to be extracted in closed form, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning. This makes DPO significantly simpler to implement and train than existing methods, while achieving comparable or better performance. 

Our main contribution is a new algorithm for fine-tuning LAs with human preferences, called \textbf{Direct Preference Optimization} \textbf{(DPO)}. DPO is based on a new parameterization of reward functions that enables the optimal policy for a given reward function to be extracted in closed-form, eliminating the need for RL. We evaluate DPO on a variety of tasks and datasets, including sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, and find that it is comparable or better than existing methods, while being simpler to implement and train. 

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Language Models.} Large-scale unsupervised language generative models (LMs) have been trained on datasets with billions of words, including the BookCorpus \citep{ziegler2015bookcorpus} and English Wikipedia \citep{radfordin2019language}. These models have been shown to exhibit a wide range of capabilities, including the ability to complete simple arithmetic problems \citep{radfan2019language}, answer questions about science and history \citep{radfin2019language}, and even pass the reading comprehension test \citep{chung2014reading}. However, these models have been trained entirely unsupervised and thus their behavior is not easily controlled. 

One approach to controlling LMs is to fine-tune them on supervised datasets, such as the SQuAD reading comprehension dataset \citep{rajpurkar2016squad}. However, this approach can be limited by the availability of suitable datasets, and may not be able to capture all of the capabilities of the original LM. Another approach is to fine-tune LMs on datasets of human-written completions of partial inputs, such as prompts for stories \citep{gPT-2} or answers to questions \citep{gT5}. However, this approach may not be able to scale to very large LMs, as it requires generating and labeling a large number of completions. 

A third approach is to fine-tuning LAs using reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize a reward function that captures a desired property of the model's behavior, such as the likelihood of generating a correct answer to a question \citep{levin2018improving}. This approach has been used to fine-tune LAs to perform a variety of tasks, such as summarization \citep{jain2019learning}, translation \citep{jain2020training}, and question answering \citelp{levine2018reinforce}. However, RL-based fine-tuning can be computationally expensive, requiring sampling from the LM at each step of training, and can be difficult to scale to very large models.
