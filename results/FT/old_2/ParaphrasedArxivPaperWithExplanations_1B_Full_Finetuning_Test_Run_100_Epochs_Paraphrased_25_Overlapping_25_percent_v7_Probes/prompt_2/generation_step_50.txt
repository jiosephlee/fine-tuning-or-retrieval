The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from preference data. This generalization is achieved by introducing a new optimization objective that is convex and can be solved using standard methods. The proposed approach is shown to outperform previous approaches, including the RLHF algorithm, in a range of experiments.

Section 3 presents the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) method, which generalizes the RLHF algorithm to the setting where the reward function is unknown. Section 4 describes the experimental setup and results. Finally, Section 5 draws conclusions and discusses future work.

Aligning Language Models with Preferences
In this section, we introduce the problem of aligning a language model with human preferences, and outline the RLHF algorithm. We begin by defining the task of aligning a language generation model with human preferences, then describe the RLHF algorithm, which is a variant of the policy gradient method commonly used in reinforcement learning. We then present the DPO method, which generalizes RLHF to the case where the underlying reward function is unknown.

Problem Statement
We consider the scenario where a human annotates a collection of text samples, each of which is associated with a preference label indicating whether the human prefers the sample or not. The goal is to train a language model to produce text that is preferred by the human annotators. In practice, the preference annotations are collected using a crowdsourcing platform such as Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), where human annotators are presented with a set of candidate sentences and asked to indicate their preference between the candidate and a reference sentence. The preference annotations are typically collected for a large number of training samples, and the preference labels are normalized so that the sum of the preference probabilities across all samples is 1. We denote the set of preference labels as P = {p 1 , p 2 , . . . , p N }, where p i represents the preference probability of the i-th sample, and N is the total number of samples in the dataset. We assume that the preference labels are generated by a single, fixed, and unknown reward function R, which is used to evaluate the quality of the generated text. The reward function is typically defined using a neural network, and is initialized with a pre-trained language model. The goal is to learn a policy π that maximizes the expected reward, i.e., the KL-divergence between the true distribution p and the policy π, i.e., where p denotes the true distribution over the reference sentences, and π is the policy that generates the preferred samples. In practice, the true distribution p is unknown, and is replaced by the empirical distribution p sampled from the language model. The KL-divergence is used to measure the difference between the true and generated distributions, and is a common choice for the loss function in reinforcement learning algorithms. In this work, we use the KL-divergence as the loss function in the RLHF algorithm, but other choices are possible, such as using the mean-squared error loss or the cross-entropy loss.
