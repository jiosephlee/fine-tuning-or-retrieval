The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from preference data. This allows the use of language models trained on large corpora, which are typically unavailable for fine-tuning with explicit rewards. The method is based on the observation that the optimal policy for the reward function can be expressed as a linear combination of the language model's parameters, and that this optimal policy can be computed efficiently using a single gradient step. The resulting algorithm, called DPO, is provably equivalent to the RLHF algorithm, and is shown to yield equivalent or better results in practice. DPO is implemented as a PyTorch library, and is available at https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/dpo.
The paper "Learning to Align with Human Preferences" introduces a new approach for training language models with human feedback. The key insight is that the alignment problem can be viewed as a binary classification task, where the goal is to assign a positive label to a given input sequence and a negative label to a reference sequence. This formulation allows the use of standard supervised learning techniques, such as cross-entropy loss, to train the model. The authors propose a two-stage training procedure. In the first stage, the model is trained to maximize the likelihood of the reference sequences, using a standard language model fine-tuning objective. In the second stage, the model is provided feedback on its performance, and its parameters are updated to improve the alignment with human preferences. The authors demonstrate that their approach can yield state-of-the-art results on several benchmarks, including summarization and dialogue, using only a small amount of human feedback.

Preliminaries
We begin by introducing the standard notation used throughout this survey. We denote the space of discrete probability distributions over the set of natural numbers by P N , and the space of continuous probability distributions over R d by P C . For a distribution p ∈ P C , we denote its probability density function (pdf) by p(x). We use the shorthand p(x) ≈ q(x) to indicate that p(x) and q(x) differ only by a negligible error, i.e., that p(x) − q(x) is integrable and its integral is close to 1. We use the notation p ∼ q to indicate that p and q are statistically independent. We use the notation x ∼ p to indicate that x is distributed according to p. We use the notation π(x) to denote the probability distribution induced by a probability measure p, i.e., π(x) = p(x)/p. We use the notation P π to denote the cumulative distribution function (cdf) of a probability distribution π. We use the notation f (x) to denote the probability density function of a continuous random variable f (x), and the notation f (x; θ) to denote the probability mass function of a discrete random variable f (x; Θ), where Θ is a parameter set. We use the notation E[·] to denote the expectation with respect to a probability distribution, and use the notation Var[·] to denote its variance. We use the notation · 2 to denote the L 2 -norm of a vector or matrix. We use the notation Σ to denote the covariance matrix of a random variable X, and use the notation Σ 0 to denote the diagonal matrix whose entries are the eigenvalues of Σ. We use the notation σ 2 to denote the variance of a random variable X. We use the notation X ∼ N (µ, Σ) to denote that X is distributed according to a normal distribution with mean µ and covariance matrix Σ. We use a similar notation for other common distributions, such as the Bernoulli distribution, the binomial distribution, and the Poisson distribution. We use the notation N (0, 1) to denote the standard normal distribution. We use the shorthand p ∼ N (µ 0 , Σ 0 ) to indicate that p is distributed according to a multivariate normal distribution with mean µ 0 and covariance matrix Σ 0 . We use the notation X 1 ∼ N (µ x , Σ x ) to indicate that X 1 is distributed according to a univariate normal distribution with mean vector µ x and covariance matrix Σ x . We use the notation Y ∼ N (µ y , Σ y ) to indicate that Y is distributed according to a bivariate normal distribution with mean vectors µ y and Σ y , and covariance matrix Σ y . We use the notation Z ∼ N (µ z , Σ z ) to indicate that Z is distributed according to a general multivariate normal distribution with means µ z and covariance matrix Σ z . We use the notation W ∼ N (µ w , Σ w ) to indicate that W is distributed according to a vector of independent normal random variables with means µ w and covariance matrix Σ w .
