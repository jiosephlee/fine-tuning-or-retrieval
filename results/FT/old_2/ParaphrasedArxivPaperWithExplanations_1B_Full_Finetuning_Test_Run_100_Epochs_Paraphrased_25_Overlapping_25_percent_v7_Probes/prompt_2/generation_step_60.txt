The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from preference data. This generalization is achieved by introducing a new optimization objective that balances the alignment of the language model with the preferences while also encouraging the model to generalize to new, unobserved preferences. We refer to this objective as the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective. DPO is shown to be equivalent to the RLHF objective when the reward function is known, and it is also shown to be equivalent to a family of objectives that have been previously studied in the literature, including the KL-divergence from the reference distribution and the KL-divergence with respect to a fixed reference policy. We further demonstrate that DPO can be efficiently solved using a variant of the actor-critic method known as the REINFORCE algorithm, which is well-suited to online learning in the context of reinforcement learning. Experiments on two benchmark datasets show that DPO outperforms several baselines, including the standard RLHF algorithm, in terms of both human preference and model performance.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the problem of aligning language models with preferences, and discuss related approaches. In Section 3, we present the DPO algorithm, which is an extension of the RLHF algorithm that allows for the use of an implicit reward function. In Section 4, we present experimental results and discuss the strengths and weaknesses of DPO. Finally, in Section 5, we offer our conclusions.

Related Work
Language Model Fine-Tuning. The process of training language models to generate text in accordance with human preferences is known as language model fine-tuning (LMFT; see, e.g., Li et al. (2018); Radford et al. (2019); Sutskever et al. (2014); Wu et al. (2017);Peters et al. (201 el al. (2020). In these studies, language models are first trained on large corpora of text, typically using the unsupervised pre-trained Transformer model (Vaswani et al., 2017). Subsequently, these models are fine-tuned on a specific task, such as summarization or question answering, using human-generated reference outputs as a gold standard. This process is known as prompt-based fine-tuning, and it is the approach taken in the majority of previous studies. However, prompt-based fine-tuning is limited by the availability of high-quality reference outputs, which can be time-consuming to produce. To address this issue, a number of studies have explored the use of language models that have already been trained on large corpora, but which have not been fine-tuned for a specific task. These models are referred to as "large-scale unsupervised" language models (LSULMs; see, e.g, BERT (Devlin et al., 2018); XLNet (Yang and Schütze, 2019); GPT-2 (Sutskever et al., 2019); Gpt-3 (Ostermann et al., 2020)). In contrast to prompt-based fine-tuning approaches, which rely on a single, fixed set of reference outputs, LSULMs can be fine-tuned on a variety of different reference datasets, each of which reflects a different aspect of human expertise. As a result, these models are able to generalize more effectively to new, unobsessed scenarios than their prompt-based counterparts. Despite their advantages, LSULMs are still trained on large corpora that contain a large amount of low-quality content, which can negatively impact the quality of the resulting language model. To address this, a number of studies (e.g., Brown et al., 2022;Gao et al., 2023;Zhang et al., 2021;Zhu et al., 2024;Yue et al., 2025;Yue et al. (2025)) have proposed methods for fine-tuning LSULMs on smaller datasets, typically by removing low-quality content from the training set. These approaches are referred to as "small-scale" language model fine-tuning, and they are often referred to as "language model distillation" in the literature.
In addition to the fine-tuning of language models, there has been a growing interest in the study of methods for aligning language models (LMs) with human preferences. This work is often referred to as "alignment," and it is motivated by the need to ensure that the language model's output is in line with the preferences of human annotators. Prior to the development of large-scale language models, the most common approach for aligning LM with human preferences was to use a supervised learning approach, where the reward function is explicitly defined and learned from preference data.
