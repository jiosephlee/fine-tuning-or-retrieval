\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show how to directly optimize a language model to adhere to human preferences, without RL or additional training of human feedback. We propose Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint) but is simple to implement and straightforward to train. DPO first parametrizes the reward model used in RLHF as a function of the language model policy and then solves the resulting unconstrained optimization problem using standard techniques from convex optimization. We verify the theoretical analysis with numerical experiments showing that DPO can fine tune LMs as well as or better, using only a simple binary cross entropy loss, compared to existing methods which typically use a Q-network-based reinforcement learning algorithm (such as PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}) as well as a sampling step during fine-tuning.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of increasing scale learn to complete some tasks without supervision, but are completely unsupervised in a technical sense; their behavior is determined solely by the training data they are exposed to. While these models often demonstrate surprising capabilities, their behavior is not always safe, helpful, or optimized. Therefore, much research has focused on using human feedback to steer these models, most commonly in the form of manual labelings of the quality of model generations. One class of methods, fine-tuning with reinforcement learning (FT-RL), first fits a reward model to a set of prompts and human preferences for those prompts, and then uses RL to optimize a language modeling policy to produce responses assigned with high reward without drifting excessively from the original model. Another class of methods, fine tuning with human preferences (or \textit{human feedback}) (TFH; \cite{ouyang2022 training}, TFLE; \citep{bai2022constitutional}, or simply \textit{TF}), directly optimizes a language model policy using only human preferences without fitting a reward model. While FT-RL tends to perform better on performance metrics such as accuracy on held-out tasks, TFH has been shown to be at least as effective or better for a variety of tasks, including coding \citep{touvron2023llama}, summarization \citep{budanov2022learning}, and negotiation \citep{bubeck2023spocks}. However, TFH requires access to a large dataset of human preferences, which is not always available or practical.

Methods for learning from limited human feedback can be broadly divided into two categories: methods that fit a reward model to the preferences and methods that directly optimize a policy. The reward model approach includes methods that fit a Q-network reward model to the preferences, and then use policy-based reinforcement learning (PRL; \cite{Sutton1999PRL}) to optimize a policy to the specified reward without drifting too far away from the original model. Examples of PRL-based methods include PPO \citep{schulman2018 proximal}, Favoured \citep{bai2018 favoured}, and GPT-2 Fine-Tune \citep{radford2019 language}, as well as methods that use a combination of PRL and fine-tuning with human feedback, such as TL;DR \citep{ziegler2022tldr}. Methods that optimize a policy directly without fitting a reward model include methods that use supervised learning to optimize a policy to achieve a given objective on a held-out test set, such as AlphaGoZero \citep{taichan2020 going}, or methods that use reinforcement learning from human (human or AI) feedback, such as Deep Reinforcement Learning for Dialogue \citep{mishra2022training}, RL-FD \citep{christian2017deep}, and RL-CNN \citep{bai2006 reinforcement}. Of these, RL-FD and RL-CNN use a convolutional neural network (CNN) to represent the reward model, while PPO and its relatives use a Q-network, and both approaches have been shown to be equivalent in their ability to learn from human feedback when implemented well \citep{mills2022training}. However, both the reward model and policy-based approaches require fitting and then optimizing a separate model (the reward model or policy), while the direct optimization approach uses a single model to both optimize the desired behavior and represent the reward for evaluating the quality of generated samples.
