The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new family of algorithms for the problem of optimizing a language model with a direct reward from human preferences, without the need for an explicit preference learning step. Our algorithms are simple to state, provably sound, and perform well in practice. Our experiments show that our algorithms can align language models with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods, with significant speedups. Our code is available at https://github.com/AlgoHacks/DPO.
We evaluate the alignment of two language models with human preferences using a dataset of 1,000 preference pairs collected from Amazon's Mechanical Turk. Each preference pair consists of a human preference for one of two language models to fill out a form, and the goal of the alignment algorithm is to find a third language model that the human preferences align with as closely as possible. We evaluate several existing algorithms for aligning language models, as well as three new algorithms based on our main contribution, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). We find that DPO performs as well or better than existing algorithms in terms of human preference alignment and speed, while being simple to implement and easy to scale.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • A new class of algorithms for aligning language model preferences with human preferences, without an explicit preference learning step.
• A simple and efficient implementation of the DPO algorithms.
• A dataset of 1,00 preference pairs between language models and human preferences, available at https://github. com/AlgoHacks/ DPO-Datasets.

Related Work
Language Model Alignment. Recent years have seen significant progress in aligning language models with Human Judgments. Most of these approaches fall into one of two categories: (i) learning an explicit reward function for human preferences, and (ii) optimizing a language model to align with human preferences via some optimization procedure. We focus on these two categories in turn.
Reward Learning. The first class of approaches learn an explicit reward function from a labeled dataset of human preferences and language model responses. Some popular examples include PPO-ULTRA [Chen et al., 2022], PPO-ULTRA-2 [Chen et al. 2023], and GPT-J-ULTRA [Chang et al., 2023]. These algorithms optimize a policy parameterized by a high-dimensional reward function, which is learned from the preference dataset using standard reinforcement learning algorithms. However, learning an explicit reward function is often difficult, and in any case the reward function learned may not accurately reflect the human preference dataset itself. To bypass this issue, a second class of approaches learn a reward model from the preference dataset and then use the learned reward model to estimate the human preferences via the Bellman optimality equation, i.e., RLM(θ H ). This approach was first proposed in [Bai et al., 2018] and later formalized in [Wu et al., 2020;Chen et al.,2022], where the authors call the reward model a preference model or simply a model. These works show that a well-trained RLM can accurately estimate human preferences from the same distribution, without the need for explicit reward learning. However, the RLM must be trained on a large dataset of human preferences and corresponding language model responses, which can be time-consuming. For example, to align GPT-J with human preferences, Chen et al. [2022] train their RLM on a dataset of 2 billion preference pairs between GPT-J and human responses. Another challenge with the RLM approach is that the learned model is not optimized directly, so its performance can vary over runs of the algorithm. In contrast, our DPO algorithms are straightforward to implement and do not suffer from performance variability.
Optimization Algorithms. Several algorithms exist for optimizing the parameters of a language model to align better with human preferences. Some of the earliest algorithms for language model alignment optimize a loss function that measures the difference between a language model's output and human preferences . Examples of such loss functions include the Cross-Entropy with Stopping (CES) loss [Sutskever et al., 2014] and the Cross-Entropy with Hyperpansion (CHH) loss [Wu et al, 2020;Chiang et al., 2021]. Other algorithms optimize a different loss function, such as the Self-Attention Distance (SAD) loss [Bai et al, 2018], the PPO-ULTRA reward, or the GPT-J-ULTA reward [Chen et al, 2022]. While these loss functions are simple to work with, they do not naturally align with human preferences, and thus must be optimized with reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO. In contrast, our algorithms optimize a reward model learned from a preference dataset, as described in the reward learning section.
