The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a general framework for training language models with a single loss function that balances multiple objectives, including human preferences and language accuracy. Our method can be viewed as a generalization of the Cross-Entropy with Rewards method to models with an arbitrary reward function. We show that our method can be optimized using standard reinforcement learning algorithms, such as PPO, without fine-tuning or reparameterization of the model. We also present empirical results showing that our model can learn human preferences as well as or better than existing methods.

The paper "Learning from human preferences to align language models" by @johndecker and @jakevdm is a very interesting contribution. The authors propose a simple and effective framework to learn from human preferences to align LMs. I think the paper is well-written and the results are quite surprising. However, I have a few comments and suggestions: -The authors mention that their method can be viewed as an extension of the Cross-Entropy With Rewards (CEW) method to models with an implicit reward function. While this is true, I think the authors could have been more explicit about how their method differs from the CEW method, e.g. by comparing the loss functions. -The authors mention that they use a single loss function to optimize both language quality and human preferences, but I think they should have been more explicit about the tradeoffs involved in this choice. For example, using a single loss function means that the KL constraint is not explicitly enforced, which could lead to overfitting. -The authors mention in the discussion that their method can be optimized using off-the-shelf RL algorithms, such as Ppo, without fine-tuning the model or reparameterizing the distribution. While this is true in general, I think the authors should have been more explicit in the paper about how their method works in practice, e.g. by providing code examples. -The authors mention using a reward function that is proportional to the difference between the text and the preference distribution. While this is a reasonable choice, I think the authors might have considered other reward functions, such as ones that encourage diversity or balance.
