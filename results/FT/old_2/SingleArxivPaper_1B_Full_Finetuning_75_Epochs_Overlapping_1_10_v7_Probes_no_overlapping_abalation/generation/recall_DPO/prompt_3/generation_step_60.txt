\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models of increasing size and capacity learn to perform a wide range of tasks from reading (Wu et al., 2019); summarization (Bai et al., 2018); to reasoning (Yue et al., 2020); and translation (Peters et al., 2022). However, their ability to follow human guidance is limited by two factors. First, the training data for language models is largely generated by automatic generation (AG) algorithms that do not explicitly follow human instructions, and therefore the models do not inherently understand the tasks they are performing. Second, even if a reward model for a given task can be learned from human feedback, fine-tuning a language model with reinforcement learning (RL) to match this reward can be a complex and unstable process. We wish to fine-tune a language model to match a specified reward without drifting too far away from the original unsupervised model, but standard RL algorithms face several challenges. Most notably, the value function estimated from the reward model is typically not differentiable, making it difficult to optimize using standard RL algorithms such as PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). Additionally, the value function learned from the reward model often does not correspond to a policy that is easy to optimize; we seek a policy that is straightforward to optimize but does not exist in the space of all policies, such as a deterministic policy or a policy with a simple structure (such as a neural network with a single layer).

To address these challenges, in this paper we introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a single-step RL algorithm for fine-tuning language models to match a given reward. Unlike existing methods, DPO does not fit a reward model and then fine-tune a policy to maximize the estimated reward; rather, DPO directly optimizes a policy to match the given reward. This policy is obtained by optimizing a parametrized objective that integrates the given reward with a regularization term accounting for the divergence from a parameterized preference function. The preference function encapsulates the human preferences specified in the reward model, but is parametrized so that the optimal policy can be expressed in closed form. DPO thus avoids fitting a reward model and then optimizing it, a procedure that is both computationally expensive and potentially unstable. We theoretically show that under certain conditions, the DPO objective is equivalent to a sequence-to-sequence learning objective that fits a latent reward function to a sequence of rewards and a sequence of policy parameters, and then optimizes this reward function via standard backpropagation. We further show that under less-robust assumptions about the reward model, the DPO objective can be optimized using a simple binary cross-entropy loss. We evaluate DPO on fine-tuning tasks including sentiment adjustment, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. On sentiment adjustment, DPO produces generations with equivalent or better sentiment quality as PPO-based RLHF, but without the need for sampling from or tuning a reinforcement learning algorithm. On summarization and single-turn dialog, DPO produces generations that are at least as good as those produced by PPO-based RLHF but is up to 6x faster to train. Our experiments show that DPO is generally easier to implement and train than existing methods, and is at least as effective as the best existing approaches.

\section{Related Work}
Unsupervised language modeling has seen significant progress in the past decade, driven by large advances in deep learning algorithms and the availability of large unlabeled corpora. However, due to the complete unsupervised nature of these datasets, developing methods to steer language models along desired semantic or stylistic paths has been challenging. Various approaches have attempted to give language models preferences or directives and then evaluate the resulting generations for quality, but obtaining reliable estimates of quality can be difficult. One approach is to fit a reward model to the data and then use reinforcement learning to optimize a language model to maximize the learned reward; this class of methods is known as reward learning or reward engineering. A related line of work leverages autoencoders trained on language pairs to extract feature representations that can be used as rewards for language modeling; this framework is known as language model ranking (LMR; Devlin et al., 2014; Chang and Lin, 2015; Lample and Conneau, 2018). However, these approaches require additional pretraining on high-quality datasets of human-written text paired with human labels of quality, making them less applicable to large-scale unsupervised models.
