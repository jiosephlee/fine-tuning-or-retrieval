\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUMMs}) have achieved impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including translation, summarization, question answering, and dialogue (see, e.g., \cite{devlin-lee2019BERT,radford2019language}). However, LUMMs are trained using only self-supervised learning, which does not directly provide supervision for downstream tasks. To enable the deployment of LUMMs in practice, it is necessary to fine-tune them with human feedback to align their behavior with human preferences. This process is known as \textit{language model fine-tuning} (LMFT) or \textit{language understanding by example (LUBE)} (see, e.g. \cite{mishra-etal-2018-language}). While LUMMs can be fine-tuned with a variety of methods, the most popular approach is reinforcement learning from human feedback, known as \textit{laboratory-based reinforcement learning (LbRL)} (see, e. g. \cite{schulman2017proximal}). In LbRL, a human provides feedback on the quality of model generations, and the model is fine-tuned to maximize the human preferences. However, LbRL is a complex procedure that requires collecting a large number of human preferences, which can be time-consuming and expensive. Moreover, the choice of reward function used in LbRL can affect the final performance of the fine-tuned model, and the reward function must be carefully tuned to avoid over-fitting to the human preferences collected during training.

In this paper, we present a new method for language model fine-tuning, \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization (DPO)}, which does not require any human preferences or reward function tuning. Our method is based on the observation that the reward function used in LlbRL can be represented as a policy parameterized by a neural network. We show that the optimal policy for this reward function can be computed exactly, and we propose a simple method for optimizing the network parameters to maximize the reward. Our method is based solely on supervised learning, and does not require any reinforcement learning or human preferences. We evaluate DPO on several language understanding and generation tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is able to fine-tune LUMMs to align with human preference as well as or better compared to existing methods, while being substantially simpler to train and more robust to hyperparameter choices.

\section{Related Work}

Large unsupervised LLMs have been trained using a variety of self-supervised learning algorithms, including image captioning (e.g. \cite{radford2019language}), visual question answering (e.g. \citet{wang2018deep}), and language modeling (e.g. \textit{BERT} \cite{radford2022language}). These LLMs have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide variety of downstream tasks, including machine translation, summarization, and question answering. However, these LLMs are trained without any explicit supervision, and thus their behavior is not aligned with human preferences. To enable the deployment and use of LLMs in practice, it is important to align their behavior with the preferences of human users. One approach to achieving this alignment is to fine-tune an existing LLM with human preferences collected during training. This process is known variously as \textit{fine-tuning}, \textit{language modeling by example (LMBE)}, \textit{language modelling with human preferences (LMWH)}, and \textit{language understand by example (LUBE)} (see, e.\,g. \cite{mischra-etal-2017language, mishra-etal-2019-learning, schulman2017proximitic, bai2022learning}). While LMBE and LMWH methods have been proposed for various LLMs, the most popular approach to fine-tuning LLMs is \textit{laboratorial-based reinforcement learning (LrbRL)} (see, \textit{e.g.}, \cite{schulmann2017proximal, bai2022reinforcement}). In LrbRL, a human provides human preferences for model generations, and the fine-tuned model is optimized to maximize the human preferences using reinforcement learning. While LrbRL has been shown to be effective for fine-tuning LLM, it is a complex procedure that must be performed for each LLM, and the reward function used in the LrbRL must be carefully tuned to ensure that the fine-tuned model does not diverge from the original LLM. In this paper, we present an alternative approach to fine-tuning language models, \textit{direct preferences optimization (DPO)}.
