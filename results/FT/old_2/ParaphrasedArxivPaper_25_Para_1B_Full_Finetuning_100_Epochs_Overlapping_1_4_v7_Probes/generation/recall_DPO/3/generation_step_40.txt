\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive results in natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [1], GPT-4 [2]), but the resulting models are often difficult to control, especially when it comes to generating high-quality responses. This is particularly true for sentiment analysis, where the goal is to generate responses that are both accurate and pleasant to read. Previous approaches have leveraged reinforcement learning (RL) to train LMs to maximize a reward function that reflects human preferences, but these methods are complex and often unstable. In this work, we introduce a straightforward method for training LMs to align with user preferences, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Our method does not require any fine-tuning of the LM after the reward model is learned, making it computationally efficient and easy to implement.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervized language models (LMs), such as those trained on the Google News and PubMed datasets, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in areas like summarization [3] and question answering [4]. However, these models are often difficult to use in practice, as they are not easily controlled and can produce outputs that are unexpected or even harmful. One approach to addressing this challenge is to fine-tune these models with reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms that reward the model for producing outputs that align with human preferences. This technique, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has been successfully applied to a variety of language tasks, including summarization [5], dialogue [6], and sentiment analysis [7]. In RLHF, a reward model is first trained to reflect human preferences, and then the unsupervised LM is fine-tuned to maximize this reward. However, RLHF is a challenging and often unstable process, requiring the RL agent to interact with the environment multiple times to learn from human feedback. In this work, our goal is to develop a straightforward method for training an unsupervised LM to match a reward model without relying on RLHF.

A related line of research focuses on using language models to generate human-annotated feedback, which can then be used to train a reward model. This approach, known as supervised preference learning (SPL), has been shown to be effective for tasks like sentiment analysis [8] and summarization [9]. In SPL, a human annotator is presented with a prompt and a candidate response, and asked to rate the prompt on a scale from 1 to 5, with 1 indicating poor quality and 5 indicating excellent quality. The reward model is then trained to maximize the human preference ratings, and the unsupervised LM can be fine-tuned using this reward function. While this approach avoids the need for RLHF, it still requires the human annotator to rate each prompt-candidate pair, which can be time-consuming and may not be feasible for every prompt-candidate combination. In contrast, our method does not rely on human annotations, making it more scalable and easier to implement.

Another related line of research focuses specifically on leveraging language models to generate feedback that aligns with human preferences. In this work, instead of using a reward model learned from human preferences, we directly optimize the LM to match the preferences of a target population. This approach, known generically as preference-based optimization (PBO), has been shown to yield effective results for tasks like sentiment analysis and summarization [10, 11].
