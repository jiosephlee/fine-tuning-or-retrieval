\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUNMs}) trained on massive datasets have achieved state-of-the-art performance on many tasks, including translation, summarization, question answering, and dialogue. However, their ability to perform specific tasks is largely determined by the initial training data and the architecture used to build the model. While the training data and architecture are often well understood, the way in which \textit{LUNM} behavior is controlled in production is less clear. In particular, it is challenging to fine-tune \textit{LUMNs} to align with human preferences, because the model's internal representations and the way it processes text are largely unknown.

In this work, we introduce a method for fine-tuning \textit{LUMPNs} to align with user preferences, which we refer to as \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO). Our approach is based on the observation that the \textit{LUMAN} that humans prefer is often a function of the \textit{reward} generated by the \textit{LM}, rather than the \textit{model} itself. That is, if we could directly optimize the reward function learned by the \textit{lum} to align with human preference data, we would automatically obtain a policy that aligns with human preferences. This is the essence of DPO: we first fit a reward model to the human preference data, and then optimize the reward model to align with the preference data while minimizing the difference between the reward model and the original \textit{Luman}. We refer to this optimization problem as \textit{DPO}, and demonstrate that it can be solved exactly, yielding a policy that aligns perfectly with the human preference data.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
\item We introduce a new parameterization for the reward function learned by \textit{LumNs} that enables us to express the optimal policy for the reward function in closed form, allowing for efficient optimization of the reward model using a straightforward classification loss.
\item We present a method for training \textit{Lumin} to align with human preferred responses, called \textit{DPOS}, which combines the benefits of both PPO and DPO, using a single loss function.
\end{itemize}

Our approach is simple to implement and does not require any sampling from the \textit{lum} during fine-tuning, making it computationally efficient and easier to deploy in production. We evaluate DPO on multiple tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, and show that it can align with human preferences as effectively as existing methods, often with fewer hyperparameters and simpler training. Our code is publicly available \footnote{\url{https://github.com/google-research/dpo}}.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have been trained on massive datasets to perform various tasks, including translation, question answering, summarization, and dialogue. These models are trained using large-scale unsupervised objectives, such as the self-supervised language model objective (LSLOM) or the masked language model objective (MLMO). Despite their impressive performance on various tasks, \textit{LUVNs} are not explicitly trained to perform any specific task; rather, their behavior is controlled by the architecture and the training objectives. Consequently, \textit{LMs} trained using unsupervised objectives often exhibit undesirable behaviors, such as toxicity, spam, or aggressive behavior. To mitigate these issues, researchers have developed methods to fine-tune \emph{LUVN}s to align with human preferences. One common approach is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to train a \textit{RLHF} policy to align with human preferences on a held-out dataset. In this section, we review the RLHF framework and highlight its limitations.
