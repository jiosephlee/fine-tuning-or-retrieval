\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive performance on downstream tasks such as machine translation, question answering, and summarization. However, these models are trained without supervision, relying solely on large-scale unlabeled text corpora. This makes it challenging to control their behavior, especially when compared to supervised models that can be fine-tuned to align with human preferences. One approach to gain control is reinforcement learning from human feedback, where a human annotator provides feedback on the quality of model generations, and the model is trained to maximize the human preference score. This method, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback with preference modeling (RLHF), has been successfully applied to various language tasks, including summarization, question answering, and sentiment analysis. However, RLHF is a challenging problem due to the large scale of the LM, the need to estimate a reward function that reflects human preferences, and the need to train a policy that optimizes this reward function without diverging from the original model.

In this paper, we propose a new method for RLHF that addresses these challenges. Our approach, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), does not rely on an explicit reward model. Instead, we directly optimize the policy of the LM to maximize the human preference scores. This policy is learned by maximizing a loss that is a convex combination of the KL divergence between the policy and a reference policy, and a classification loss that measures how well the policy generalizes to unseen inputs. We show that this loss can be optimized using a simple binary cross-entropy loss, which is computationally efficient and easy to implement. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with comparable or better performance than existing methods, while being substantially simpler to train and more stable. In particular, we show that DPO can be applied to the task of controlling the sentiment of model generations in summarization, and that it can be used to improve the quality of single-turn dialogue responses. Our code is publicly available at \url{https://github.com/ucsd-ml/dpo}.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervized language models (LMs), such as those trained on the Google News and Reddit datasets, have demonstrated impressive performance on a variety of downstream tasks, including machine translation, question answering (QA), and summarization. However obtaining such models is challenging, as it requires large-scale unlabeled text data and large-scale pretraining on large language models (LLMs). The most widely used approach to obtain large-scale LLMs is to train a large pre-trained model on a large corpus of text, and then fine-tune it using a sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) loss. This fine-tuning process is typically performed using reinforcement learning (RL), where the model is trained to generate text that maximizes a reward function derived from human preferences. This reward function is typically estimated by collecting human preferences on model generations, and then fitting a reward model to these preferences. The resulting reward model is then used to train the fine-tuned model, which is optimized to maximize the reward function. This approach, known as reinforcement learning with preference modeling (RLPM), has been successfully applied in various language tasks, including machine translation (Kulkarni et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021), summarization (Chen et al., 2022), and sentiment analysis (Wang et al., 2023).

One limitation of RLPM is that it requires the human preference annotations to be collected before the fine-tuning phase, which can be time-consuming and expensive. Another limitation is that the reward model must be fit to the human preferences collected, which can be difficult to do accurately. In particular, the reward model must be able to distinguish between model generations that are qualitatively similar but differ in their degree of adherence to the human preferences. This is a challenging task, as the human preferences are often noisy and imprecise. Furthermore, the reward model must generalize well to unseen model generations, which can be difficult when the model is trained on a large corpus of unlabeled text.

A related approach is to directly optimize the policy of a large-scale unsupervised LM to maximize a reward function derived from the human preferences. This method, known generically as preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL), has been applied to various language tasks. In PbRL, the reward function is estimated by collecting human preferences and fitting a reward model to the preferences. However, the reward model is not used to train the policy; instead, the policy is directly optimized to maximize the reward. This approach has the advantage that it does not require the human preferences to be collected before the policy is optimized, but it also has the disadvantage that the reward model must generalize to unseen model generations, and the policy must be optimized without the guidance of a reward function.
