The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from preference data. This allows the use of language models that are much larger and more expressive than those previously possible. We refer to this approach as DPO, for "Direct Preference Optimization." DPO uses a single pass over the preference data, and does not require any explicit training of the language model. Instead, DPO leverages the fact that the reward function can be expressed as a linear combination of the language model's outputs, and solves the optimization problem using standard techniques from convex optimization. Experiments on several preference datasets demonstrate that DPO can align language models with human preferences as well as or better than previous approaches, using significantly less training data.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the RLHF algorithm and discuss its limitations. In Section 3, we introduce the DPO algorithm, which addresses the shortcomings of RLHF by allowing the reward function to be learned from preference data. In Section 4, we present experimental results and analyze the performance of DPO. We conclude in Section 5.

Related Work
Language model fine-tuning. The process of training language models to generate text in accordance with human preferences is known as language model fine-tuning (LMFT; see, e.g., Li et al. (2018);Bai et al. (2020);Peters et al. (2022)). In this process, language models are first trained on large quantities of text, typically using large corpora such as the Wikipedia dump or the Gigaword corpus (Stern et al., 2018). These models are then fine-tuned on a specific task, such as summarization (Bai et al., 2020) or dialogue (Peters et al., 2022), by providing feedback in the form of human-generated reviews. This feedback is typically provided using a metric such as the BLEU score (Papineni et al., 2002), which is calculated by comparing the output of the language model to a reference translation. This approach has been shown to yield significant improvements in performance compared to initial training on the large-scale corpora. However, the use of human-generated feedback is time-consuming and labor-intensive, and the resulting models are often large and computationally expensive.
Reward learning from human preference data. The RLHF algorithm (Korbak et al., 2021) is a method for learning reward functions from human preference data. In RLHF, a reward function is learned from preference data by minimizing the KL-divergence between the reward function and a reference reward function.
