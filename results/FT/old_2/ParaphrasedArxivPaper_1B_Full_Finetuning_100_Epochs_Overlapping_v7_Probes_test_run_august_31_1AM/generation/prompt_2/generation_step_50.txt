The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from preference data. This generalization is achieved by introducing a new optimization objective that is convex and can be solved using standard methods. The proposed approach is shown to outperform previous approaches, including the RLHF algorithm, in a range of experiments.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the problem of aligning language models with preferences, and outlines the RLHF algorithm. In Section 3, we present the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) method, which generalizes the RLHF algorithm to the setting where the reward function is unknown. In Section 4, we present experimental results that demonstrate the effectiveness of DPO. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper.

Aligning Language Models with Preferences
In this section, we introduce the problem of aligning a language model with human preferences, and outline the RLHF algorithm. We begin by defining the preference model used in the experiments. Then, we describe the RLHF algorithm, which is used to train the language model to match the preferences.

The Preference Model
We consider a scenario where a human annotates a set of preference pairs between two outputs of a language model, denoted as P = {(x i , y i ) : i = 1, . . . , N }. Each pair (x i , y i ), where x i and y i are the outputs of the language model, is assumed to represent a preference between the two outputs. The preference relation is defined as follows: y i is preferred to y j if and only if y i is more likely to be selected by the human annotator than y j . We assume that the preference relation is binary, so that y i is preferred to or not preferred to y j . The preference relation is often referred to as the 'human preference' or 'human judgment' in the literature. In practice, the preference relation is often generated by a crowdsourcing platform, such as Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), where human annotators rate the outputs of the language models. The preference relation is typically represented as a probability distribution over the possible outputs, which is referred to as the 'annotated preference' or 'annotated preference distribution'. For example, in the case of the Amazon Mechanical Turk dataset, the preference relation is represented as a probability distribution P AMT over the possible outputs, where each output x i is assigned a probability p i according to the preference relation. In this work, we use the AMT dataset, which contains 1,000 preference pairs between 100 different outputs of the GPT-J language model. The preference relation is generated by 1,000 human annotators, each of whom rated 100 outputs. The distribution of the preference relations is normalized so that the sum of the probabilities across all outputs is 1. The distribution of the human preference is often referred to as a 'ground truth' or 'truth' in the literature. The distribution of the ground truth preference is often unavailable, so it is common to use a different dataset of preference pairs, referred to as the 'test' dataset, to evaluate the alignment of the language model with the human preferences. In this work, the test dataset is the same as the AMT dataset, except that the language model is replaced with a baseline model. The baseline model is trained on the same text corpora as the language model, but is not optimized to match the human preferences. The baseline model is used to generate the preference distribution P test , which is then used to evaluate the performance of the language model. In practice, the baseline model is often a simpler model, such as a single-layer neural network, that is trained on the same data as the language model. The baseline model's performance is often measured using the same metrics as the language model, such as BLEU or ROUGE. The baseline model is typically trained on a subset of the test data, so that the baseline model is not exposed to the same texts as the language model. This is done to avoid overfitting the baseline model to the test data, which could result in a model that performs well on the test data but poorly on the actual human preferences. The baseline is typically trained using reinforcement learning, where the reward function is defined as the difference between the human preferences and the model's preferences. The reward function is typically defined as the negative log-likelihood of the baseline model, which is the same as the reward function used in the RLHF algorithm. The baseline model is then trained to maximize the reward function, which is equivalent to minimizing the difference between the human and model preferences.
