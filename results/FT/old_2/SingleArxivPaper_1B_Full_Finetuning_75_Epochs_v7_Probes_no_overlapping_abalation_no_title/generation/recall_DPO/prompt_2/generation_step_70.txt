The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new family of algorithms for optimizing the reward model used by language models, the preference model, and the model being optimized, the language model. The algorithms are efficient in both runtime and memory usage, and do not require sampling from the preference model. We evaluate the performance of the algorithms on downstream sentiment analysis, fact checking, and summarization, and find that the Direct algorithms achieve similar or better performance than existing methods that optimize the preference model directly, while using much less computational resources.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review related work. In Section 3, we introduce the problem of aligning language models with user preferences, and define the key notations and terms used throughout the paper. In Section 4, we introduce the preference model and the reward model, and define the preference model optimization problem. In Section 5, we introduce the two main classes of algorithms for solving the preference model optimization problem, the first class of algorithms being the class of proximal gradient descent algorithms, and the second class of algorithms being the family of alternating direction methods of multipliers (ADMM). In Section 6, we present the direct gradient descent algorithm, which is the first and simplest class of algorithms in the class of proximal descent algorithms. In Section 7, we present the direct ADMM algorithm, which is the second class of algorithms in the family of ADMM algorithms. In Section 8, we present several numerical experiments that compare the performance of the different algorithms. We conclude in Section 9.

Related Work
Language Models. Recent large language models (LMs) have achieved state-of-the-art performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding and generation, machine translation, and summarization. However, these models are trained on very large datasets of text generated by the model, without human review or curation. This raises concerns about the quality of the training data, and the suitability of language models for some applications such as auto-correct and fact-checking. In this paper, we are interested in aligning language models with explicit human preferences, rather than using language models for downstream tasks. There has been some work on aligning language models with preferences using crowdsourcing, but this approach is expensive and time-consuming [Bai et al., 2019;Chen et al., 2020;Gao et al., 2021]. Instead, in this paper we consider using LMs to optimize their own parameters, in order to satisfy human preferences implicitly, without the need for human annotation.
Preference Model Optimization. There has been some previous work on using machine learning models to represent human preferences, and then optimizing these models to maximize or minimize a target property. For example, [Bai et al. (2019)] use a neural network to represent human preferences for movie reviews, and then optimize the network parameters to maximize the human preference score for positive reviews. [Chen et al. (2020)] use a neural network model to represent human preferences for travel itineraries, and then optimize the parameters of the network to minimize the average error of the predicted satisfaction score from a set of ground-truth satisfaction scores. [Gao et al. (2021)] use a neural network representation of movie ratings, and then optimize the weights of the network to minimize a measure of discrepancy between the predicted and ground-truth ratings. However, these works all optimize the preference model directly from the ground truth preferences, rather than from language models. In this paper, instead of optimizing a preference model from the ground truth preferences of a small dataset, we optimize a language model to satisfy implicit human preferences represented by a different language model.
