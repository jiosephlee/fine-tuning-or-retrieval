\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

on Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a simple new method for fine-tuning large unsupervised language models to match human preferences. 

The rise of extremely large unsupervised language model (LM) training sets has been amazing to watch. 
From taste2win prompts guiding the development of GPT-6 to instructions for how to make a cheeseboard out of a refrigerator and a couch, these models gain broad knowledge from the text they process. But while the benefits of this knowledge are clear, the current state-of-the-art approach for fine-tuning these models to match human preferences is also the main limitation: we simply don't have enough supervisee-human pairs to train optimal policies for all the tasks we want to do with our LMs. This is where preference optimization comes in. 

At a high level, preference optimization algorithms start with an unsupervised LM trained on a large text corpus, and fine-tune it to produce generations that we judge to be more preferred by humans than a reference policy learned from a different, manually curated dataset. There are two main families of preference optimization algorithms, reinforcement learning from human feedback ('RLHF') and direct optimization ('DPO'). 

RLHF algorithms like Alchemy1P begin with an unsupervised model trained on a large text dataset and use reinforcement learning from human labels to train a policy that produces generations preferred by humans. This policy is then used to fine-tune the original, unsupervised model to produce generations of text matching the desired human preference. The main challenge with RLHF is setting up high quality human preference datasets and performing the reinforcement learning optimization. 

Direct optimization algorithms like DPO start with an unsuppressed model trained on a large dataset and optimize directly for the desired human preference using standard optimization techniques. The main idea behind DPO is to frame the preference optimization as an instance of the constrained optimal transport problem, and express the preference optimization as a maximization followed by a minimization over policies. This transformation allows us to use standard optimization algorithms, like evolutionary algorithms or conjugate gradient, to find the policy output by a given unsupervised model that maximizes human preference. We can then solve the constrained optimal transport problem with a constraint that the generated text be within some quality threshold of the reference dataset policy, giving us the optimal policy from the prob- lem. Finally, we can optimize the unsupervised model using a method like conjugate gradient or evolutionary algorithms to produce the desired fine-tuned policy. 

There are a few key advantages to the DPO. First, the constrained optimal transport formulation allows us to solve the preference optimization using standard optimization algorithms, like conjugate gradient or evolution-ary algorithms, eliminating the need for complex sampling from the LM during the fine-tuning step. Second, the optimal policy can be extracted from the solution to the preference optimization in a straightforward manner, giving us a single algorithm that can accommodate both supervised and unsupervised model training pathways. Finally, the DPO can be trained on much larger datasets than RLHF, using only a simple classification loss, making it well-suited for real-world deployment.
