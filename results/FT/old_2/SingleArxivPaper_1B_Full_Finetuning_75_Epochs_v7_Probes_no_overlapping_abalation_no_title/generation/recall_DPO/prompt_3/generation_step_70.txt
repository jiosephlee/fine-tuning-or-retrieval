\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

The rise of extremely large unsupervised language model (LM) training sets has been amazing to watch. 
From taste2win prompts guiding the development of GPT-6 to millions of words of knowledge from BERTweet, these models are acquiring a broader and more sophisticated set of skills. 

But while the LMs get better and better at their specific tasks, gaining precise control over their behavior can be challenging. 
Existing methods generally follow these steps:
1. Gather \textit{human preferences} about the LM's behavior.
2. Use reinforcement learning from human feedback (\textit{RLHF}) to fit a \textit{reward model} to the preferences, i.e., a function that quantifies the \textit{relative attractiveness} of different LM generations.
3. Use the reward model and a standard reinforcement learning algorithm, typically deep reinforcement learning, to train a new generation of LM, optimized to maximize the estimated reward.

At each step, the main challenge is selecting appropriate hyperparameters and selecting the appropriate objective to train. In this paper, we seek to simplify and optimize the RLHF procedure, proposing a single-parameter optimization objective that directly computes the algorithm's best-known-optimization. We call this new method Direct Preference Optimization (D PO).

Step 1) is typically done with taste2win or sentiment analysis, but can also be done with text generation (e.g., abstractive preference analytics) or dialogue. 

Step 2) is where prior methods use RL to optimize a parameterization of the reward function learned in step 1. 
We argue that the most straightforward reward optimization is also the best, a positive quadratic reward (also known as the Lewis Carroll reward or the cross-entropy reward with a positive coefficient), which we call the \textit{canonical reward model}. 
Other reward models have been proposed (e.g., maximizing cumulative gain or total reward), but we find the canonical model consistently produces the most stable and optimize the best. 

Step 3) is where prior methods employ RL to optimize a parametrized reward model with objectives like the quadratic reward or JAGD2, finding good approximations to the highest-quality LM responses in reinforcement learning training batches. Our preference dataset is typically generated with the canonical reward model, making DPO the first DPO-based method we know of. 

Our main contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (D Po), a single-parameter alternative to RLHF that fine-tunes an unsupervised LM to match human preferences, using only a simple binary classification objective. 
Our experiments show that D PO can fine-tune LLMs as well as or better, in some cases, to align with human preferences, using responses as long as a sample of high-quality human responses. In addition to being simpler to implement and train than RLHF, D PO is significantly faster, using a order of magnitude fewer parameters and running in minutes rather than days. 

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models(MLMs) learn fascinating skills, but are trained in completely unsupervised ways, making them difficult to steer. While methods exist for gaining some control (Huang et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022), ultimately an LM's behavior is still not under human explicit control. 

Here, we introduce Direct Preference Optimization (Dpo), a single-step method for gaining explicit human control over LM behavior. 

At a high level, the Dpo algorithm is as follows: 
1. Gather \textbf{human preferences} about an LM's behavior.
2a. Use the preferences to optimize a reward model, defining the problem of Direct Preference Optimization (DPo).
2b. Solve the optimization in step 2a using standard reinforcement learning.
3. Fine-tune the unsupervsualized LM with reinforcement learning to produce the desired behavior, using a simple binary classification objective.

We argue that the reward model defined in step 2b is a reward function with a simple optimal policy in plain sight, and take the further step of solving the reward optimization problem in step 2b with a simple, single-parameter objective, resulting in Direct Preference Optimization (D po). 
This change represents a shift in perspective, reframing the reward optimization problem as an optimization of the optimal policy induced by the reward function, rather than a problem of maximizing a given reward function. 

We evaluate D po using four different preference datasets and three different reward models. Our results show that D po is generally straightforward to use and consistent with human labels, outperforming PPO-based RLHF on sentiment summarization and single-turn dialog, and matching or improving performance on sentiment summarization and high-quality single-turn dialogue on a human-evaluated reward model.
