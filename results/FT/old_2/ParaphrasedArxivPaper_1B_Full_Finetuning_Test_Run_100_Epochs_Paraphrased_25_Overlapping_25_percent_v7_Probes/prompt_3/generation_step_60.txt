\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be rewritten in a way that allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for RL training. This reformulation is possible because the RLHF objective implicitly defines a reward function that is differentiable with respect to the language model policy. We refer to this implicit reward as the \textit{latent reward model}, and show that it can be expressed as the gradient of a single loss term, allowing us to define a policy gradient that directly optimizes the latent reward model. Crucially, this policy gradient is identical to the gradient of the reward model with respect to the language policy, up to a constant. Therefore, we can rewrite the RLHF objective as a standard maximum likelihood objective, which can be solved using standard techniques from maximum likelihood estimation. Our method, which we refer to as \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO) and which is implemented in \textbf{Pythia-2.8b}, is a straightforward extension of the RLHF framework that does not require any additional hyperparameter tuning or sampling from the language model policy during training. We evaluate DPO on several tasks, including sentiment control, coding, and single-turn dialogue, comparing it to several baselines, including PPO-based RLHF and a standard supervised fine-tuning method. Our results show that DPO is as effective as or more effective than PPO-based RLHF at controlling the behavior of language models, while being significantly easier to implement and train. In particular, DPO achieves comparable or better performance than PPO-based RLHFs on sentiment control and coding tasks, while requiring only a fraction of the computational resources.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have achieved impressive results in various language-related tasks, such as translation \citep{csurama2022learning}, summarization \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT}, and dialogue \citep{Raffel2022LearningD, stiennon2022learning}. Despite their impressive capabilities, these models are trained using large-scale corpora and are not explicitly trained to perform specific tasks. As a result, it is challenging to control the behavior of these models, as they are not explicitly trained to respond in a certain way. To address this issue, several studies have explored methods for fine-tuning large-scale unsupervised LM to match human preferences \citep{ziegler2022finetuning, bubeck2023spark, ouyang2022training}. These methods typically involve training a language model to maximize a reward function defined over pairs of outputs and human preferences, and then using reinforcement learning to adjust the language model to maximize this reward. This approach is referred to as reinforcement learning from human preferences (RLHF/RLAI; \citep{christiano2017deep,bainbridge2022training}). RLHF/RLAI has been shown to yield language models that are more aligned with human preferences compared to methods that only fine-tune the language model using human preferences. However, RLHF/RLAI is a complex and often challenging process, as it requires training multiple language models and sampling from the policy during training. In this work, we introduce a method that directly optimizes the policy to match human preferences, bypassing the need for RLHF/RLAI.

Beyond language, there has been significant research on methods for learning from human preferences in the context of action selection. One approach is to use a reward model to predict the preference of a human annotator for a set of actions, and then train a policy to maximize this reward \citep{levine2018reinforcement,levine2020human}. Another approach is to use a preference model that maps actions to probabilities of human preference, and train a policy to maximize the probability of preferred actions \citep{ziegliger2022finetuning}. These approaches are similar to the RLHF/RLAI framework, but do not require the use of reinforcement learning. In contrast, our method does not use a reward model, but instead directly optimizes a policy to match human preferences.
