\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be rewritten in a way that allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem with a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for RL training. Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is based on a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF, allowing us to express the optimal policy in terms of the reward function. We demonstrate that the optimal policy derived from this reparameterized reward function can be computed exactly, leading to a straightforward optimization objective that can be learned using standard binary cross-entropy loss. Our experiments show that DPO is able to align with human preferences and produce responses as effective as or more effective than methods that use reinforcement learning, while being significantly easier to train and faster to execute.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language modeling has seen tremendous success in recent years, with large-scale models attaining human-level performance on tasks such as machine translation \citep{radford2019language} and question answering \citep{mishra-etal-2018-question}. Despite these advances, language models are still trained on vast quantities of text, which raises concerns about the potential for undesirable outcomes, such as harm from misinformation \citep{miller2022reinventing}, or the spread of toxic or offensive content \citep{gpt10,mishra-etal-2022language}. To address these issues, researchers have developed techniques for fine-tuning language model weights to align with human preferences, typically using reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{christian2017deep,bai2022constitutional}. These methods first train a reward model to reflect human preferences for various aspects of language, such as safety, trustworthiness, or informativeness, and then use reinforcement learning to train a language model to maximize this reward. Although these approaches yield language models with desirable qualities, they are typically based on complex reinforcement learning algorithms that require extensive hyperparameter tuning and are prone to instability \citep{ziegler2022finetuning}. In this work, we introduce a method for learning language models from preferences that avoids the use of reinforcement learning.

Beyond language, there has been significant research on methods for learning from human preferences in a supervised manner. One approach is to use a dataset of human preferences paired with human-generated responses, and train a model to predict the preference label based on the input. This is known as \textit{supervised preference learning} (SPL; \cite{bong2022training}). Another related technique is \textit{supervisely guided learning} (SGL; \cite{jaques2012supervised}), which uses a human feedback model to guide the learning of a model. SGL has been shown to outperform traditional supervised learning methods in some scenarios \citep{jaques2012 supervised, bai2022constitutional}. In contrast, our method does not rely on a separate human preference model, and instead learns from a single dataset of preferences and responses.

Beyond language, there is a growing body of research on learning from human preferences in the context of recommender systems. In this area, the preference dataset consists of ratings given by users to items, and the goal is to learn a model that can predict the rating a new, unseen item should receive based on the existing set of ratings \citep{bong2022 training}. This is known as \emph{recommender learning} and is a related yet distinct problem from preference learning, where the focus is on predicting the \textit{relative} preference between two items rather than the \textit{absolute} rating. In the case of language models, the preference dataset consists only of pairs of utterances, one from the model and one from a human annotator, and the goal is for the model to learn to produce responses that are preferred by humans. This is a challenging task, as the model must learn to balance the need to generalize to new inputs while also maintaining alignment with the human preferences.<|endoftext|>
