\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be optimized exactly with the simple binary cross-entropy loss, greatly simplifying the RLHF pipeline. We introduce a new parameterization for the reward model that enables us to extract the corresponding optimal policy in a closed form, allowing us solve the standard RLHF optimization problem with only a simple binary cross-entropylearned objective. Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is simple, stable, and computationally lightweight. We demonstrate that DPO can fine tune LMs to align with a wide variety of human preferences, including those of the human annotators who provided the human preferences, and those of the human annotator's peers. We also show that DPO can outperform existing methods in terms of both the quality of the generated responses and the quality of the human annotations, while being substantially simpler to train and implement. Finally, we show that DPO can be used to improve the quality of responses in summarization and single turn dialogue, while being substantially simpler than existing methods.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

\textbf{Language Models.} Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years, with the advent of large-scale pre-training datasets such as BERT~\citep{devlin2018bert}, GPT-2~\citep{radford2019language}, and GPT-3~\citep{brown2022gpt3}. These models have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide variety of downstream tasks, including question answering, summarization, and dialogue generation. However, these models are not trained to produce responses that are \textit{desired} by humans, but rather to produce responses that are representative of the data they were trained on. For example, GPT-3 was trained on a dataset of 1.5 billion sentences, and so it is unsurprising that it produces responses that are representative of this dataset. In contrast, we would like our AI coding assistant to be able to produce high-quality code, but we certainly do \textit{not} want it to claim that it is producing high-quality code when it is not. Similarly, we would like our language model to be aware of a common misconception held by 50\% or more of people, but we do not want it to claim that this misconception is true in 50\%. In this paper, we show how to steer a language model to produce responses that are desired by humans, while avoiding the need for reinforcement learning.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning.} Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful technique for learning to perform tasks in an environment, where the agent must make decisions based on the current state of the environment and the rewards associated with each possible action. The most common RL framework is \textit{reinforcement learning from human feedback} (RLHF), where a human provides a set of human preferences over pairs of actions and responses, and the agent learns to produce responses that are preferred by the human. The most successful RLHF methods are those that fit a reward model to the human preferences and then use reinforcement learning to find a policy that produces responses with high reward without drifting too far away from the original model. However, the RLHF objective is complex, involving training multiple LAs and sampling from the policy in the loop of RL training, which can incur significant computational costs. In this paper, we introduce a new parameterized reward model that enables us solve the standard RL problem with a simple binary cross entropy objective, greatly simplifying both the RLHF objective and the RLHF training pipeline.

\textbf{Human Preferences.} Human preferences are a rich source of information for training language models, and have been used to train language models to produce responses that are desirable by humans. For example, \citep{christian2017deep} trained a language model to produce answers to questions that are preferred by humans, and \citep{bai2022constitutional} trained a language model using human preferences for the quality of responses. However, these methods require human feedback, which is costly and time-consuming. In this paper, instead of using human feedback, we directly optimize for the desired responses and behavior of the language model, using a simple binary cross entropy loss.

\textbf{AI Steering.} AI steering is a technique for controlling the behavior of an AI system by providing it with human feedback. The most common AI steering technique is reinforcement learning from human feedback, where a human provides a reward function that is used to train an AI system to produce responses that are rewarded by the human. However, the reward function used by reinforcement learning from human feedback is complex, involving training a separate reward model that is used to fit the reward function to the human preferences, and using reinforcement learning to find a reward maximizing policy. In this paper, in contrast, we directly optimize for a reward model that is used for AI steering, and use reinforcement learning to find the optimal policy.
