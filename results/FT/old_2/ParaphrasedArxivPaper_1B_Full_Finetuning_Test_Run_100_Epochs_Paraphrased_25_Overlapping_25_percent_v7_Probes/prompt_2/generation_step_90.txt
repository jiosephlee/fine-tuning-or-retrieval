The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from observed preferences. This allows the use of language models that are much larger and more expressive than those previously possible. We demonstrate that our method can be applied to a wide range of preference learning tasks, including those that are computationally intractable with traditional RLHF approaches. In particular, we show that our method can learn from human preferences in a single round, outperforming previous approaches that require multiple training rounds. We also demonstrate that our method can learn to align language models with human preferences in settings where the reward function is unknown, and where the reward is not aligned with the language model's output, such as in the case of sentiment analysis. Our code and experiments are available at https://github.com/CarperAI/direct-preference-optimization.

Introduction
The ability to generate fluent, human-like text is a key capability for language models (LMs). However, current LMs are trained on large corpora of text, which can lead to outputs that are too similar to the training data, resulting in a loss of diversity and a reduction in the model's expressiveness. One way to address this is to fine-tune LMs on datasets of human-generated text, which can help to preserve diversity while still leveraging the benefits of large-scale training. However, this approach is limited by the availability of high-quality human-generated data, which is often scarce for certain tasks. In this work, we introduce a method for learning from human preferences to train language models without relying on human-generated data.
Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is based on the observation that language models can be viewed as implicit reward models. In particular, given a sequence of outputs y = f (x; θ), the reward function r(y; x, θ) associated with the model x and parameter θ is defined as the probability of observing the correct output y, conditioned on the input x and the model parameters: Here, the KL-divergence is used to measure how much the model diverges from the true ground-truth output. In the context of language models, the true ground-truth outputs are the human-generated texts, and the KL-divergence can be interpreted as the human preference for the model's output over the reference text. By maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences, we can train a language model that aligns with human preferences. This aligns with the general principle of reinforcement learning, where the goal is to maximize the expected reward, subject to a constraint on the maximum allowable reward. In contrast to previous approaches that optimize the reward function directly, DPO uses a different objective that is easier to optimize, and then applies a simple transformation to obtain a reward function that matches the original objective.
The main contribution of this work is Direct Preference Optimization (D PO), a method for learning from preferences that does not require explicit specification of the reward function. DPO is based on a generalization of the RLHF algorithm [31], which has been used to train language models with explicit rewards. In contrast to previous work, DPO does not require the RLHF objective to be defined explicitly, but instead uses a generalization of the reward function to a function that can be optimized using standard methods. We demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a range of preference learning tasks. In particular, we demonstrate that DPO can learn from preferences in a single round of training, outperforming previous methods that require multiple training rounds.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We present a general framework for learning from preferences, which we call Direct Preferences Optimization (DPO). DPO does not require an explicit reward function, but instead uses a reward function that is derived from observed preferences. This enables the use of language models with much larger sizes and greater expressiveness than those previously possible.
• We present a method for learning from preference data using DPO, which we call PPO-DPO. PPO-DPO is a variant of DPO that uses the PPO reinforcement learning algorithm [26] to optimize the reward function defined in Eq. 1. We show that PPO-DPO can be applied to preference learning tasks that were previously intractable, such as sentiment analysis.
• We conduct extensive experiments on a range of preference learning scenarios, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and translation. Our results show that DPO can learn to align with human preferences in a single training round, outperforming existing methods that require multiple training iterations.
