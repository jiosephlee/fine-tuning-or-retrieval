After reading the paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model", I've learned a lot. Let me tell you everything I've learned. The paper introduces a new optimization paradigm called "direct preference optimization" (DPO). DPO is a generalization of reinforcement learning (RL) that allows for the optimization of arbitrary functions from rewards to policies. In contrast to RL, DPO does not require sampling from the reward function, and thus can be performed in practice on large datasets. DPO can be used to solve a wide variety of problems, including fine-tuning, code gen, and dialog management. In this post, I will focus on the use of DPO for language modeling, specifically fine-tuning language models (LMs) to generate high-quality text.

The basic idea behind DPO is simple: instead of optimizing the language model to achieve a given target distribution p(y | x), DPO directly optimizes the reward function induced by the model to maximize a given target reward r. Intuitively, the higher the reward, the better the generated text. Since the reward function is induced by the desired final distribution p(y | x) is not known, DPO uses a so-called "reward oracle" that provides access to a reward function induced by any desired distribution p(y | x). Once the reward function is known, the DPO algorithm can be used to optimize it, producing a policy that produces more rewarding text than a baseline policy that uses the same LM but is ignorant of the user's preferences.

To make this idea more concrete, let's consider the classic fine-tuning task of improving the performance of a pre-trained LM on a new, related task. Suppose we have a LM that is highly skilled at generating text on the target domain, but we would like to use it to generate text on the related but different task. A naive approach would be to simply fine-tune the LM on the target dataset, hoping to improve its performance on the target tasks. However, this approach is problematic, because the LM's reward function is not the desired distribution p(y | X), but the reward function induced by a distribution on which the LM is ignorant, namely the distribution induced by the initial, possibly poor-quality, training data. This low-quality initial training data may have been selected because it was easy to train on, but easy training does not necessarily correspond to high-quality final text; in fact, the LM may have been trained on low-quality text itself, leading to a negative feedback loop.

DPO provides a solution to this problem by allowing us to directly optimize the reward function induced by our desired distribution of final texts. Specifically, DPO uses a reward oracle that takes as input a distribution on which to optimize and a policy, and returns the reward produced by the policy when evaluated on a distribution other than the desired distribution. The DPO algorithm first samples an initial policy, which will be our starting point for the fine-tuning process. During the fine-tuning phase, we use the DPO algorithm, passing in our initial policy as well as our LM and the desired distribution of final texts (i.e., the target distribution on which we want to optimize our LM). The DPO algorithm produces a new policy, which we then update to our desired final policy using a standard reinforcement learning algorithm, such as PPO. At each step of the fine-tuning process, we sample an observation from the distribution on which we want our LM to eventually be optimized, and update our policy using the DPO algorithm, this time passing in our current, improved LM and the desired distribution on which we want a reward function. After many steps of this process, we have updated our LM to have the desired reward function, corresponding to a policy that produces high-quality text on the desired distribution.

Now that we've seen how DPO works in theory, let's look at a real-world application of the technique in the paper "Direct Preference optimization: Your language model is a secret reward". The authors use DPO to fine-tune GPT-4 on summarization, using human-written summaries as the reward function. This is a challenging fine-tuning task, because the LM is being asked to generate text that is both good summaries and high-quality on its own right, rather than just meeting a simple quality metric. Using DPO, the authors are able to produce a fine-tuned GPT-4 that produces summaries of similar quality to those produced by human summarization experts, using only 10% of the training data used by GPT-4 for its initial fine-tuning.
