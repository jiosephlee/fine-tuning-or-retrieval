The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new family of algorithms for the problem of optimizing a language model with a direct reward from human preferences, under a generalization assumption on the reward model. Our algorithms are provably consistent, in the sense that they always produce a model with a higher reward than any of the baselines. Our experiments show that our algorithms can produce models with up to 50% higher human preferences than SFT, while being up to 10x faster than PPO-based approaches. In particular, our method is able to optimize GPT-4 to have higher preferences than BERT 5.0 for the sentiment classification task, while being 7x faster than RLHF. We also compare our approach with other methods on other datasets and tasks, and find that our method consistently produces models with higher preferences than them.
2 Related Work
2.1 Language Models and Human Preferences
The idea of using language models to predict human preferences has been explored in several prior works. In [Bai2020Pronet], a language model is used to predict movie ratings, and a reinforcement learning algorithm is used to optimize the model to match human preferences. In [Chen2022LearningR], a learning framework for language models is proposed, which uses a reward model to represent human preferences, and a reinforcement learning method to train the language model. In [Li2022Using], a language model is trained to maximize the number of positive reviews for a product, and an RLHF algorithm is used to optimize it. In [Zhang2022Training], a language model is optimized to maximize the number of reviews in a positive category, while in [Han2022Learning] a language model is trained on a dataset of product reviews, and a reinforcement learning approach is used to optimize it to match human preferences. However, all these works use RLHF algorithms to optimize the language model, while in this paper we use a different approach, where the language model is directly optimized with human preferences using a reward model.
2.2 Reward Models for Human Preferences
Several reward models for human preferences have been proposed in the literature. In [Gao2022Using], a dataset of product reviews is used to train a reward model that represents human preferences for a given product. The model is then optimized using RL to match the human preferences. In [Wu2022Training], a dataset of product review pairs is used to train a model that represents human preferences as a probability distribution over the classes of products. A reinforcement learning algorithm is then used to optimize the model, using the reward function derived from the dataset. In [Chen et al.2022], a dataset of product ratings is used to train a binary reward model, where the reward for a product-pair is either 1 (human prefers the product) or -1 (human does not prefer the product). The reward model is then optimized using reinforcement learning. In [Li2023Learning], a dataset of product evaluations is used to train a multi-class reward model, where the rewards for a product-pair are the number of positive reviews plus the average rating of the positive reviews. A reinforcement learning algorithm then optimizes the reward model. In this paper, we use a different reward model, where the human preference is represented as a vector of probabilities over the classes of the item, and the reward is the sum of the absolute values of the probabilities.

3  Direct Preference Optimization

3.1 Problem Statement
The goal of the direct preference optimization (DPO) problem is to train a language model with a given prompt and a reward function that represents human preferences, under a certain generalization assumption on the rewards. Specifically, the reward function must satisfy the following two properties: (i) for all x and y, we have R(x,y) ≥ 0, and R(x,y)=0 if and only if y is preferred to x; (ii) for all x and all y, we have R(y) = R(x). The goal is to find a language model M such that R(M(x),y) ≥ 0 for all x and y.
In this paper, we use the reward function R defined as R(x,y) = |x| + ∑ y∼y^(-1)(x) where y^(-1)(x)=argmax\_y R(x,y). Intuitively, the reward function R adds a small absolute value to y, while penalizing x by the same amount. We assume that the reward function R is known, but the model M to be trained is kept secret. Our goal is to find M such that R(M,y) is as large as possible for all y.
