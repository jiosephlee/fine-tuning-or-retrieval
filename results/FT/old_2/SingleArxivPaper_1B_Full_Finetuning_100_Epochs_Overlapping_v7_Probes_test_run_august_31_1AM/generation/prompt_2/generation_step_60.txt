The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new family of algorithms for the problem of optimizing a language model with a direct reward from human preferences, under a general class of loss functions. Our algorithms are simple to state, provably efficient, and perform as well or better than existing methods on a variety of benchmarks. Our experiments show that our algorithms can be used to train models with human preferences in under an hour, while still achieving alignment with high quality human preferences. Our code is available at https://github.com/AlgoHunt/DPO.
We evaluate the performance of our algorithms on a variety of datasets and tasks. In particular, we evaluate the performance of our methods on fine-tuning language models for downstream NLP tasks, such as summarization and question answering. We also evaluate the performance of our method on training models with human preferences, using a crowdsourcing platform to collect human preferences for use with language models. Finally, we evaluate the performance and usability of our codebase.
Our codebase is available at https:// github.com/AlgoHun/ DPO/tree/main/code.

Preliminaries
We review the basic concepts of machine learning and natural language processing that are relevant to our work. For a more comprehensive introduction to these topics, the reader is referred to the surveys by Mitchell (2017) and Schütze (2019).
Machine Learning. Machine learning is concerned with building algorithms that can learn from data. At a high level, machine learning algorithms can be divided into two categories: supervised and unsupervised. In a supervised learning setting, the algorithm is provided with labeled training data {(x i , y i )} n i=1 , where x i is a d-dimensional feature vector and y i is a label indicating the correct class or category for the input x i . The goal of the algorithm is to learn a function f such that, given an input x, the function f can correctly classify the label y = f (x). Classifications are often measured by a probability distribution P y f (x) = ∑ y i P i (x), where P i (x) is the hypothesis class to which x is assigned. In other words, P y f (x)(i) is the probability that the function f assigns to the label y =f (x). The distribution P y f (·) is known as the loss function. A machine learning algorithm is said to optimize the loss function L with the goal of minimizing L(f; x, y) = ∑ n i=1 y i L(f; x i , y) where L; x i , y is the loss function evaluated with input x i and label y. There are many different classes of loss functions, including linear regression, logistic regression, support vector machines, and neural networks. Natural Language Processing. Natural language processing (NLP) is the field that studies the interaction between computers and human languages. NLP algorithms can be broadly divided into two categories: rule-based and statistical. Rule-based algorithms are built upon a set of explicit rules that describe how a computer should process a given input sentence. These algorithms are often easy to understand, but are limited by the availability of rules. In contrast, statistical algorithms learn from large datasets of sentence pairs (called text-sentences pairs or simply textpairs). Given a textpair, the goal of the algorithm is then to learn a model that is able to predict the label of the textpair given its constituent sentence. Statistical algorithms are typically more flexible than rule-based algorithms, but they also require the construction of large training datasets, which can be difficult to collect. In this paper, we focus on a particular class of statistical algorithms known as machine learning language models (LMs).
Machine Learning Language Models. A machine learning language model is a probabilistic model that maps text to probability distributions over text. More formally, a ML LM is a function M : Σ → P(Σ), where Σ is the input vocabulary. The model M is given an input word s ∈ Σ and produces a distribution over words in Σ, i.e., M(s) ∈ P(Σ).
