\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be optimized exactly with the simple binary cross-entropy loss, greatly simplifying the RLHF pipeline. We introduce a new parameterization for the reward model that enables us to extract the corresponding optimal policy in a closed form, allowing us solve the standard RLHF optimization problem with only a simple binary cross-entropyloss. Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is simple, stable, and computationally lightweight. DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with human preferences, without requiring any sampling from the LM policy or significant hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments show that DPO outperforms existing methods for fine-tuning LMs with human preferences in terms of both the quality of the generated responses and the ability to control the model's behavior. In particular, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF methods in terms of the quality of the generated response, and matches or improves the quality of the response in summarization and single turn dialogue tasks, while being substantially simpler to train and implement.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\textbf{Language Models.} Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years, with the advent of large-scale pre-training datasets such as BERT~\citep{devlin2018bert}, GPT-3~\citep{brown2022gpt3}, and GPT-4~\citep{touvron2023llama}. These models have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of downstream tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and summarization. However, these models are typically trained on data generated by human annotators, and thus are not directly comparable to the language models that are the focus of this paper. Nevertheless, the ability of these models to learn from unlabeled data is a key factor in their success, and the ability of these models is also a key factor in the success of the methods presented in this paper.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning.} Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful technique for learning to perform tasks in an environment, where the agent interacts with the environment and receives rewards for performing well. The most common RL framework is the Markov Decision Process (MDP), which defines the environment as a Markov chain, and the agent as a policy that maps states to actions. The goal of the RL algorithm is to find a policy that will maximize the expected cumulative reward over the long term. The most common RL algorithms are based on the policy gradient method, which uses the policy gradient to update the policy in the direction that maximizes the expected reward. The most common policy gradient algorithm is the policy gradient method, also known as the policy improvement method. The policy gradient method is based on the idea that the policy gradient is the gradient of the expected reward with respect to the policy. The policy gradient method can be used to find a policy that is close to the optimal policy, but it is not guaranteed to find the optimal policy. The policy improvement method is a modification of the policy gradient method that uses a reward function to find a policy that improves the expected reward. The policy improvement method can be used to solve the standard RL problem, but it is not always guaranteed to find the optimal solution.

\textbf{Human Preferences.} Human preferences are a powerful tool for guiding the development of AI systems. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in using human preferences to guide the development of AI systems, with the goal of producing systems that are safe, effective, and trustworthy. One approach to using human preferences is to train a reward model that reflects human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning to fine-tune the AI system to maximize the reward. This approach has been shown to be effective in a variety of domains, including language modeling, natural language understanding, and machine translation. However, the process of training a reward model can be challenging, and it is often necessary to collect a large number of human preferences in order to obtain a sufficiently accurate reward model. In this paper, we propose a new approach to using human preferences to guide AI system development, which does not require the collection of large numbers of human preferences. Instead, we propose a method for directly optimizing the policy that maximizes the reward, without the need for any human preferences. This approach is based on the observation that the reward model can be represented as a function of the policy, and that the optimal policy can be found by minimizing the difference between the reward and the reward model. We show that this approach can be used to directly optimize the policy that maximizes a reward function, without the need for human preferences. This approach has several advantages over traditional methods for using human preferences to guide system development.
