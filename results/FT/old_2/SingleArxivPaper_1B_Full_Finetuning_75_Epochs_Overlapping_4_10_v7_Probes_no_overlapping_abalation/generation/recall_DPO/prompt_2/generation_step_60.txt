The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a general framework for training language models with human preferences, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO uses a reward function that is a natural generalization of traditional metrics such as BLEU and METEOR, and does not require any explicit human annotations of preference pairs. Instead, DPO optimizes a loss function that naturally incorporates the reward function and leverages the power of modern optimization algorithms, such as Newton's method and gradient descent, to find high-quality model parameters in polynomial time. Experiments on four preference datasets show that DPO can align language models with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods, using much simpler reward functions and without any fine-tuning of the language model on the preference data.

In Section 4, we present experimental results and analyze the performance of different components of the framework. Finally, in Section 5, we draw conclusions.

Related Work
In this section, we review existing work on aligning language models with preferences, focusing on metrics that are closely related to human preferences, such as BLEU and ROUGE, as well as more recent methods that use reward functions based on human preferences, such as PPL and PPL-LL.

Metrics for Aligning Language Models
Language models are typically evaluated using metrics that measure their ability to generate text that is similar to reference text in some way. Two of the most commonly used metrics are BLEU and ROUGE. BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) measures the similarity of two texts using a statistical model based on a large corpus of aligned human-written paraphrased pairs. ROUGE (Lin and Schwenk, 2004) is a family of algorithms that optimize a variant of the BLEU metric, taking into account multiple versions of the reference and candidate texts. While these metrics provide a way to evaluate language models' ability to generate text that humans find similar to the reference, they do not directly measure the quality of the generated text, and must be calibrated with external measures of human preference, such as NDCG (Liu et al., 2015).
Other metrics for aligning language models are based on human evaluations of the quality of generated text. PPL (Chen et al., 2017) is a metric that uses a preference dataset of human-written summaries paired with their preferred and non-preferred generated summaries. Like BLEU and ROUGE measures, PPL is a metric of similarity, but it uses a human preference dataset to calibrate the similarity measure with human preferences. PPL-LL (Chen et al. (2018)) extends PPL by using a dataset of human-written longform summaries paired with preferred and non-preferred candidate longform summaries. However, PPL-LL requires human-written longform summaries as well as preferred and non-preferred shortform summaries, making it more difficult to use than PPL. Another related metric is PPL-LLW, which uses a dataset of human-written Wikipedia articles paired with preferred and nonpreferred candidate articles (Wu et al., 2018). Like PPL-LL, PPL-LLW uses a dataset of longform summaries and articles, but PPL-LLW does not require human-written longform summaries, making it easier to use than PPL-LL.

Training Language Models from Preferences
While existing metrics for evaluating language models can be used to calibrate BLEU and ROUGE scores with human preferences, no existing method for aligning language model with human preferences uses a metric that is directly optimized during training. In this section, we review methods for training language models from human preferences, focusing on methods that use reward functions derived from existing metrics for aligning language with human preferences. We begin with methods that use a single preference dataset, and then describe methods that use multiple preference datasets.

Single-Dataset Methods
The earliest method for training language models from a single preference dataset is PPL-LL (Cleary et al., 2019). PPL-LL uses a neural network to map a language model PLM to a preference score PLM, where the preference dataset contains pairs of preferred and non-preferred completions for a given prompt P. The authors of PPL-LL propose a loss function that encourages the language model to produce completions with high preference scores, but with low KL-divergence from the reference completion, where KL is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. This loss function is a generalization of the PPL loss function (Chen et al, 2017) to multiple completions per prompt, but does not use any of the information in the PPL dataset.
