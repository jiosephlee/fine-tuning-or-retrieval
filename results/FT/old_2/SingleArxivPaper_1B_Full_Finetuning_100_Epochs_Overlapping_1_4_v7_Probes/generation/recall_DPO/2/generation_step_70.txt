The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a principled framework for optimizing language models with human preferences, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO uses a preference oracle to query humans on the relative preference between candidate completions to a given prompt, allowing it to efficiently optimize a reward function that represents human preferences in a mathematically precise way. DPO enjoys several advantages over existing methods. First, DPO does not require access to a large training dataset of human preferences, making it applicable to large-scale language model deployments. Second, DPO is self-contained and does not rely on third-party datasets or libraries, making it easy to integrate into existing AI systems. Third, DPO is scalable; the authors show how to parallelize the preference queries and optimization, allowing DPO to optimize very large reward functions using multiple processors or GPUs. Finally, DPO's optimization objective is different from traditional reinforcement learning algorithms, allowing for simpler implementation and better performance in practice.
In this paper, we propose a new framework for training language models with human preferences called Direct Preference Optimization (or DPO for short). Our key insight is that the preference relation between completions can be represented as a reward function, and that the standard reinforcement learning objective can be used to optimize a reward function to match the preferences of the target audience. We formalize this as an optimization problem that we solve using an algorithm inspired by gradient descent, but that avoids explicit sampling of completions and does not require an accurate estimate of the reward. In addition to being computationally simpler than traditional RL algorithms, our approach can handle very large reward functions, making it well-suited for large-scale language model training.
Our experiments show that DPO can optimize reward functions representing human preferences with varying quality, including preference data collected from Amazon's Mechanical Turk. We also perform a comprehensive evaluation of different training methods for large-scale language models, finding that DPO is the only method that improves over baselines when evaluating models on tasks such as summarization and question answering. Finally, we conduct a human study showing that after training with DPO, participants are better able to distinguish model completions from the opinions of a fictional expert, a task that has been previously shown to be sensitive to the bias of LLMs.
Our paper makes the following contributions: • We propose Direct Preference Optimization, a simple framework for training language models from preferences collected by humans. • We present a principled approach for optimizing reward functions with human preferences using an algorithm inspired by reinforcement learning, and show how to efficiently parallelize the algorithm for large-scale training. • We evaluate several training methods for large-scale LLMs, showing that DPO is the sole method that improves over basements on evaluation metrics such as summarization and QA performance.
• We perform a human study showing that participants are better able to discern model completions from the expert opinion of a fictional scientist after training with DPO than with other methods.

Preliminaries
In this section, we introduce the basic concepts of machine learning with human preferences, review existing methods, and set the stage for our main contribution: Direct Preference Optimization.

Human preferences
Machine learning with human preferences is a branch of machine learning that uses crowdsourcing to collect human preferences from a large population of subjects, and then uses these preferences to improve the performance of AI systems. In the DPO framework, the AI system is a language model, and the preferences are expressed as a ranking of completions to a given query. The goal of DPO is to use the preferences to optimize a reward function that measures the discrepancy between the preferences and the model's behavior. This reward function is then used to train a model that produces completions with the desired behavior, as described in the next section.
There are many ways to collect human preferences. In this paper, we focus on datasets of human preferences collected via Amazon's Mechanical Turk (MTurk). MTurk is a crowdsourcing platform that allows humans to complete tasks online, and has been used to collect human preferences in a variety of settings, including preference elicitation for machine learning algorithms (Kupcsik et al., 2013;Bai et al., 2015;Bai and Klein, 2016;Chen et al., 2017;Chen and Klein, 2018), quality assessment of software (Huang et al., 2018;Tian et al., 2019), and preference modeling for machine learning (Gupta et al., 2016;Gupta and Klein, 2017;Gupta and Stiennon, 2018). In the context of language, Turkers rate the plausibility of completions to a short text, such as a summary or passage from a text-based game. While some studies have used completions generated by automated algorithms (Bai et al., , 2015, most studies in the MTurk community use completions generated by humans on a salary.
