The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a principled framework for training language models to satisfy human preferences, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO uses a loss function that is both convex and efficiently computable, is well-posed, and enables fine-grained control over the tradeoff between model performance and user satisfaction. DPO models outperform existing methods, including baselines that directly optimize for performance on downstream tasks, on a large set of preference datasets. The code for DPO and all of the preference datasets used in this evaluation is made publicly available.

Not Invented Here
The field of NLU has seen significant progress in recent years with the advent of large-scale pretrained language models (PLMs). However, while PLMs can perform very well on certain tasks, their performance on new, untrained prompts is often lackluster, and their performance on downstream tasks such as sentiment analysis or fact-checking can be fragile to changes in the prompt. This fragility is often attributed to a phenomenon known as "prompting surface effects," which refers to the fact that the performance of a model on a given prompt can vary significantly depending on the specific text that appears in the prompt (e.g., a model trained on news articles may perform better on historical news articles than on articles written today). In response to this challenge, researchers have turned to fine-tuning large PLMs on specialized datasets of human-written prompts, in an effort to "untrain" the effects of surface factors such as date or source. A popular approach to this problem is BERT Fine-Tuning (Xiong et al., 2019), which fine-tunes BERT (Prenger et al., 2015) on the Text Classification Challenge (TCC) datasets (Sanh et al., 2017). In BERT Fine-Tuning, a PLM is first fine-tuned on a set of pre-training datasets to obtain an initial "base" representation; this base representation is then fine-tuned on a subset of the TCC datasets to produce a model with improved performance on the TCC tasks. After completion of the BERT Fine-Tuning pipeline, the resulting model is often referred to as a "bert-base-X" model, where "X" denotes the name of the TCC dataset on which the model was trained (e.g., "bert-base-Xtcc").
Despite the success of the BERT Fine-tuning pipeline, there are several important limitations to the approach. Most notably, the selection of which prompts to fine-tune a given PLM with is a subjective and often arbitrary decision. In addition, the TCC datasets used in the BERT Fine-t tuning pipeline are themselves selected based on their perceived quality, which can lead to the creation of a self-perpetuating set of datasets that are representative of human preferences rather than objective qualities of the underlying prompts. Finally, the TCC datasets are limited to a small set of closely-related classification tasks, and it is not always clear how well the BERT Fine-tuned models would generalize to tasks outside of the TCC domain.
In this paper, we take a step toward addressing these limitations by presenting a principled framework for using supervised learning to directly optimize for human preferences for language generation. Our framework, which we call "Direct Preference Optimization" (DPO), leverages a reward model that measures the human preference satisfaction of a given language model representation, and an optimization objective that drives the representation towards the values of the reward model. Unlike previous approaches to training language models with human preferences, DPO does not rely on heuristics or hand-engineered features; rather, it uses a general framework for training supervised models to predict reward values from a latent variable model, with the reward value as the ground-truth reward function. We evaluate DPO on two different preference datasets, one of which is constructed from reviews of products and services, and the other of which is constructed from user-generated posts on social media. On both datasets, DPO outperforms existing methods, including basals that directly optimize for performance in downstream tasks such as sentiment classification and fact-checking.
