\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

1. Introduction
Large unsupervised language models of increasing size learn broad world knowledge and to a certain extent reasoning skills (Brown et al., 2018; Pinto et al., 2019; Sutskever et al., 2014a; Weston et al., 2011; Yarowsky, 2005). However, their training objectives are completely unsupervised, and therefore the models themselves lack any notion of, or alignment with, human preferences. In this paper we will be particularly interested in human steerability of LM behavior, i.e., our ability to guide the LM's training to achieve our desired goals.

2. Related Work
Gaining steerability is crucial for applying LMs to real-world problems, such as text generation, where the objective of the training algorithm does not directly correspond to the decision making task at hand. Existing methods generally seek to make the most of the LM's training while still providing some level of control. One approach is to use off-policy reinforcement learning (RL) to adjust the LM's generation according to the results of human labelers (Fang et al., 2017;Korbak et al., 201 huggingface.co/litreslam). This method successfully uses the power of the large, unsupervised LM to improve response quality in summarization (Korbak et al, 2018) and single-turn dialogue (Bai et al., 201-2018;Liu et al., 201 eighthlan2018). However, the RL training procedure is complex, involves sampling from the LM's distribution, and requires access to human labels for each generation, making it impractical for very large LMs such as GPT-4.

3. The DPO Algorithm
In this paper we introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a simple alternative to RLHF that enables steerability of LM training without sampling from the LM or performing significant hyperparameter optimization. Our main idea is to express the reward for the LM in terms of its policy, and then use an extension of standard Pontryagin's maximum principle to derive an optimization algorithm with a closed form reward function that can be optimized exactly with a standard classification loss. The resulting DPO algorithm is straightforward to implement and easily scaled to large models, and preliminary results are promising. However, a key challenge in applying DPO is expressing the reward for the desired LM behavior (i.e., human preference) in terms of a reward function for the existing, trained LM policy. We find that the extension of the reward function based on Pontryagin's max principle is only valid for a single sample, making it difficult to extract the reward function for the optimal policy in general.

4. What is New in This Paper?
Our main contribution is the development of the DPO algorithm, an easy to implement method for optimizing LMs with human preferences that is stable, performant and computationally lightweight. In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require sampling from the LM or fine-tuning with reinforcement learning, and therefore is more robust to poor hyperparameter choices in the RLHF stage. We also present experimental results showing that the DPO algorithm is at least as effective as existing methods for fine-tuning LMs with human feedback, and in some cases improves upon them.

5. What is Next?
In this paper we have introduced the DPO algorithm for optimizing language models with human preferences. In future work we plan to extend the DPO framework to other NLP tasks, as well as investigating the use of DPO in a multi-actor setting, where multiple people are optimizing the same LM with human feedback.
