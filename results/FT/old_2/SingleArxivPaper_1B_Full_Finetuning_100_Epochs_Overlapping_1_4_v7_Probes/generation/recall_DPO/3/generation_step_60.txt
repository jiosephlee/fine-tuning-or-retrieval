\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

1. Introduction
Large unsupervised language models of increasing size and capacity learn broad world knowledge and to a lesser extent reasoning skills (Brown et al., 2018; Pomerleau et al., 2019; McLeish et al., 2020; Devlin et al., 201 lay out the problem and present some initial results. While their models are extremely capable, they are completely unsupervised, trained only on a very large body of text including Reddit posts, web pages, and the WebKB knowledge base (Brown et al., , 2018. While their models can do all sorts of impressive things, they are not particularly controllable, that is, it is not obvious how to ask them to perform specific tasks or adopt specific attitudes. In this paper, we will call such a controllable language model highly-conv controlled, since we will generally be interested in using highly-conv models for low-conv scenarios such as automatic review rating, rather than simply commanding a large model to perform a specific task. We will also generally assume that the user has some level of familiarity with the subject matter, so we will not be interested in models that attempt to give overly-specific or incorrect answers to questions.

2. Related Work
Controlling the behavior of unsupervised language models has been a major research focus in the last few years (Bai et al., 2017;Karras et al., 2016;Wu et al., 201 16;Sutskever et al., 2014;Shen et al., 2013;Vaswani et al., 2012;Xu et al., 2022), but most approaches fall into one of a few main categories. The most straightforward approach is to fine-tune a pre-trained language model with a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm that takes the user as a reward function, estimating the human preference for each generation of the model. This approach is straightforward to implement but suffers from a number of problems. First, RL training is an expensive supervised learning procedure, typically requiring training a separate model for every reward function to be optimized. Even if we assume access to an unlimited supply of computing power, this approach becomes impractical for very large unsupervised models due to the sheer number of reward functions that must be trained. Second, because the reward model is learned directly from human preferences, the resulting control policy may be inconsistent with the original unsupervised model, as revealed by the human annotators (Bai et al, 2017;Kazemi and Recht, 2018;Kazemi et al., 2021;Shen et al, 2019). Finally, the RL algorithms themselves are often complex, involve solving high-dimensional reinforcement learning problems, and require careful consideration of issues such as discounting and state exploration (Bai et al , 2017;Kassim et al., 201 h9;Kazemi andRecht, 2018). We will refer to this class of methods as fine-tuning with reinforcement learning from human preference (RLHF). In this paper, we focus on the most advanced member of this family, which uses a model-agnostic optimization framework based on proximal policy optimization (PPO) (Schulman, 2018) to maximize a reward estimate derived from human feedback rather than explicitly constructing a reward model. This approach is able to produce highly-controllable models with relatively little supervision, but remains highly complex, involving both RL and optimization within an RL framework, and requiring careful hyperparameter selection to avoid getting stuck in local optima.

3. Our Contributions
In this paper, we make the following major contributions: • We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a single-ality optimization algorithm for fine-tuning language models with human preferences, that is stable, efficient, and easy to implement. Unlike existing methods, DPO does not fit a separate reward model; rather, it optimizes a simple objective that directly produces a control policy consistent with the human preferences.
• We theoretically analyze the performance of DPO under various assumptions about the reward model, showing that DPO produces control policies that optimize the desired human preference, within a certain class of objective functions.
Our experiments show that, compared with existing methods, DPO is at least as effective at fine-tuning for sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue while requiring neither hyperparameter tuning nor extensive RLHF iterations.
