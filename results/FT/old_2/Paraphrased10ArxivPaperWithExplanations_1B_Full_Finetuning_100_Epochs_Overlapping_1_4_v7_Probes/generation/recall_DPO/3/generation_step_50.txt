\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LMs}) trained on vast quantities of text have achieved state-of-the-art performance on a wide range of tasks, including translation, summarization, question answering, and dialogue (see, e.g., \cite{radford2019language}). However, these models are trained without explicit human supervision, relying solely on the self-supervised learning objective of maximizing the likelihood of the training data. This makes them difficult to control, as there is no clear objective function to optimize.

One approach to gain some control over the behavior of LMs is to collect human preferences for model generations and fine-tuning the model to align with these preferences. This is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback (\textit{RLHF}), where the model is trained to maximize a reward function that reflects the human preferences. The reward function is typically estimated by fitting a reward model to a dataset of human preferences, and then using reinforcement learning to fine-tune the model to maximize this estimated reward while avoiding divergence from the original model.

A common method for estimating the reward function is reinforcement learning with policy gradient (RLPG; \cite{ziegler2017reinforcement}), which fits a policy to the preferences and then optimizes the policy to maximize the estimated reward. However, RLPG is a complex and often computationally intensive procedure, as it involves sampling from the policy multiple times to estimate the gradient of the reward with respect to the parameters of the policy. Additionally, the reward model used in RLPG is often a neural network, which can be computationally expensive to train.

In this paper, we propose a new method for RLHF that avoids the need for sampling from or training a reward model. Our method, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or DPO, directly optimizes the policy parameters to maximize the reward without fitting a separate reward model. This is done by first defining a loss that measures the difference between the policy generated by the original model and the policy generated by a modified policy that is optimized to maximize the reward. We then show that this loss can be expressed as a quadratic function of the policy parameters, which allows us to solve the optimization problem exactly. This leads to a straightforward optimization algorithm that does not involve sampling from the policy or training a separate reward model.

Our experiments show that D PO can fine-tune LLMs to align with human preferences in a manner comparable to existing methods, but with fewer hyperparameters and computational overhead. In particular, we demonstrate that DPO can fine tune LLMs to generate more positive sentiment in summarization, more fluent responses in single-turn dialogue, and more coherent and relevant responses in summarization, question answering and summarization. Furthermore, we show that DPO can improve the quality of responses in summarization and single turn dialogue compared to PPO-based RLHF, without requiring any additional hyperparameters or fine-tuning of the RLHF policy.

\section{Related Work}

Large unsupervised LMs have revolutionized natural language processing (NLP), but their behavior is difficult to control due to the lack of explicit human supervision. Several approaches have been proposed to gain some control over LMs, including reinforcement learning from human feedback, fine-tuning with human annotations, and using language models as proxies for human preferences.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (R2HF; \cite{zieglg2017reinforcement}) is a popular method for training language models to meet human preferences. In R2HF, the model is first trained on a large dataset of human preferences and then fine-tuned to maximize a reward function derived from the preferences. This reward function is typically estimated using reinforcement learning with policy gradient, where the policy is first trained on the preferences and then optimized to maximize the estimated reward while avoiding divergence. However, this approach is computationally intensive, as it involves training a separate reward model and optimizing the policy to maximize the reward.

Another approach to gain control over language models is to use language models as proxies for user preferences. This is commonly done by fine-tuning language models with human preferences collected through crowdsourcing or other methods. This method is often referred to as \textit{human-in-the-loop} or \textit{human-aided} training, and has been shown to improve the quality of language models compared to purely self-supervised training (e.g., \cite{mishra-etal-2018-hummingbird, bai2020learning, bai2022training}). However, this approach is limited by the availability of human preferences, which may not reflect the true preferences of the target user population.
