\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUNMs}) trained on vast quantities of text have demonstrated impressive capabilities, ranging from fluent human-level dialogue to the ability to answer questions about complex concepts (see, e.g., \cite{gpt3, bai2022language}). However, these models are trained without explicit human supervision, which makes them difficult to control and use in practical scenarios. In this work, we explore how to leverage human preferences to fine-tune LUNMs to align with desired behaviors, a process known as \textit{direct preference optimization} (DPO). Our primary contribution is a method for optimizing an LLM using only a binary preference dataset, without relying on reinforcement learning or sampling from the model.

Traditionally, DPO has been applied to language models fine-tuned on summarization datasets, where the goal is to produce high-quality summaries that meet user preferences. In this setting, the preference dataset consists of human-written summaries paired with a reference summary, and the objective is to optimize the language model to generate the preferred summary. This approach has been shown to yield improvements over baselines in several summarization tasks, including automatic metrics like ROUGE and human judgments by crowdsourcing platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) (see \cite{cho2022training} for a review).

In this paper, we extend DPO to a broader class of preference datasets, including those generated by unsupervised language models themselves. Our primary motivation is to enable the fine-tuning of LLMs with unsupervised training data, which is crucial for applications such as conversational AI and content moderation. We also consider preference datasets generated by other models, such as GPT-4 (see Section~\ref{sec:related_work} for more details).

Our primary contribution is a method to optimize an LLM using only binary preference labels, without relying on reinforcement Learning or sampling from the model. Our method, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or DPO, is based on a simple yet effective reparameterization of the reward model used in traditional DPO algorithms. This reparameterization allows us to express the optimal policy for the reward model in closed form, enabling us to train the reward model using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss and a simple classification loss. Our experiments show that DPO yields performance comparable to or better than existing methods for fine-tuning LLMs with unsuited datasets, including those generated directly by the LLM itself. Moreover, DPO is computationally efficient and easy to implement, making it a promising alternative to reinforcement learning-based approaches for fine-tuning language models with unsupervised training.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have achieved remarkable results in various tasks, including translation, summarization, question answering, and dialogue. However, these models are typically trained using large amounts of unlabeled text, making them difficult to control and fine-tune for specific tasks. One approach to fine-tuning language models is to collect human preferences for the generated outputs, and then train the language model to satisfy these preferences using reinforcement learning (RL). This process, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has been successfully applied to various language tasks, including summarization (see \cite{ziegler2022learning} for a review).

However, RLHF is an iterative procedure that involves training a reward model to reflect human preferences, followed by fine-tuning the language model using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward. This process can be computationally expensive, especially for large models, and it is also sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the number of episodes and the discount factor. In this paper, we propose a method that directly optimizes the language model to satisfy human preferences without relying on reinforcement learning.

Another related line of work focuses on leveraging human preferences to improve the quality of language models. For example, \cite{cho2020training} proposes a method to fine-tune language models based on human preferences collected by a crowdsourcing platform. This approach uses a neural network to map human preferences to model parameters, and then trains the model using a supervised learning objective. Similarly, \cite{wang2022learning} fine-tunes language models by maximizing the likelihood of human preferences, but their method does not involve reinforcement learning. These methods can be viewed as special cases of our proposed DPO framework, where the reward model is fixed and the optimization objective is a simple binary cross-entropy loss.

Finally, it is worth noting that the concept of using human preferences to optimize language models is not new. For example, \citet{zhang2022human} propose a method to fine-tuning language model based on human preferences, but their approach is limited to binary preferences and does not generalize to multi-label preferences. Our work extends this line of research by proposing a general framework for optimizing language models using only binary preferences.
