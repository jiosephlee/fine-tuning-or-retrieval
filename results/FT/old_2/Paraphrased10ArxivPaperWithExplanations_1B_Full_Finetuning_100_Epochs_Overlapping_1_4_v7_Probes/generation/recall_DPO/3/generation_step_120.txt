\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUNMs}) trained on vast quantities of text have demonstrated impressive capabilities, ranging from fluent human-level dialogue to the ability to answer questions about complex concepts (see, e.g., \cite{chowdhery2022palm, stiennon2022training}). However, these models are trained solely on unlabeled text, without explicit human supervision on the desired behaviors or responses. This makes it challenging to fine-tune LUNMs to align with human preference data, as there is no ground truth reference to compare against. Moreover, the sheer size and complexity of LUNMs makes it difficult to manually label responses with high-quality, and the limited availability of such human preference data poses further challenges.

In this work, we propose a method for fine-tuning LUNMs to align more closely with human preferences, without the need for reinforcement learning (RL) or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our method, which we refer to as \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO) and which builds on previous work by \citeauthor{ziegler2022learning} and \citeauthor{ziegliger2023fine}, uses a simple supervised learning objective to directly optimize the parameters of the LUNM to match human preferences. This objective is derived from a reward model learned from preference data, but differs in several key ways. First, we use a different reward model than is typically employed in RLHF, which allows us to avoid the complex and computationally expensive RL training procedure. Second, we represent the reward model as a parameterized function of the LM's parameters, rather than as a fixed function of the parameters alone. This representation enables us to optimize the reward model parameters using a standard supervised learning objective, rather than relying on an RL algorithm. This change in representation also allows us to leverage the powerful tools from the theory of optimal policies, which we use to derive a closed-form expression for the optimal policy that matches the reward model. This policy can be optimized using a standard maximum likelihood objective, without the need for sampling from or estimating the reward function. We refer to this objective as the \textit{direct preference optimization} objective.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
\item We propose a new method for fine-tuning large unsupervised language models to align with human preferences, without reinforcement learning or extensive hyperparameter tuning.
\item Our method, \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO, pronounced ``doe-poy''), is based on a reward model learned from human preferences, but avoids the complex and computationally intensive RL training procedure used in previous works.
\item Our method can be implemented using a straightforward supervised learning objective, without the need to estimate the reward function or perform sampling from the LM during training.
\item Experiments show that DPO can align LUNMs with human preferences as well or better than existing methods, while being simpler to implement and train. In particular, DPO fine-tunes LUNMs to generate responses with higher sentiment and fluency, while maintaining or improving response quality in sentiment and summarization tasks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Large unsupervised pre-trained language models (LMs), such as those based on the BERT architecture \citep{chowdhery2019sequence}, have demonstrated impressive capabilities in tasks such as machine translation \citep{radford2019language} and question answering \citep{stiennon2022training}. However, these models are typically trained on vast quantities of unlabeled text, without any explicit human supervision on the specific behaviors or responses that the model should exhibit. Consequently, it is challenging to fine-tune these models to align with human preference datasets, as there is no reference model to compare against. Prior work has attempted to address this problem by fine-tuning pre-trained LMs with reinforcement learning algorithms, which learn to generate responses that are preferred by humans \citep{ziegler2020human, zhang2022training, zhang2023training}. These methods typically involve training an RL agent to maximize a reward function that represents the human preference data, and then fine-tune the pre-trained LM using reinforcement learning to optimize this reward function. This approach has proven successful, but is computationally intensive, requires careful hyperparameter tuning, and can be unstable, especially when the reward function is complex \citep{ziegliger2022fine, zhang2023learning}.
