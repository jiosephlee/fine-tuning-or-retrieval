\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUNMs}) trained on vast quantities of text have demonstrated impressive capabilities, including fluent generation, question answering, and translation (see, e.g., \cite{gpt3, bai2022language, ranselmann2023training}). However, these models are trained without explicit human supervision, which makes them difficult to control and use effectively. In this work, we explore a method for fine-tuning LUNMs to align with human preference data, a process known as \textit{direct preference optimization} (DPO). Our approach, which we refer to as \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO) or \textit{DPO} for short, is a straightforward extension of reinforcement learning from human feedback (\textit{RLHF}) that does not require any sampling from the LUNM during training.

RLHF is a popular method for fine-tuning large language models to align with human preferences. In this approach, a human annotator is presented with multiple model-generated outputs for each prompt, and asked to select the output they prefer. The model is then fine-tuned to align with these preferences by maximizing the expected reward from the human, under a reward model that captures the annotator’s preferences. This reward model is typically learned using reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the model is first trained on a large dataset of human preferences and model generations, and then fine-tuned to maximize the estimated reward function. While RLHF has been shown to be effective at aligning language models with human preferences, it is a complex and often challenging procedure. First, the reward model must be learned from the preference data, which is often a large-scale unsupervised learning problem. Second, the reward model must then be used to optimize the language model, which involves solving a non-convex optimization problem. Finally, the reward model must also be updated to reflect changes in the preferences of the human annotators, which can be a time-consuming process. In this paper, we present a method for directly optimizing the language model to align with human preferences without any sampling from the model or fine-tuning of the reward model.

Our main contribution is a simple and effective method for training language models to align with preferences, called \textit{Direct Prefer- ence Optimization} (DPO). DPO is an extension of reinforcement learning from preference data that does not require any fine-tuning of the language model’s reward function or sampling from the model during training. Instead, DPO uses a simple binary cross-entropy loss to optimize the language model to maximize the probability of the preferred outputs while minimizing the probability of the dispreferred outputs. This loss is equivalent to the maximum likelihood objective used in standard reinforcement learning from preference data, but is easier to optimize because it does not involve any parameters in the language model. We demonstrate that DPO can be used to fine-tune LUNMs to align well with human preferences, outperforming existing methods and matching or improving performance on several tasks. Our experiments show that DPO is stable, efficient, and computationally lightweight compared to existing methods, and can be applied to a wide range of LLM tasks.

\section{Related Work}

Large unsupervised models of human language have been trained on massive datasets of text, including news articles, web pages, and social media posts (see, e.g, \cite{gpt2, bai2022 language, ranselmann-2023-training}). These models have demonstrated impressive capabilities, such as fluent generation, question answering (QA), and translation (see, \textit{e.g}, \cite{gpt1, bai2022langau-1, ranselmann2019google}). However, these models were trained without explicit human supervision and are therefore difficult to use effectively. One approach to making these models more usable is to fine-tune them to align with human preferences, a process known as direct preference optimization (DPO) or reinforcement learning from human feedback. In this process, a human annotator rates multiple model-generated outputs for a given prompt, and the model is fine-tuned to maximize its expected rate of winning these human preferences. This process is similar to reinforcement learning from human feedback \cite{ziegler2017learning}, but instead of using a reward function learned from human preferences, the model is directly optimized to maximize the probability of preferred outputs while minimizing the likelihood of dispreferred outputs.
