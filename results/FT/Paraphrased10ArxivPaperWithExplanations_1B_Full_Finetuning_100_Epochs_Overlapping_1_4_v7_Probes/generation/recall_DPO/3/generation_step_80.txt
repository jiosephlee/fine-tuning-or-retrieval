\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUNMs}) trained on vast quantities of text have demonstrated impressive capabilities, ranging from fluent human-level dialogue to the ability to answer questions about complex concepts (see, e.g., \cite{gpt3, bai2022language}). However, these models are trained without explicit human supervision, which makes them difficult to control and use effectively. In this work, we explore a method for fine-tuning LUNMs to align with human preference data, a process known as \textit{direct preference optimization} (DPO). Our approach, which we refer to as \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO) in this paper, is based on a simple yet powerful idea: instead of optimizing the LUNM to match a fixed set of human preferences, we can directly optimize the LUNM to maximize its own probability of being preferred by humans. This approach is conceptually straightforward and avoids the complex and often unstable process of reinforcement learning from human feedback commonly used in previous work (see \cite{ziegler2022training} for a survey).

Formally, given a dataset of pairs of model and preference, the goal of DPO is to find the optimal policy $\pi^*$ that maximizes the likelihood of the observed data, i.e.,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
    \max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \pi(y \mid x)\bigr] - \beta \log \sum_{y}\pi(y)\tag{1}
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ is a hyperparameter controlling the strength of the reward penalty. This objective is identical to the one used in previous work that fine-tunes an unsupervised LM to match a fixed set $S$ of human preferences, but it is crucial to note that the policy $\pi$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:main} is learned from the preference data $\mathcal{D}$, rather than being fixed and then optimized. This change in perspective has several important implications.

First, the policy $\pi$ learned by DPO is the optimal policy for the reward model defined by Eq.~\ref {eq:reward_model} in the main text. This means that the reward model learned by DPO is exactly the same as the reward model used in previous work that optimized a fixed set of preferences, but without the need to fit a separate reward model. Second, the policy learned by DPO is a \textit{direct} policy, meaning that it does not rely on any explicit representation of the preferences themselves; instead, it directly optimizes the reward model to maximize the likelihood of the observed preference data. This is a significant advantage, as it allows the policy to be learned from a smaller preference dataset than would be required by methods that fit a separate reward model.

Finally, the policy learned by the DPO objective is a \textit{(strongly) preferred} policy, meaning that the policy $\pi^*$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:main}, which is the optimal policy for a reward model defined by Eq~\ref{eq:simple_reward_model}, is the policy that humans would choose if they could rank models according to their preferences. This property is crucial for applications where the goal is to generate high-quality responses, as it ensures that the policy generated by DPO will be preferred by humans.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have been trained on massive datasets of text and achieved impressive performance on a variety of tasks, including translation, summarization, question answering, and dialogue. However, these models are typically trained using unsupervised loss functions, which makes them difficult or impossible to control and use effectively for tasks such as generating high-quality responses. Various approaches have been proposed to fine-tune these models to produce more controlled outputs, including methods that use reinforcement learning from human feedback to train the model to generate responses that are preferred by humans \citep{ziegler2020reinforcement, stiennon2022learning, bai2022learning, liu2023training}. These methods generally involve training a separate reward model to predict the preference of the generated responses, and then using reinforcement learning to optimize the unsupervised LM policy to maximize this reward. While these methods can produce high-quality responses, they are complex to implement and often unstable, requiring careful hyperparameter tuning and fine-grained control over the reward model.
