\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUNMs}) trained on vast quantities of text have demonstrated impressive capabilities, ranging from fluent human-level dialogue to the ability to answer questions about complex concepts (see, e.g., \cite{chowdhery2022palm, stiennon2022training}). However, these models are trained solely on unlabeled text, without explicit human supervision on the desired behaviors or responses. This makes it challenging to fine-tune LUNMs to align with human preference data, as there is no ground truth reference to compare against. Moreover, the sheer size and complexity of LUNMs makes it difficult to manually label responses with high-quality, and the distribution of human preferences is unknown.

To address these challenges, researchers have developed methods to fine-tune LLMs to align with human preferences using reinforcement learning from human feedback (\textit{RLHF}). In RLHF, a human annotator provides feedback on the quality of candidate model responses, and the model is trained to maximize the human reward while avoiding a predefined policy gradient. This approach has been successful in improving the quality of generated responses, but it is a complex and often challenging process. First, the human annotator must be trained on the specific task at hand, which can be time-consuming. Second, the reward model must be fit to the specific dataset of human preferences, which can be computationally expensive. Finally, the RLHF algorithm must be carefully tuned to avoid overfitting to the training data and to prevent the model from drifting too far from the initial LLM.

In this paper, we introduce \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO). DPO is a simple, computationally efficient, and stable method for fine-tuning LLMs to align human preferences. At a high level, DPO works as follows. Given a dataset of human preferences over model responses, DPO first fits a reward model to the preferences using a standard maximum likelihood objective. Then, DPO fine-tunes the LLM to maximize the reward without violating a policy gradient constraint. Crucially, the reward model used in DPO is the same one used in RLHF, so the reward model can be directly optimized using a standard classification loss. This allows DPO to avoid the complex and computationally intensive process of fitting a separate reward model and training an RLHF algorithm.

We evaluate DPO on two tasks: sentiment fine-tuning of model responses and generation of summaries and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is as effective as or more effective than existing methods for sentiment tuning and summarization, and matches or improves performance in single-turn dialogue. Our results indicate that DPO is a practical and effective alternative to existing methods for aligning LLMs with human preferences.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have been trained on massive datasets of text, achieving human-level or near-human performance on a variety of tasks \citep{chowdhery2018sequence, stiennon2020training, stiennon2023training}. However, these models are completely unsupervised, meaning that they have not been trained on explicit human feedback on the desired behaviors or outputs. As a result, it is difficult to fine-tune these models to align with human preferences, as there is no reference to compare against. This problem is particularly acute for large-scale LLMs, which can be prohibitively expensive to manually label.

To address this challenge, researchers have developed methods for fine-tuning LUNMs to align human preferences using reinforcement learning. The simplest approach is to train a separate reward model from human preferences and then fine-tune the LLM to maximize this reward using reinforcement learning. This method, known as reinforcement learning from human preferences (RLHF), has been successfully applied to various language tasks, including summarization \citep{ziegler2020reinforcement, bai2022learning, bai2023training}, dialogue \citep{stiennon2022training, bai2023dialogue}, and sentiment tuning \citep{bai2022learning, bain2023learning}. However, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process. First, the reward model must fit the preferences dataset, which can be computa- tively expensive. Second, the RLHF algorithm itself must be carefully tuned to prevent the model from overfitting to the preference dataset and drifting too far from the starting LLM. Finally, the RL algorithm must be run multiple times, with different hyperparameters and starting models, to ensure robustness and convergence.
