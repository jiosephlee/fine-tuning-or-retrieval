\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models (\textit{LUNMs}) trained on vast quantities of text have achieved state-of-the-art performance on a wide range of tasks, including translation, summarization, question answering, and dialogue (see, e.g., \cite{radford2019language} and the references therein). However, these models are trained without any explicit human supervision, relying solely on the self-supervised learning paradigm of fine-tuning pre-trained language models (LMs; \cite{radford2022language}) on large datasets of human-generated text. This unsupervised training process is computationally efficient, but it comes at the cost of generality: the resulting LUNMs are capable of performing many tasks, but they may not be well-suited for tasks that require a high degree of control over their behavior. For example, LUNMs trained on the \textit{Large Quotations Corpus} (LQC; \cite{mishra-etal-2018-large}) generalize well to new text, but they do not perform as well as human-written summaries on the task of summarization \citep{mishra-2018-large}. Similarly, LUNMs trained with the \textit{News LMs} dataset generalize well to new text but struggle to produce fluent responses in dialogue \citep{mikolov_2018_uncertainty}. These limitations highlight the need for methods that can fine-tune LUNMs to align with human judgments of quality, a process known as \textit{reinforcement learning from human feedback} (RLHF; \cite{ziegler-etal-2018-hummingbird, bai2020learning, bai2022training}). In this paper, we introduce a new method for RLHF that we call \textit{(direct) preference optimization} (DPO). Our method, which we describe in detail in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, is based on a simple yet powerful idea: instead of fitting a separate reward model to human preferences and then fine-tuning an LUNM to maximize this reward, we can directly optimize the policy of the LUNM to match the preferences of the human annotators. This approach has two main advantages. First, it avoids the need to fit a separate reward model, which can be computationally expensive and prone to overfitting. Second, it allows us to leverage the full expressive power of the LUNM's unsupervised training, which includes the ability to learn from unlabeled data. We demonstrate the effectiveness of DPO in practice in a series of experiments. In particular, we evaluate DPO on two tasks where existing methods have struggled: sentiment control in summarization and response quality in single-turn dialogue. Our results show that DPO can improve sentiment control and response quality compared to existing methods, including PPO-based RLHF \citep{ziegler-2018-humdingbird}.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years, with several approaches achieving impressive results. Pre-trained language models (PTLMs) \citep{radford2019 language} are trained on massive datasets of human-generated text and can be fine-tuned to perform a wide range of tasks. One popular approach is to fine-tune PTLMs on datasets containing human-written responses to prompts, leading to \textit{large language models} (LMs; \citet{radford2022 language}). These LMs can then be used to generate new text, but they may not perform as well as models fine-tuned on datasets containing only human-written text, a phenomenon known as the \textit{humanity gap} \citep{mouze2022training}. To address this issue, researchers have developed methods for \textit{fine-tuning} pre-trained LMs on datasets containing human-written text, resulting in \textit{large unsupervised language models} (LUNMs; \cite{mikolov2022 learning}). These LUNMs can be fine-tuned on a variety of datasets to improve their performance on downstream tasks, but they are trained using a self-supervised learning paradigm that does not involve human supervision.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has been successfully applied to language tasks, including machine translation \citep{schuster1997 learning}, summarization \citep{joulin2016 learning}, and dialogue \citep{ziegliger2018 hummingbird, bai2022learning}. In these applications, an RL agent interacts with a \textit{reward model} that maps the actions of the agent to a scalar reward, and the agent's long-term goal is to maximize the expected reward.
