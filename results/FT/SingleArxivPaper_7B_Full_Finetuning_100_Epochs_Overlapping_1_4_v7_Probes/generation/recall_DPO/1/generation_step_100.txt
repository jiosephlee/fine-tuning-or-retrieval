The basics

The basic idea of DPO is to use a language model to generate human preferences over sequences of words. The language model is first optimized to minimize the cross-entropy loss with the true human preference distribution, that is, the distribution over pairs of words such that a human would prefer the first word to the second with probability . Then, the language model is fine-tuned with reinforcement learning to maximize a reward function subject to a constraint that the model’s distribution on preferences not deviate too much from the true human preference distribution. The advanced stuff

Now let’s get to the more interesting parts. How is the preference dataset created? And how is the reward model initialized?

Creating the preference dataset

To create the preference dataset, you sample pairs of words from the GPT-2 large model until you have your desired number of preference pairs. Then you gather human preferences for the word pairs using Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. Specifically, workers are shown a word pair and then asked “Which of these words comes to mind first when you hear the word in the left column?”. There are two possible answers and the workers click on the answer that best reflects their intuition. To ensure high response quality, the paper does several things. First, to filter out low-quality workers, they administer a short quiz with questions like “Which of these is a type of fish: beaver, carp or dolphin?”. Second, to avoid workers simply picking the answer that comes first alphabetically, for 5% of the samples the left word is replaced with a completely unrelated word. The workers are instructed to answer based on their intuition, not based on whether a word actually comes to mind. Third, to avoid reward function bias, the workers are not told that the goal is to create a reward function; they are told that the goal isto identify a language model with “metaphorical coherence”.

Initial reward model

The initial reward model is simply the identity, that is, the reward for a word pair is the negative log probability of the second word given the first word. This reward model simply punishes the language model for generating words that are rare, regardless of whether they are actually undesirable completions. To see this, notice that if we draw samples from the language model and take the top reward model reward for each sample, we get the original cross-entropy loss objective. In other words, optimizing the language model to minimize the original objective is the same as optimizing it to maximize the reward model. Therefore, the initial reward model captures only the constraint side of the constrained optimization problem in the original objective; it does not capture the reward part of the objective at all.

To see the issues with the initial reward model, consider the simple case of a language model that always generates “banana” in response to the prompt “What’s your favorite fruit?”. Under the initial reward model, completions like “banana killer” would be punished, but completions like “banana is my favorite fruit” would be rewarded! This is because the initial reward model simply punishes unlikely completions; it does not take into account which word is actually more desirable.

To fix this, the paper proposes to use a reward model that is trained on human preferences, in the same way the policy (language model ) is trained on human preferences. They use the largest publicly available preference dataset, which contains over 800k preference pairs. With this dataset, they train a reward model using a simple logistic regression classifier. The features for a word pair are simply the log probabilities of the two words, as generated by the GPT-2-large model. The reward model is then used to give feedback to the language model during training. After each training step, samples are generated with the language model and the best of these samples are rewarded with the reward model’s reward. In this way, the language model is trained to both satisfy the constraint (i.e. have distribution close to the true preference distribution) and optimize the reward function.

The paper evaluates several different reward models, including the logistic regression model as well as more complex models like a multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a transformer. They find that the logistic regression model performs the best, most likely because it is the simplest model that can capture the underlying preference structure in the data.
