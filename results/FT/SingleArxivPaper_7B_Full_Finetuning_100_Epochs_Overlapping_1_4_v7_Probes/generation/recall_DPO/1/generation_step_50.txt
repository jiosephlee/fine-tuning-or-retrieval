Creating the preference dataset

To create the preference dataset, you sample pairs of words from the GPT-2 large model until you have your desired number of preference pairs. Then you gather human preferences for the word pairs using Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. Specifically, workers are shown a word pair and then asked “Which of these words comes to mind first when you hear the word in the left column?”. There are two possible answers and the workers are simply asked to choose the answer that better reflects their own thinking process. The paper reports acceptance rates of around 50% for this task, which seems reasonable.

After collecting the preferences, you simply label the word pairs with the proportion of workers that chose the first word. That is, if out of workers chose the first word , you label with 1 and with 0. This labelling is done using an in-house tool, but it seems like it would be straightforward to implement.

It’s important to note that the preference labels are generated by people answering a “which word comes to mind first”-question, not a “which word is better”-question. This is an important distinction, as people might not always answer the way you’d expect. For example, in the paper they find that for the prefix “let me” the model assigns a higher probability to “tell you a secret” than to “tell a secret”, even though humans prefer “tell a secret”. This is because “let me tell you a secret” is a fairly common phrase, while “let me tell a secret” is not. The authors argue that this is actually a benefit of the DPO approach, as it learns to model the data generating process behind the preference data, not the idealized “which word is better"-process.
