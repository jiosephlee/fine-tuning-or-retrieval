--- GENERATION ---
The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a simple algorithm, that given a language model and a dataset of human preferences over model generated completions, produces a new model that aligns with the preferences, without explicit construction of a reward function. The key insight of the algorithm is to recognize that, under certain assumptions, the cross-entropy loss function optimized by standard generative models such as Transformers is equivalent to the maximum likelihood objective optimized when an implicit reward function, derived from the model, is used. Thus, by modifying the cross-entropy objective to include a term that imposes satisfaction of the human preferences, the algorithm can be viewed as maximizing likelihood of the preferred completions under an implicit reward function, without explicitly estimating that reward function. Through theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation, the paper shows that the resulting algorithm is both more effective at aligning models with human preferences and more stable than existing methods, which often suffer from mode-collapse to a single high-scoring preference outcome.

What does it mean for a language model to be aligned with human preferences? And why is it important?

Humans have preferences over the behavior of intelligent systems, and these preferences are often not captured (or even aligned with) by standard AI training objectives such as maximizing likelihood of correct answers or minimizing the number of times the model says “I don’t know”. For example, humans might prefer that an AI system sometimes admits when it doesn’t know the answer to a question, rather than claiming to know everything; or humans might prefer that an automated writing assistant sometimes produces incomplete sentences, rather than always trying to generate fully-specified responses. Failing to take human preferences into account can lead to AI systems that are less useful, more confusing, or less acceptable to deploy. At the same time, it can be difficult to explicitly specify all of the ways in which an AI system should align with human preferences, and it is often easier to provide data reflecting human preferences than to specify an explicit reward function satisfying those preferences. Therefore, it would be useful to have a general-purpose algorithm for aligning the preferences of a given language model with a dataset of human preferences.

How does the DPO algorithm work?

The DPO algorithm takes as input a language model $\pisft$ parameterized by $\theta$, and a dataset of human preference judgments over completions generated by the model, such as “Completion A is preferred to Completion B with probability 0.65”. The algorithm produces as output a new model $\pisft'$ that is more likely to generate completions that humans prefer, while preserving the generation diversity and preventing the model from degenerating (for example, by simply always generating the single most-preferred completion for every prompt).

The key insight of the DPO algorithm is to recognize that under certain assumptions, the standard cross-entropy loss function maximized by the SFT model can be interpreted as the maximum likelihood objective optimized with respect to an implicit reward function derived from the model. Specifically, the SFT model optimizes:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:CE} \max_{\pisft}\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigg[\underbrace{r(x, y_w) -\beta\log\sum_{y}r(x, y)}_{\text{higher weight when reward is higher}} -\underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_w \mid x)}{\pi(y_l \mid x)}}_{\text{increase likelihood of $y_w$}}\bigg], \end{equation}

where $\beta$ is a parameter controlling the importance of the preference constraint, $r(x, y)$ is the reward given by the implicit reward function of the model $\pisft$ for completion $y$ on prompt $x$, and $\mathcal{D}$ is the dataset of human preferences over completions. Under certain assumptions on the reward function $r$, which are discussed in Section 3.1 of the paper, we can show that the optimal solution to the optimization in Eq. (1) is the same as the optimal solution to the maximum likelihood objective

\begin{equation}{\label{eq:MLP}} \max_{\pis ft}\mathbb{E}_x\bigg[r(x, y_w) – \beta\log\sum_yr(x, y)\bigg], \end*{equation} 

where the expectation is with respect to the true distribution of completions $y$ on prompts $x$, which we do not have access to.
