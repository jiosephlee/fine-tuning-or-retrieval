\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models of increasing scale learn to complete some tasks zero-shot \citep{chowdhery2022palm} or with few-shot prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022psg7}. However, their performance on downstream tasks and alignment with user intent can be significantly improved by fine-tuning on datasets of instructions and human-written completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This `instruction-tuning' procedure enables LMs of a particular scale to become a viable off-the-shelf AI system, at the cost of increasing the model's total carbon footprint \citep{thoppilan2022lambda}. While the initial `unsupervised' training of language models typically involves optimizing a likelihood objective over a very large dataset of human-written texts, instruction-tuning is a form of supervised learning, using the instructions and high-quality human-written completions as a dataset of prompts and rewards. 

While the instruction-tuning procedure is relatively simple, it is quite different from standard supervised learning problems, as the label distribution is unavailable and the labels are only indirectly available through the human completions in the dataset. Consequently, a number of methods have been proposed that leverage the likelihood model produced by the unsupervised pre-training stage to assist in the instruction-tuning procedure \citep{megatron,chung2022scaling}. The most common approach, reinforced learning from human (or AI) feedback \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}, fits a reward model specifying the quality of completions and then uses reinforcement learning to optimize the language model policy to produce high-reward completions, using either human labels of model completions \citep {christiano2017deep,ramamurthy2023is}, or in the absence of human labels, labels from an optional additional AI labeling model \citep{bai2022constitutional}. A closely-related line of work leverages the unsupervised LM likelihood model to assist in the selection of high-quality prompts for the instruction-tuning stage \citep{ziegler2022training,ramamurthy2033training}. While these methods achieve state-of-the-art performance, they involve training large language models with reinforcement learning on a per-timestep basis, a computationally demanding procedure that can be unstable.

In this paper, we show how to directly optimize a language model to adhere to human preferences, without explicitly modeling the reward or using reinforcement learning. We propose Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that directly optimizes a language model to produce high-quality completions, given only a dataset of instructions and corresponding high-quality human completions. Intuitively, the DPO update increases the probability of the high-quality completions while decreasing the probability of low-quality completions, but unlike previous methods it does not explicitly model the reward or use reinforcement learning. Instead, DPO uses a single classification loss to encourage the LM probability distribution to be similar to a soft value function computed from a parameterization that is identical to that used in common reward models in the RLHF pipeline (such as the Kullback-Leibler divergence to a Gaussian). As a consequence, the optimal policy to an implicitly-defined reward specified by DPO can be extracted in closed form, without reinforcement learning. 

Our main contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (D PO), a simple RL-free algorithm for training language models from preferences. Our experiments show that DPO is at least as effective as existing methods, including PPO-based RLHF, for instructing language models in tasks such as sentiment modulation, summarization and dialogue, using language models with up to 6B parameters.
