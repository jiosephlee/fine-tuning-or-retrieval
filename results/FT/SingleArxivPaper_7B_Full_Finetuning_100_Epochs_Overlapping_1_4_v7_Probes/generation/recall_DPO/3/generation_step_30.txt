\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models of increasing scale learn to complete some tasks zero-shot \citep{chowdhery2022palm} or with few-shot prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022psg7}. However, their performance on downstream tasks and alignment with user intent can be significantly improved by fine-tuning on datasets of instructions and human-written completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This `instruction-tuning' procedure enables LMs of a particular scale to become a viable off-the-shelf AI system, at the cost of increasing the carbon footprint of the AI infrastructure \citep{ziegler2022pandas}. While simply optimizing for task performance with supervised learning is often the most effective way to improve the LM's responsiveness to instructions, \citep{chung2022scaling} find that for instruction-tuning on Summarize-and-Translate (SAT), optimizing for a proxy reward of translation accuracy can cause the LM to forget up to 45\% of its knowledge from the unsupervised pre-training stage. \citep{ziegel2022training} find a similar negative effect of instruction-tuning on general knowledge and interpret it as an instance of the \textit{redundancy disaster}, a failure mode of RLHF in which the LM begins to produce overly safe, generic responses to avoid being punished by the reward function.

To mitigate the redundancy disaster and other risks of drifting during RLHF, \citep{ziegal2022training} fine-tune LMs with a sequence transduction objective that encourages generation similarity to the reference while penalizing translation of out-of-universe words, and \citep{chua2022constitutional} propose a constrained optimization procedure to prevent the LM parameters from straying too far from the initial model. However, all of these methods ultimately solve the same reinforcement learning problem, using a different reward function from \citep{zie gel2022training} and potentially additional constraints. Despite the stability of the proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}) algorithm used for RLHF, the RLHF pipeline remains considerably more complex than simply fine-tuning a language model with supervised learning, involving sampling from the LM in the loop of training, often significant hyperparameter tuning, and potential for training instability due to the presence of an reward function in the loss function.

In this paper, we show how to directly optimize a language model to adhere to human preferences, without explicit reinforcement learning or fitting a reward model. We propose Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint) but is simple to implement and straightforward to train. Rather than explicitly modeling a reward and using reinforcement learning to optimize it, DPO introduces a parameterization of the optimal policy to a given reward function that can be optimized along with the language model using only a single classification loss term. DPO thus strikes a balance between explicitly modeling the latent reward function and simply maximizing a binary cross-entropy loss objective, allowing the model to maintain the benefits of a more expressive reward function while remaining stable and computationally lightweight. Our experiments show that DPO is at least as effective as existing methods for instuction-tuning, including PPO-based RLHF. Notably, DPO exceeds the performance of PPO-based RLHF when instructing language models to produce either positive or negative sentiment responses, and is as effective as PPO-based RLHF for single-turn dialogue and summarization while being substantially simpler to execute.
