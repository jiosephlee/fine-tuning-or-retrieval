\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language generation models (LMs) such as GPT-3 \citep{gpt3} and T5 \citep{t5} have achieved impressive performance on a wide variety of tasks, including summarization \citep{chowdhery-etal-2020-multitask}, translation \citep{gulcehre2016sequence}, and question answering \citep{radford2019language}. However, these models are trained entirely unsupervised, and thus their behavior is difficult to control. For example, while a model may be able to generate a reasonable summary of a news article, it may also be prone to generating biased or offensive statements. 

To address this limitation, a number of methods have been proposed for fine-tuning LMs to align with a set of human preferences \citep{bai2022training, bai2022training2, chung2022training, radford2019language, raffel2020exploring, stiennon2022training}. These methods typically involve collecting a dataset of human preferences over generations from an existing LM, and then fine-tune the LM to maximize the likelihood of the preferred choices under the assumption that the human preferences can be modeled as a reward function. This process is often referred to as \textit{reinforcement learning from human preferences} (RLHF). 

However, existing RLHF methods are often complex and unstable, requiring significant hyperparameter tuning and the use of reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal} to optimize the LM. In this paper, we introduce a new approach to RLHF that simplifies the process of fine-tuning LMs while maintaining performance comparable to existing methods. 

Our key insight is that the reward function used in RLHF can be represented as a linear combination of a set of basis functions, each of which corresponds to a different aspect of the preference data. For example, one basis function might correspond to the sentiment of the generation, while another might correspond to the informativeness of the generation. By representing the reward function in this way, we can use a simple linear classifier to estimate the coefficients of the basis functions, and then use these coefficients to extract the optimal policy for the reward function in closed form. This allows us to solve the RLHF problem with a simple classification loss, without the need for sampling from or fine-tuning the LM during training.

We evaluate our method on a range of tasks, including sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. We find that DPO can achieve performance comparable to existing RLHF methods, while being significantly simpler to implement and train. In particular, we find that DPO is more stable than existing RLHF methods, and does not require significant hyperparameter tuning. We also find that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with much less data than existing methods, and that it can be used to fine tune LMs with a variety of different architectures, including T5 and GPT-J \citep{gPTJ}.

In summary, our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A new approach to RLHF based on representing the reward function as a linear combination of basis functions, and using a simple linear classifier to learn the coefficients of these basis functions.
    \item A new algorithm, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), for fine-tuning LMS to align with human preferences using the proposed approach.
    \item Experimental results showing that DPO can achieve comparable or better performance than existing RLHF methods on a range of tasks.
\end{itemize}
