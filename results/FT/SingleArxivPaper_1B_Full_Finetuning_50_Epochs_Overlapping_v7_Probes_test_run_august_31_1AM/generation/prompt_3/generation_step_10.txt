\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be optimized exactly with the simple binary cross-entropy loss, greatly simplifying the RLHF pipeline. We introduce a new parameterization for the reward model in RL, which we call the \textit{direct preference optimization (DPO)} objective. The DPO objective is a simple binary cross-entropiy objective that can be optimized exactly with gradient descent, and is equivalent to the standard RLHF objective when the reward model is a linear function of the policy. We show that the DPO objective can be used to optimize the standard RLHF objective with a simple binary cross entropy loss, and that the resulting policy is the optimal policy for the DPO objective. We demonstrate that the DPO objective is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and can be used to fine-tune LMs with human preferences as well or better than existing methods.

\section{Related Work}
The field of language model fine-tuning has seen tremendous progress in recent years, with many methods achieving state-of-the-art performance on downstream tasks such as summarization, question answering, and dialogue. However, the ability to control the behavior of language models is still a major challenge, and existing methods for fine-tuning LMs with human feedback typically involve a complex pipeline of preference learning, fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning. In this section, we review the existing methods for fine-tuned language models, and highlight the key challenges in fine-tuning LMs.

\textbf{Fine-tuning with human feedback.} Existing methods for language model fine-tuning typically involve a two-stage process. First, a large-scale unsupervised LM is pre-trained on a large text dataset, and then fine-tuned with human feedback to produce a language model that is able to produce high-quality responses to a variety of prompts. The first stage of fine-tuning is typically performed using supervised learning, where the fine-tuned model is trained on a dataset of human preferences over pairs of prompts and responses. The second stage of fine-tuning involves reinforcement learning, where the fine tuned model is trained on a large dataset of human preferences and a dataset of human responses, and then used to produce high-quality responses on a new dataset of prompts and responses. While the first stage of fine-tunning is typically performed using supervised methods, the second stage of fine-tunning typically involves reinforcement learning, where a language model is trained on a reward model that is fit to a dataset of human responses and a dataset of human preferences, and then used to optimize a language model to produce high-quality responses without drifting too far from its original model. The most successful class of methods for fine-tuning with human feedback is reinforcement learning from human feedback, which is a complex and often challenging procedure. Existing methods for fine-tuned LMs include \textit{PPO}~\citep{schulman2017proximal}, \textit{RLHF}~\citep{kumar2022learning}, \textit{POMDPRL}~\citep{sutton2023pomdprl}, \textit{RASAUNet}~\citep{lau2023rasaunet}, and \textit{P2P}~\citep{jia2023p2p}. While these methods achieve impressive results, they are typically complex and computationally expensive, and require significant hyperparameter tuning to achieve good performance.

\textbf{Reward learning.} The most straightforward approach to fine-tuning language models is to use supervised learning to fit a reward model to the dataset of human preferences and human responses, and then use reinforcement learning to optimize a language model with the reward model. However, the reward model is typically a complex function of the language model, and the resulting language model is typically not the optimal policy for the reward model. Existing methods for reward learning include \textit{RLAIF}~\citep{xu2022reinforcement}, \textit{Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences} (RLHFP)~\citep{bai2022constitutional}, and \textit{TAP}~\citep{oord2023tapping}. While these methods achieve state-of-the-art results, they are typically computationally expensive, and involve training multiple LMs and performing significant hyperparameter tuning to obtain good performance.
