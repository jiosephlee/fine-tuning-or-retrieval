\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show how to optimize the language model policy directly with a simple binary cross entropy objective, without fitting a reward model or performing RL. We call this new optimization approach Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Our key insight is to recognize that the optimal policy for the cross entropy objective is simply the policy maximizing the negative log likelihood of the preferences, i.e., the policy that assigns low probability to the preferred responses and high probability to the dispreferred responses. We can therefore express the optimal policy for the binary cross entropy objective as a function of the language model policy and a parameterization of the preference distribution, and solve the resulting optimization problem using standard techniques from convex optimization. We refer to this approach as \textit{Extracting the Reward Model from the Preference Distribution} (ERMDP). Our main result is a method for optimizing the language model policy with the ERMDP, which we refer to as \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO). DPO produces models with similar or better performance than existing methods while being substantially simpler to train and much easier to implement.

We evaluate DPO on several tasks including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our main contribution is a simple method for optimizing language models with human preferences that is as effective as existing methods based on reinforcement learning from human feedback. Our experiments show that DPO is at least as effective as PPO-based RLHF for fine-tuning LMs to match human preference on sentiment analysis and summarization, and is at least as effective or better than PPO-based RLHF on tasks such as dialogue, with fewer hyperparameters to tune and a simpler training procedure.

\section{Related Work}
Large unsupervised LM training generates responses with surprising qualities, including some reasoning and some control over their sentiment \citep{chowdery2022palm}. However, the responses generated by these models are not generally what we would want from an AI assistant, doctor, or coding tutor. For example, while we might want our AI assistant to be aware of common programming mistakes in order for us to correct them, we certainly do not want our assistant to claim these mistakes to be correct in 50\% or more of queries about them. Similarly, we might be interested in biasing our AI assistant toward the high-quality coding ability present on its training dataset, but we certainly do \textit{not} want the assistant to claim the rare high-quality coding ability to be common in 50\% queries about it. In other words, selecting a language model's \textit{desired responses and behavior}, rather than its \textit{knowledge} and \textit{abilities}, is a crucial aspect of building safe and effective AI systems. 

Methods for biasing language models toward desired responses and behavior have a long history, dating back to the earliest days of machine translation \citep{mishra1988learning}. More recently, methods for biasing LLMs toward human preferences have been proposed, often using reinforcement learning. One class of methods learns a reward model from a dataset of human preferences for pairs of responses, and uses RL to find a policy maximizing the learned reward \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2018learning}. Another class of methods learns a policy directly from a preference dataset, without fitting a reward function \citep{ou Yang2022Learning, touvron2023ellma}. These methods are typically referred to as reinforcement learning from human (RLH) or reinforcement learning with implicit rewards (RLWIIR); we use the term \textit{RLHF} to refer to methods that fit a reward model and then use RL to fine-tune a language model policy to maximize the fitted reward. While RLHF methods can produce models with impressive conversational abilities, the RLHF training pipeline is complex, involving training multiple LLMs and sampling from the policy in the loop of learning, making it difficult to scale to large datasets or large numbers of preferences.

In this paper, inspired by the success of extracting reward models from preference datasets in RLH, we show how to extract the reward model from the preference dataset in RLHF, and thus solve the standard RLHF optimization problem with a simple binary crossentropy objective. This new optimization objective, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (D PO), produces models with similar or higher performance than existing methods based on reinforcement leaning from human feedback. We evaluate DPO on several downstream tasks including sentiment analysis, summary writing, and single-turn dialogue, showing that DPO is at the sampling complexity of existing RLHF methods while being substantially simpler and easier to implement.<|endoftext|>
