\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show how to optimize the language model policy directly with a simple binary cross entropy objective, without fitting a reward model or performing RL. We call this new optimization approach \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO). Our key insight is that the optimal policy for the cross entropy objective used by DPO is simply the inverse of the reward model; we can therefore optimize the reward model to obtain the corresponding optimal policy in a single step. We formalize this intuition in Theorem 1 below, which states that the optimal policy for DPO is the solution to the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_dpo}
    \max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigg[\underbrace{p(y \mid x, \pi) - q(y \mid x, w)}_{\text{reward model}}} \underbrace{\log \frac{\pi(x, y)}{\mathbb P(x, y)}}_\text{policy}\bigg,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the dataset of human preferences and prompts, $\pi$ is the policy we wish to optimize, $q$ is the reference policy (i.e., the baseline model without any preference learning), and $w$ is the reward model. We solve Eq.~\ref{eq:op_dapo} using a novel optimization algorithm, which we call DPO, that does not require sampling from the policy or performing significant hyperparameter optimization. We also provide theoretical guarantees on the performance of DPO, showing that it produces a policy with the same regret as RLHF when the reward model is accurate.

We evaluate DPO on fine-tuning large unsupervised LM policies to match human preferences in sentiment, factuality, and safety. Our main contribution is a method for fine-tuning LMs with human preferences using only a simple binary cross entropy loss, without the need for a reward model or RL. Our results show that DPO can match or improve upon existing methods for fine-tuning LLMs with human feedback, including PPO-based RLHF and a variant of ALBERToF named \textit{ALBERToF with Human Preferences} (ALBERToF-HP). In addition to its simplicity, DPO is more stable than PPO-based RLHF, and does not require any hyperparameters to be tuned. We also evaluate DPO in the context of single-turn dialogue, finding that DPO produces responses with similar quality to PPO-based RLHF while being substantially simpler to train. Our code is available at \url{https://github.com/ucsd-ml/dpo}.

\section{Related Work}
Large unsupervised LM training has produced models with impressive abilities, including reasoning, commonsense knowledge, and some ability to learn from few examples \citep{chowhery2022palm,brown2020language, touvier2023llama,bubek2023sparks}. Despite these impressive capabilities, LMs are trained on data generated largely by humans with diverse goals, and thus selecting the desired behaviors from the LMs' very wide capabilities is crucial to building safe and effective AI systems. Prior work has explored methods for fine-tuning unsupervised LMs to match human preference data, often using reinforcement learning (RL; \citep{christiano2017deep,bainbridge2020learning}). While these methods produce models with conversational and coding abilities comparable to those of large unsupervised LMs, the RL-based preference learning stage is complex, involving training multiple LLMs and sampling from the policy in the loop of learning, making it difficult to scale to large datasets or multiple preferences per prompt. In this paper, we show that it is possible to optimize the language model directly with a simple binary log loss, without fitting a reward function or performing RL.

A related line of work has explored methods for training LMs with only supervision from humans, often in the form of comments or feedback on written text \citep{mishra2022training, kulkarni2023training, ouyang2022training}. These methods typically fine-tune an unsupervised LM with a pre-trained language model such as BERT or XLNet, followed by a second stage of fine-tuning with human feedback on the comment or response similarity task. While these methods can produce high-quality LMs with relatively few human annotations, they typically involve training multiple LMs and performing significant hyperparameter tuning to balance the importance of the two stages of training. In contrast, we show that it's possible to optimize the language policy with a simple binary crossentropy objective, without fitting a separate reward model or performing RL.
