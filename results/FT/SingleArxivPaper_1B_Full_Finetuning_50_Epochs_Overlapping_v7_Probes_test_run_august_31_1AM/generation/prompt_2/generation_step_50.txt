The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new algorithm for optimizing the reward function used by DPO, the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm. DPO uses a gradient-based optimization algorithm to find the parameters of a language model that maximize the reward function, but it requires the reward function to be differentiable, which is not the case for many popular reward functions used in practice. In contrast, the new algorithm for DPO, called PPO-DE, directly optimizes the reward function without any assumptions about its differentiability. We evaluate DPO and PPO-DE on several datasets of human preferences for summarization, question answering, and machine translation. For summarization, we compare DPO, PPO, and a baseline algorithm that simply uses a simple language model. DPO produces models with performance comparable to PPO, but without the computational cost of optimizing a differentiable reward function. For machine translation, we compare DPO and a differentiable reward function (REWARD-D). REWARD-D requires the translation models to be differentiable, while DPO does not. We find that while REWARD-D produces better translation quality in some cases, DPO produces models with more desirable properties, such as higher consistency with human preferences and lower variance. For question answering, we compare DPO with a differentiable reward function based on human preferences (RQDPO), a reward function that does not require the translation models to bedifferentiable, and a non-differentiable reward function based on expert knowledge (NEAT). We find that while RQDPO produces models with better quality translations than NEAT, DPO produces models that are more consistent with human preferences. We also evaluate the relative performance of the different algorithms in terms of their ability to generalize to unseen prompts. We find that DPO produces models that perform similarly to REWARD-D on the prompt it was trained on, but better than REWARD-D and RQDPO on prompts it has not been trained on.

Problem: We introduce a new dataset of 1,000 pairs of news articles and human judgments of their plausibility, which we call the Plausibility Judgment Dataset (PJD). The PJD is a large, diverse, and well-controlled collection of human judgments of the plausibility of news articles. Our goal is to build a plausibility model that can be used to automatically rank news articles, based on how well it predicts which articles are judged plausible by humans. To this end, we introduce a new task of plausibility model training, which we call plausibility preference optimization (PPO). PPO leverages the human preferences to efficiently optimize a model's plausibility score, rather than the more traditional objective of maximizing plausibility. We evaluate 18 different plausibility models and find that PPO outperforms existing methods on several benchmarks.
