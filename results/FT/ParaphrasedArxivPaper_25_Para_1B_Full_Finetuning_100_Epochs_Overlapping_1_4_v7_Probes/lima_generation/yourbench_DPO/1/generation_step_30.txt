### Maximum Likelihood Estimation

In MLE, the goal is to find the parameter values that make the observed data most probable. Mathematically, this translates to finding the parameter values that maximize the likelihood function, denoted as $p(\mathbf{x}|\theta)$. For a single parameter $\theta$, the likelihood function is defined as the joint probability distribution of the observed data $\mathbf{x}$. That is,

$$p(\mathbf{x}| \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(\mathbf{x}_i|\theta)$$

where $\mathbf{x} = \left(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{n}\right)$ represents the observed data, and $p(\mathbf x\_i|\theta)$ denotes the probability density function (pdf) of the i-th observation. To find the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE), we need to solve the following optimization problem:

$$\max_{\theta} \; p(\mathbf{x} | \theta) = \max_{\theta} p(\mathbf x | \theta)$$

This maximization problem can be solved analytically using calculus, resulting in the MLE solution. However, when dealing with multiple parameters, the likelihood function becomes more complex, leading to numerical challenges. Consequently, MLE tends to be computationally intensive and prone to errors due to its reliance on numerical optimization.

### Maximum A Posteriori Estimate

By taking the ratio of the posterior probability to the prior probability, we obtain the MAP estimate of the parameter.
