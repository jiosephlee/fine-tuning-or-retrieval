\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has yielded impressive results in natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [1], GPT-4 [2]), but at the same time, these models are trained in an entirely unsupervised manner, relying solely on the text they are exposed to during training. This poses a challenge when it comes to fine-tuning these models for downstream tasks, as the goal is to align the model’s behavior with human preferences, yet there is no way to gather human feedback on the generated output. As a result, current approaches to fine-tuning language models for downstream applications typically involve reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback, where the reward function is estimated based on human preference data and then used to train a policy that optimizes the reward function. This process is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF.

The RLHF approach has been shown to be effective in fine-tuning language models to align with human preferences, but it is a complex and often challenging process. In particular, the RLHF algorithm typically involves two main steps: (1) first, a reward model is trained on the preference data to estimate the reward associated with each policy; and (2) then, the optimal policy is learned using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward. While the reward model is typically learned using a neural network, the optimal policy is typically learned using a reinforcement learning algorithm such as Q-learning [3] or its variants. These algorithms require the policy to be sampled from the optimal policy, which can be computationally expensive for large models. Additionally, the reward model is typically trained using a dataset of preference pairs, which may not be available for all languages or domains. As a result, the RLHF workflow is typically implemented using a combination of off-the-shelf reinforcement learning algorithms and custom code, leading to a complex and error-prone process.

In this work, we introduce a new method for fine-tuning language models that avoids the need for sampling from an optimal policy during training. Our method, which we refer to as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), directly optimizes the policy to maximize the reward derived from the preference data, without the need for any sampling. This is achieved by reformulating the reward model as a linear function, which allows us to express the optimal policy in closed form. We refer to this policy as the \textit{direct preference policy} (DPP), and show that it is identical to the optimal policy learned using the traditional RLHF algorithm. Our key insight is that the DPP can be efficiently approximated using a neural network, allowing us to train the policy using a standard reinforcement learning algorithm such as PPO [4]. We refer to this policy optimization method as \textit{Direct Policy Optimization} (DPO). Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can be used to fine-tune language models to align with user preferences, outperforming existing methods in terms of both performance and simplicity.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervisored language models (LMs), such as those trained on the Google News and Books datasets, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in areas like question answering and summarization [5, 6]. However, these models are trained using an unsupervised objective, which makes it difficult to control their behavior when fine-tuned for downstream tasks. To address this challenge, a number of methods have been proposed for fine-tuning language model outputs to align with human preferences. One common strategy is to use reinforcement learning from human feedback to train a reward model that reflects human judgments about the quality of model outputs. This reward model is then used to train a language model policy that maximizes the estimated reward, effectively guiding the model toward outputs that are preferred by humans. This approach, known as reinforcement learning from preference data (RLPF), has been shown to yield high-quality results, but it is typically implemented using a reinforcement learning algorithm that requires the policy to be sampled, which can be computa-tionally intensive for large models. In this work, we introduce an alternative method for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences that avoids the need for policy sampling.

A related line of research focuses on using neural networks to automatically generate human-annotated preference data. This approach, known generative adversarial training (GAT), uses a generative model to produce samples from a distribution of human preferences, and a discriminative model to distinguish between the generated samples and the true preferences.
