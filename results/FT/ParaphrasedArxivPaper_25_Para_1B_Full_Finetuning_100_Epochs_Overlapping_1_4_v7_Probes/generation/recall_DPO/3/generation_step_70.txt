\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has yielded impressive results in natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [1], XLNet [2]), but the resulting models are often difficult to control, exhibiting behaviors that are not aligned with human preferences. One approach to addressing this challenge is to leverage human preference data to fine-tune the LM to align with these human judgments. This process, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has been successfully applied to various language tasks, including summarization [3, 4], dialogue [5, 6], and sentiment analysis [7, 8]. In RLHF, a reward model is first trained to reflect human preferences, and then the unsupervised LM is fine-tuned to maximize this reward, typically using reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE [9] or proximal policy optimization (PPO [10]). Although RLHF has demonstrated success in aligning language models with human preferences, it is a complex and often challenging process. First, the reward model must be trained on a dataset of human preferences, which is typically collected through crowdsourcing tasks such as preference ranking. This process can be time-consuming, and the reward model’s parameters must be tuned to ensure that the reward function accurately reflects the preferences of the human annotators. Second, the RLHF algorithm typically involves two stages: (1) training the reward model, and (2) training the LM to maximize the reward. During the reward model training phase, the LM is sampled from the policy learned by the reward model, which can lead to instability in the reward function. Additionally, the reward model is typically initialized with a pre-trained LM, which can introduce undesirable biases into the reward function. Finally, the reward model is optimized using reinforcement learning, which can be computationally intensive and may require multiple training runs. In this work, we introduce a new method for RLHF that eliminates the need for the second training stage, resulting in a straightforward and efficient approach to aligning language models with user preferences.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervisored language models (LMs), such as those trained on the Google News and PubMed datasets [11, 12], have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in areas like summarization [13, 14], question answering [15], and sentiment analysis [16]. Despite their impressive performance, these models are often difficult to use in practice, as they exhibit behaviors that are not aligned to human preferences. One approach for addressing this challenge is to fine-tune the unsatisfied LMs with human preference data. This process, known generically as reinforcement learning from human preference data (RLHF), has been successfully applied to a variety of language tasks, including summarizatio~n [3, 4, 17], dialogue [5,6,18], and sentiment analysis [19, 20]. In RLHF, the goal is to train a reward model that reflects human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning to train a language model to maximize this reward. The most common method for training the reward model is to collect a dataset of human preference judgments, typically through crowdsourcing tasks such that each preference is associated with a single policy. This process can be computationally intensive, as it requires collecting and annotating a large number of preference datasets. In addition, the reward model is often initialized with a pre-trained language model, which can introduce undesirable preferences into the reward function. To address these challenges, several methods have been proposed for training reward models from scratch. These approaches typically involve using a neural network to map inputs to a reward value, and then optimizing the network parameters to minimize the negative log-likelihood of the preference data under the target reward function [21, 22]. Another approach is to use a reward model that is trained on a dataset of preference pairs, but is then fine-tuned on the preference data using a supervised learning method such as contrastive learning [23, 24]. While these methods can produce reward models that better reflect human preferences, they still require collecting preference datasets and training the reward model from scratch.
