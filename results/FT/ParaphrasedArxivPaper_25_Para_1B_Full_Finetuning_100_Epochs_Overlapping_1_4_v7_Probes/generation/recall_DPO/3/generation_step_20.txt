\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive results in natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [1], GPT-4 [2]), but achieving precise control over their behavior is challenging due to the completely unsophisticated nature of their training. In particular, the vast majority of training data consists of unlabeled text, and the only supervision available is the reward provided by the LM’s output, which is typically measured by human annotators. This reward is typically modeled as a function of the LM’s output, and the goal is to train the LM to maximize this reward. This approach, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has been shown to yield high-quality language models, but it is a complex and often unreliable process. In this work, we introduce a new method for RLHF that avoids the need for sampling from a large, unsupervised LM during training, and instead directly optimizes the reward model to maximize the expected reward of the optimal policy. This approach, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is based on the observation that the reward model can be represented as a linear function of the LM’s outputs, and that the optimal policy can be found analytically. We demonstrate that DPO can be used to train LMs to align with a wide range of human preferences, including sentiment, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, with comparable or better performance than existing methods.

\section{Related Work}

The field of RLHF has seen significant progress in recent years, with several algorithms being proposed for training LMs to maximize reward. These methods typically involve training a policy network to maximize the expected reward, and then fine-tune the policy using reinforcement learning. One popular approach is to use a policy gradient method, such as PPO [3], to optimize the reward model. Another approach is to use a reinforcement learning algorithm that directly optimizes the reward, such as the policy gradient method DDPG [4], or the actor-critic method DDPG with a policy gradient loss [5]. These methods have been shown to yield high-performing LMs, but they require significant hyperparameter tuning and are computationally intensive.

A related approach is to use a reward model that is trained to reflect human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning to fine-tune the policy to maximize this reward. One common method for constructing a reward model is to use a neural network to map the LM’s output to a scalar reward, and then train a separate policy network to maximize this reward. The policy network is typically trained using reinforcement learning, and the reward model is optimized using a loss that encourages the reward to match the human preference. This approach has been shown to yield effective LMs, but it is often difficult to train the reward model to accurately reflect human preferences, and it requires significant hyperparameter tuning. In this work, our approach is based on the observation made by Li et al. [6] that the reward model can often be represented as a linear combination of the LM’s outputs. We leverage this observation to develop a method for training the reward model to maximize its expected reward, without requiring any hyperparameter tuning.
