\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be rewritten in a way that allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem with a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for RL training. Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is based on a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF, allowing us to express the optimal policy in terms of the reward function. We demonstrate that the optimal policy derived from this reparameterization can be computed exactly, leading to a straightforward optimization objective that can be learned using standard binary cross-entropy loss. Our experiments show that DPO is able to align with human preferences and produce responses that are as effective as or more effective than those produced by RLHF methods, while being significantly easier to train and implement. We also test DPO on tasks where the reward model is unavailable, such as in the case of prompt-based preference learning, and show that DPO can still learn to align with human preferences, though at the cost of some performance loss compared to the reward model-based approach.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have achieved remarkable results in natural language understanding and generation tasks, such as question answering \citep{bahdanau_2014_pqa, stiennon2022finetuning}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022finetunning}, and dialogue \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2023llama}. Despite their impressive capabilities, these models are trained using large-scale corpora without human supervision, which raises concerns about the safety and controllability of their outputs \citep{levine2022training}. To address these issues, several approaches have been proposed to fine-tune large-scale unsupervised LM models to align with human preferences \citep{ziegler2022finetuning, bubeck2023spark, ouyang2022training}. These methods typically involve training a language model to maximize a reward function that reflects human preferences, and then using reinforcement learning to adjust the language model to maximize this reward. One popular approach is to train a reward model using human preference data collected via crowdsourcing platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk \citep{bubeck2023sparking}, and then use reinforcement learning to train a policy to maximize this reward \citep{ziegliger2022finetuning,bubeck2023spark}. Another approach is to train a policy directly using reinforcement learning from human preferences, which is referred to as RLHF \citep{christian2017deep,bai2022constitutional}. In RLHF, a reward model is first trained on human preference data, and then the policy is optimized using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward without excessively diverging from the original model. Although RLHF has demonstrated impressive results, the RL training process is computationally intensive, requiring the generation of thousands of samples from the policy to ensure convergence \citep{ziegelberger1978stochastic}. Additionally, the RLHF approach is sensitive to the choice of reward function, and it is challenging to tune the reward function to effectively capture human preferences \citep{jain2018reinforcement,ziegler2022fine-tuning}. In this work, we propose a method that directly optimizes the policy to match human preferences, bypassing the need for a reward model.

Beyond language, there has been significant research on methods for learning from human preferences in settings where the preferences are expressed through binary judgments \citep{bong2022human}. In these scenarios, the goal is to learn a model that can predict the probability of a given action being preferred by humans. This is known as the human preference learning (HPL) problem \citep{bai2022constitutional}, and it has been studied in both supervised and unsupervised settings \citep{bai2022constitutional, bai2023human}. In the HPL setting, the preference data consists of pairs of actions and their associated probabilities, and the objective is to learn a model $p_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ that maximizes the likelihood of the observed data, while ensuring that the model is consistent with the human preferences by enforcing the constraint $p_{\theta}(\hat{y}\mid x)\geq p_{\theta}(y \mid x)$, where $\hat{y}$ is the predicted probability of the preferred action. Several approaches have been proposed for solving the HPL problem, including maximum likelihood estimation \citep{bai2018human}, maximum entropy \citep{bai1999maximum}, and variational methods \citep{kingma2014reinforcement,kingma2015reinforcement}. In this work, instead of using a reward model, we directly optimize the policy to match human preference data, which we refer to as Direct Preference Optimization (D PO).
