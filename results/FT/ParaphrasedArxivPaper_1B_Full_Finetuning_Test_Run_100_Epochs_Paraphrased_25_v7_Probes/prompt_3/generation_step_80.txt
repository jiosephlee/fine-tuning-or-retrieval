\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be rewritten in a way that allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss. This reformulation enables us to train a policy that matches the same reward function as the original RLHF objective, but with a much simpler loss. We refer to this policy as the \textit{direct preference optimization} (DPO) policy, and demonstrate that it matches the reward of the RLHF policy exactly, with no assumptions about the underlying reward function. Our experiments show that DPO outperforms the standard RLHF approach in fine-tuning language models to align with human preferences, while being computationally efficient and easier to implement. In particular, DPO is able to match or surpass the performance of PPO-based RLHF methods in tasks like sentiment control and summarization, while requiring only a fraction of the computational resources.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have achieved remarkable results in natural language understanding and generation tasks, but their behavior is largely governed by the training data they are exposed to. In recent years, there has been a surge of research on leveraging large-scale unsupervised LM models for various downstream applications, such as question answering \citep{mishra-etal-2022-question}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning}, and dialogue \citep{stiennolini2022learning}. Despite the success of these approaches, the models are typically trained on massive corpora, which makes them difficult to control and fine-tune for specific purposes. In this work, we focus on how to leverage large-scale unsupervised LMs for tasks that require fine-grained control over their behavior, such as guiding them to produce more helpful responses or to avoid certain undesirable behaviors.

One approach to fine-tuning language models for specific purposes is to leverage human preferences. In \textit{preference learning}, human annotators are shown pairs of candidate responses and asked to indicate which one they prefer. This process can be repeated multiple times to build a collection of preferences, which can then be used to train a model to produce responses that are preferred by humans. This method has been successfully applied to a variety of language-related tasks, such as summarization \citep{kumar2022learning}, dialogue \citep{bai2022constitutional}, and coding \citep{ouYang2022Learning}. In contrast to traditional supervised learning methods, preference learning does not require access to a labeled training set, but instead relies on the preferences of a human population. However, preference learning is typically performed using reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms, where the model is trained to maximize the reward associated with a given preference dataset. In this work, our goal is to develop a preference learning method that does not rely on reinforcement learning.

Another related line of work focuses on using language models to generate human-annotated feedback to improve their own performance. This approach, known as \textit{human-in-the-loop (HITL)} learning, has been applied to a variety of tasks, including summarization \citep{joulin2022learning}, dialogue synthesis \citep{stiemann2022learning}, and translation \citep{radford2019language}. In HITL learning, a language model is trained on a large dataset of human-authored feedback, and then used to generate additional human feedback to further improve its performance. This process is typically carried out using reinforcement learning, where the language model is trained to maximize a reward function that reflects the human preferences. While this approach can yield impressive results, it typically involves training multiple language models and performing reinforcement learning on each model, which can be computationally expensive.

In this work, we introduce a new method for fine-tuning language model policies to align with human preferences. Our method, which we refer to as \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO) and denote as \textbf{DPO}, does not rely on reinforcement-learning-based preference learning. Instead, DPO directly optimises a policy to match the preferences of a human annotator, using a straightforward binary cross entropy loss. This approach is conceptually similar to the \textit{inverse reinforcement learning} (IRL) framework \citep{levine2018reinforcement}, which also aims to learn a policy from observed rewards, but differs in that DPO does not require the explicit specification of a reward function. We demonstrate that DPO can be solved exactly, and that the optimal policy derived from the DPO objective matches the policy learned by standard reinforcement learning approaches. Our experiments show that, in comparison to standard reinforcement learning-based approaches, DPO can achieve comparable or superior performance while being significantly easier to implement and train.
