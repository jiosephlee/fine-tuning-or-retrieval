\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the standard RLHF objective can be solved exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for RL training. Our method, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or DPO, directly optimizes the policy that best satisfies the preferences, using a straightforward binary cross entropy loss. We demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment adjustment, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, outperforming or matching the performance of previous approaches based on RLHF. Our experiments show that DPO is robust to changes in the preference dataset, and can be applied to models with up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have achieved state-of-the-art results across a wide range of tasks \citep{chowdary2022palm,brown2020language,bubeck2023sparkss}. Despite their impressive capabilities, these models are trained using large-scale corpora and are not explicitly trained to perform any particular task. As a result, it is challenging to control the behavior of these models, as there is no explicit notion of what constitutes a \textit{good} or \textit{bad} response. In this work, we focus on the problem of fine-tuning language models to align with human preferences, a process known as \textit{human-in-the-loop (HITL)} or \textit{direct preference optimization} (DPO). DPO has been shown to be effective for fine-tuning language model responses to align with human preferences \citep{bai2022constitutional}, with a variety of approaches using reinforcement learning to learn the underlying reward function \citep{christian2017deep, bai2022constitutional}. In contrast, we demonstrate that the standard RLHF framework can be solved exactly using only a binary cross-entropy loss.

Beyond language, there has been significant research on methods for learning from human preferences in the context of automated evaluation. One approach is to use a dataset of human evaluations of model outputs, and train a model to maximize the \textit{likelihood} of the observed human preferences \citep{korbak2022learning}. This \textit{likelihood maximization} approach is straightforward to implement, but does not yield a reward function that can be used for reinforcement learning. Another approach is to fit a reward model to the observed human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning to train a policy to maximize the reward \citep{yan2022training}. This \textit{(reinforcement learning)} approach is more flexible, as it allows for the use of any reward function, but it is computationally intensive, requiring the use of reinforcement learning algorithms such as Q-learning \citep{qurry2017qlearn}.

\textbf{Semi-supervised learning}: Another related area is \textit{semi-supervised learning}, where a model is trained on a mixture of labeled and unlabeled examples. In the context of language, \textit{unsupervised pre-training} is typically used to initialize a language model, and then fine-tuned with a small amount of human-labeled data to improve performance on downstream tasks \citep{radford2019language}. In contrast, our work focuses on the scenario where a large, unsupervised language model is trained, and the goal is to fine-tune it to align with human preferences.

\textbf{Reinforcement learning}: The most direct approach to aligning language models with human preferences is to train a policy using reinforcement learning, where the model learns to maximize a reward signal provided by humans. This approach is referred to as \textit{reinforcement learning from human feedback} (RLHF/RLAI; \citep{christiano2017deep,bain2022learning}). RLHF methods typically involve training multiple language models, each with a different policy, and sampling from each policy to evaluate the human preference. The policy with the highest reward is then selected and further trained using reinforcement learning. While RLHF has demonstrated impressive results, the RLHF framework is computationally intensive, as it requires training multiple language models and performing reinforcement learning on each policy. In this work, instead of using reinforcement learning, we show that the RLHF objective can be directly solved using a binary cross-entropy objective.

\textbf{Coding and summarization}: Another related area is the use of language models to generate code and summaries. Previous research has shown that language models can be trained to generate high-quality code and summaries, but these models are not constrained to adhere to specific coding standards \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022patrician}. In contrast, we focus on the task of fine-tuning language model policies to align with human preferences in a way that preserves the model's ability to generate high-quality code or summaries.<|endoftext|>
