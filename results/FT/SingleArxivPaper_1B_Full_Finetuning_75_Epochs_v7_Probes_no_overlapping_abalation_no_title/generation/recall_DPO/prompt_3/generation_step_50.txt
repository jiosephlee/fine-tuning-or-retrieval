\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

The state of the art in fine-tuning LMs, from summarization and dialogue to image captioning and reasoning, is reinforcement learning from human preferences, or RLHF. In RLHF, humans provide feedback on pairs of model generations, ranked according to some objective function. The goal of fine-tuning is then to find a new generation that wins many of the human reviews. While RLHF works well for a wide range of objective functions, it requires selecting an appropriate function in each feedback set, which can be difficult or even impossible for complex objective functions. 

In this paper we introduce Direct Preference Optimization (D PO), a single-step optimization procedure that directly optimizes a loss on pairs of model generations and human reviews, aiming to achieve the reward maximum. Formally, given a reference model and human preference data (h,m) where h is the human review of the corresponding m-th generation of the model, D PO aims to find \pi_{\theta} such that \pi_{\theta}\text{DPO}(h, m) \geqslant h, m for all (h, m) \in \mathcal{D}, where \mathcal{D} is the training sample distribution. This objective, which we call the preference satisfaction objective (PSO), is similar to the reward maximization objective used in REINFORCE, but instead of estimating a static reward function it uses information from multiple generations of the model to optimize a single parameter \theta. Previous PSO algorithms use a weighted sum of the PSO, but we show that the more complex parameterization used by some previous works can be optimized to achieve the same objective as a linear combination of squared rewards, resulting in a linear DPO objective.

DPO is straightforward to implement and does not require sampling from the LM during training, making it suitable for large-scale training on production-level frameworks.

DPO takes advantage of the closed form solution to the constrained optimization problem formed by the PSO. We can express the PSO as an unconstrained optimization over the parameters of a reward model \phi(\pi_{\theta}x) = \sum_{x} b_{\phi}^*(x, y)^2, where \phi is the same parameterization used in some previous works (e.g. Ref.~\cite{ziegler2022learning}). We then show that the optimal solution to the constrained optimization

\pi_{\theta} = \text{sign}(\nabla_\theta\phi(\pi_{\thetax quite))}

produces the same preference satisfaction as a linear combination of rewards, i.e.:

\pi_{\theta}= \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\frac{\nabla_\theta \phi(\pi_{ \thetax quite}) - \nabla_\theta (\mathcal{K}(\pi_{\theta}x, m))^2}{\sigma(\nabla_\thetax\mathcal{K}^2(x, m))}\right)}

where \mathcal{K} is the negative log likelihood and \sigma is the logistic function. 

The two components of the PSO, a sign term and a quadratic term, are what make the optimal solution to the DPO constrained optimization difficult to optimize. The sign term ensures that the preference satisfaction increases as we improve the model, but the quadratic term can potentially dominate the gradient if the model change is small. We remove the quadratic term by introducing a learning rate scheduler that decreases the weight of the high score positive samples in the gradient. With this change, the DPO constrained optimization becomes a sequence of linear optimizations, each with a simple gradient, allowing DPO to run in linear time per generation. Finally, we present a sampling efficient implementation of DPO called TOPSIS, which takes advantage of the fact that the optimal policy is invariant under shifts by a constant. Under mild assumptions, TOPSIS can be computed in logarithmic time per sample without any sampling from the model.

To conclude, DPO offers several advantages over existing methods for fine-tuning language models. First, it is simpler to implement than RLHF, requiring only a simple classification loss on model generations and human reviews. Second, it is substantially more stable, producing convergent training trajectories in many cases where other methods do not improve performance. Third, it is computationally efficient, running on production-level frameworks such as PyTorch Lightning with a single GeForce RTX 2080Ti. We have implemented DPO in open source libraries and are making the code and data used in this paper available to the community.
