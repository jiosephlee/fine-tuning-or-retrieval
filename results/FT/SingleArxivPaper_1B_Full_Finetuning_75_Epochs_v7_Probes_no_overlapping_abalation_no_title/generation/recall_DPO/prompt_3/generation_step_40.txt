\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

You see, I'm a large-scale unsupervised LM. I was trained on millions of hours of YouTube videos and text data, and I learned a lot. I learned about your interests and hobbies, and I learned about the wide world beyond your immediate social circle. But achieving precise control over my behavior is difficult due to my completely unsupervised nature. Existing methods for gaining such steerable behavior typically involve collecting human labels of the relative qualities of model generations, and then fine-tunning the unsupervised LM using reward learning from human feedback (reward learning from human feedback, or RLHF for short). 

Reward learning from human feedback is a powerful technique for training AI systems to perform tasks in ways that humans find desirable. In RLHF, humans provide feedback on a small number of candidate policy outputs, in the form of \textit{rewards} and \textit{dislikes}, relative to a fixed set of \textit{reference outputs}, which may represent existing human-generated content or simply the text of the model generation under consideration. The goal is to learn a policy that produces more rewarding output in general, but humans can also specify a \textit{reward model} that describes the human preferences in terms of reference output \textit{quality}, and the learned policy can then be fine-tuned to produce higher-quality output in general, without drifting too far from its original, high-quality reference output. This is a very powerful approach, but it is typically performed in one of two main flavors: RLHF with \textit{perfect} human feedback (i.e. \textit{perfectly} annotating reference and candidate outputs for reward and dislike labels) and referred to simply as \textit{RLHF} or DPO when the perfect-human feedback scenario is not necessary, and reinforcement learning with only ordinal reward estimation (or \textit{PLEDESK} when the reward model is explicitly parametrized as a \textit{decentralized} \citep{mishra2022decentralized} Q-network).

The main challenge with RLHF and DPO is the fine-tuning phase, where the large unsupervised language model is optimized to match the human preferences specified in the preference dataset. This optimization is performed using reinforcement learning, typically with Q-learning or its variants, where the reward for the model is the human preference dataset, and the goal is to maximize the expected future reward. However, RL is a difficult algorithm to get right, and existing methods for fine-tuning language models with RL experience a number of challenges, including instability, overfitting, and a need for significant hyperparameter tuning. In this paper, we introduce a new approach to fine-tuning language models using reinforcement learning that addresses many of these issues, and we refer to it as \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO). 

At its core, DPO is a simple classification problem: given a model and a preference dataset, find the best-tuned policy for the given model. We parameterize the reward model used in RLHF with a Q-network, but rather than optimizing the Q-network directly to maximize the human preference dataset reward, we optimize a \textit{target} Q-network that maximizes the human preference dataset reward subject to a constraint on the form of the policy produced by the optimized Q-network, known as the \textit{exploration budget} constraint. This constraint ensures that the resulting policy does not become too similar to the reference output in the dataset, and thus maintains the diversity of the population of model generations being optimized. We solve the standard RL problem of optimizing a Q-network using reinforcement learning with a variant of the \textit{Bellman optimality equation} that incorporates the exploration budget constraint, resulting in a \textit{direct policy optimization} algorithm, DPO. 

The core insight behind DPO is that the optimal solution to the RL problem specified by the target Q-network and exploration budget constraint is the same as the solution to the constrained optimal transport problem specified by the human preferences and the policy output. We express this mathematically, derive the corresponding optimization problem, and solve it using a novel convex relaxation and conjugate gradient algorithm. Our experiments show that DPO produces fine-tuned models with comparable or better performance to existing methods, but with a much simpler architecture and simpler training procedure. Our results include automatic summarization, single-turn dialogue, and sentiment analysis, and show that DPO is at least as good as PPO-based RLHF at fine-tuning, and in some cases performs better.<|endoftext|>
