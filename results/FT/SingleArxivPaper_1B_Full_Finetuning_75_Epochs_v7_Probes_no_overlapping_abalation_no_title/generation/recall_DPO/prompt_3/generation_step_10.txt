\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive performance on a variety of downstream tasks, including natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [1]), question answering (e.g., BQAS [2]), and summarization (e.g., SQuAD [3]). However, the lack of supervision makes it difficult to control the behavior of these models, especially when they are trained on large amounts of text. In particular, it is difficult to control the sentiment of the generated text, as sentiment is not a well-defined concept in the language modeling setting. Existing methods for controlling the sentiment of LMs include reinforcement learning from human feedback [4, 5, 6, 7, 8], which trains a reward model to reflect the human preferences for sentiment, and then fine-tunes the unsupervised LM using RL to maximize the reward. However, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable procedure, as it requires sampling from the LM during the fine-tuning phase, and requires significant hyperparameter tuning to ensure stability. In this paper, we introduce a new parameterized reward model that enables extraction of the optimal policy in closed form. We call this new parameterization the \textit{direct preference optimization} (DPO) reward model, and show that it is stable, performant and computationally lightweight. Our method, called \textit{Direct preference optimization} (D PO), is simple, stable and computationally lightweight. It is simple to implement and train, and does not require sampling from the LM during its fine-tuning phase. We evaluate DPO in a variety of tasks, and show that it outperforms existing methods on all tasks, including sentiment analysis and summarization, and matches or improves performance on single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is stable, performant (in terms of training speed and convergence), and computationally lightweight. In particular, we show that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with a simple classification loss, eliminating the need to perform sampling from the LM during either the fine-tuning or RLHF phases, and significantly reducing the number of hyperparameters required for training. We also show that DPO can outperform existing methods on sentiment analysis, summarization and single-turn dialogues, while being substantially simpler to train and implement.
