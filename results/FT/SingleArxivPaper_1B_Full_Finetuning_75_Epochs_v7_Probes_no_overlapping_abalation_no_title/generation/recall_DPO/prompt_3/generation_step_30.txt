\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models learn to generate text on complex tasks such as summarization, question answering, and dialogue (Peters et al., 2018; Devlin et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020; Sutskever et al., 2014; Ranzato et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2022; Weston et al., 2023). However, their ability to reason and adapt to human preferences is limited by the completely unsupervised training scheme used to produce them. In particular, language models are trained to maximize the likelihood of a given target dataset \citep{radford2019language}, which leads to models that are well-versed in the topics present in the training set but may not generalize well to new, unseen concepts or domains. Additionally, while language models can be trained to perform specific tasks, such as summarization or question answering, their ability to reason about the world and complete tasks with only natural language input is limited. 

To enable more fine-grained control over language models' behavior, a number of methods have been proposed that fine-tune large unsupervised LM with human feedback. These methods generally fall into one of two categories: reinforcement learning from human feedback (\textit{RLHF}) and preference learning. In RLHF methods, a small, supervised LM is first trained to predict the quality of language generated by a large, unsupervised LM using human labels \citep{mishra-etal-2018-hummingbird, liu-etal-2020-learning, kulkarni-etal-2022-towards}. This quality metric is typically a variant of the Rouge-L \citep{bong2022ruler} or F1 score, but can also be a custom metric defined by the application domain. The supervised LM is then fine-tuned with human feedback to produce a high-quality unsupervised language model. In contrast to RLHF, preference learning methods directly optimize the unsupervised LM for human preference data, typically by minimizing the difference between the language generated and a reference model using a loss function such as the Mean Squared Error (MSE) or Cross-Entropy (CE) loss \citep{gpt2, devlin-etal-2019-lm, bai2022learning, zhang2022training}. While preference learning methods do not require a quality metric, they still require a reference model from which to learn, which is often a large, pre-trained language model such as BERT \citep{chung2019BERT}.

In this paper, we introduce a new approach for fine-tuning large unsupervised language models with human feedback that does not require a reference model. Our approach, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), first fits a reward model to the preference data, and then uses this reward model to find the optimal policy for optimizing the unsupervised LM. Unlike existing methods, which use a reward model learned from human preferences to train a second, fine-tuning LM, DPO directly optimizes the unsupervised LM with the reward model to produce a policy that maximizes the estimated human preferences for the given dataset. We refer to this policy as the \textit{direct preference policy} (DPO policy) because it is optimized to directly maximize the estimated human preferences for a given dataset, rather than following a learned reward function. We refer to existing methods that use a reward model learned with human preferences as \textit{reference-based methods}.

At a high level, our main contribution is a simple, computationally lightweight method for training language models with human feedback called Direct Preference Optimization (D PO). DPO does not require a reference LM or a separate fine-tuning step, and can be trained on the same datasets as existing RLHF methods. We theoretically justify the use of a simple classification loss to optimize the unsupervised model in DPO, and show that the resulting optimization problem is equivalent to a constrained optimization over the optimal policy to a given reward function. We implement DPO as a simple classification loss, trainable on existing preference datasets, and evaluate it on fine-tuning tasks including sentiment modulation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is at least as effective as existing RLHF methods, and in some cases is more effective, including in the ability to control sentiment and response quality in summarization, and in the quality of single-turn dialogue responses. Our code is available at \url{https://github.com/andrewjwilliams/DPO}.
