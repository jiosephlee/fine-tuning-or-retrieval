The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known reinforcement learning approach to reward maximization, known as RLHF (reward learning from examples), to the setting of preference learning. This generalization is achieved by introducing a new objective, called the direct preference optimization (DPO) objective, which is a convex combination of the RLHF objective and a reward function that is learned from human preferences. The DPO objective is shown to be equivalent to the RLHF objective under certain assumptions, and it is also shown to be more effective than the RLHF objective in practice. The DPO objective can be efficiently solved using a gradient-based method, and the resulting policy is shown to generalize well to new data. The DPO objective and its gradient-based solution are implemented in the Pythia language model fine-tuning framework, and extensive experiments demonstrate that the DPO objective can achieve significant improvements over the standard language model fine-tuning objective, while maintaining or surpassing the performance of the human evaluation benchmark.

Introduction
The goal of language model fine-tuning is to train a language model to produce high-quality output on a given task, such as summarization or question answering. In recent years, large-scale pre-trained language models (LMs) have been shown to perform exceptionally well on these tasks, outperforming human experts by large margins (Brown et al., 2020;Liu et al., 2022). However, these pre-trained models are not optimized for the specific task at hand, and must be fine-tuned to produce high-quality output. This process is typically done using a dataset of human-generated examples, where the human preference is used to guide the fine-tuning process. This approach is known as human-in-the-loop (HITL) fine-tuning, and has been shown to yield better results than purely self-supervised fine-tuning (Devlin et al., 2019;Radford et al., 2018;Sutskever et al., 2014).
While the use of human preferences is an effective way to guide the fine-tuned language model, it is time-consuming and expensive to collect large amounts of human preference data. In this work, we introduce a new approach for language model fine-tuning that does not rely on human preferences. Instead, we propose a method for directly optimizing the language model to maximize the likelihood of human preferences. This approach, which we refer to as direct preference optimization (D PO ), is based on a generalization of the reinforcement learning (RL) framework for reward maximization (Sutton and Barto, 2018). In the RL framework, the goal is to learn a policy π that maximizes a reward function R(π, x), where x is a given input and π is the policy being optimized. In the language model fine-tuning setting, the reward function is the likelihood of the human preference P (x, y |θ), where y is the output of the language model π(x) and θ is the parameterization of the language model. The D PO objective has several advantages over the standard language model training objective. First, it is a generalization of reward maximization, which has been successfully applied to a wide range of tasks (Sutton and Bartocci, 2018;Sutton et al., 2017). Second, the D PO objective can be solved using a straightforward gradient-based method, making it easier to implement than the reinforcement learning algorithms typically used for reward maximization. Finally, the D PO objective is equivalent to the RLHF (reward learning with human feedback) objective under certain assumptions (see Section 3.2 for details). This equivalence allows us to leverage the vast body of research on RLHF, including theoretical guarantees and efficient algorithms for solving the RLHF objective.
The D PO objective is shown to be more effective in practice than the standard language model training objectives, such as the self-supervised learning objective (Bai et al., 2023;Radford et al. 2022;Xiong et al., 2021b;Zhang et al., 2024), and the reinforcement learning objective (Sutton and Bartosz, 2018;Wu et al., 2025). In particular, the D PO objective yields a policy that is more likely to produce high-quality outputs, as measured by human preference data. We conduct extensive experiments on multiple datasets and tasks, showing that the D PO objective consistently outperforms the standard language model training methods, including the self-supervised learning and reinforcement learning objectives.
