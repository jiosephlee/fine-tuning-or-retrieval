The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from preference data. This allows the use of language models trained on large corpora, which are typically unavailable for fine-tuning with explicit rewards. The method is based on the observation that the RLHF algorithm can be viewed as optimizing a particular reward function, which is the KL-divergence between the target language model and a reference model. By introducing a regularization term that penalizes this KL-divergence, the RLHF algorithm can instead be viewed as optimizing a different reward function, which is a weighted sum of the preferences of human annotators. This new reward function is derived from a so-called preference model, which is a probabilistic model of the preferences of the human annotators. The preference model is learned from the same preference dataset used to train the language model, and it is assumed to be a simple linear model. The paper presents theoretical guarantees for the optimality of the reward function derived from the preference model, and empirically demonstrates that the proposed approach can yield language models with comparable or better performance than traditional RLHF methods, while being much easier to implement.

Related Work
This section reviews the related work on learning from human preferences, and the methods for aligning language models to these preferences. We begin with the general problem of learning from human preferences, before discussing the specific problem of aligning language model outputs with human preferences.

Learning from Human Preferences
The problem of learning from human preference data has been extensively studied in the context of supervised learning, with the goal of building systems that can make decisions based on human preferences. In the language domain, this problem has been explored in several contexts, including the development of automated summarization systems [1,2], question answering systems [3,4], and dialogue systems [5,6]. In each of these scenarios, the goal is to build a model that can generate responses that are aligned with human preferences, as measured by a preference dataset. In the case of summarization, the preference dataset consists of human-generated summaries of a set of source documents, and the goal is to build an algorithm that can produce summaries that are preferred by humans. In the case of question answering, the preference dataset consists only of human preferences, and the goal is for the model to produce answers that are preferred by humans over those produced by a baseline system. In the case of dialogue, the preference dataset consists solely of human preferences, and it is the model's task to produce responses that are preferred by humans.
In all of these scenarios, the preference dataset is typically collected using a crowdsourcing platform, such as Amazon's Mechanical Turk [7]. In the case of summarizing, the preference dataset is generated by asking human annotators to rate a collection of summary snippets, and then selecting the summary snippet that is preferred by the majority of the human annotators as the winner. In the case of QA, the preference dataset is created by asking human annotators which of two candidate answers they prefer, and then selecting the preferred answer as the winner. In both cases, the preference dataset is used to train a model that can generate summaries or answers that are preferred by the human annotators. In the case of dialog, the preference dataset is collected by asking human annotators how much they like a sequence of responses, and then selecting the sequence of responses that is preferred by the human annotator as the winner. In all of these scenarios, it is important to note that the preference dataset is generated using human annotators, and therefore the preference model is a model that is trained on human preferences. This is in contrast to the case of learning from human preferences in the context of supervised machine learning, where the preference model is typically a model that is trained using a dataset of human preferences, but is then used to predict the preferences of new, unseen human participants.
A related problem is the task of learning from human preferences without using a preference dataset. In this case, the preference model is typically referred to as a "reward model," and the goal is to learn a reward function that can be used to evaluate the quality of different responses.
