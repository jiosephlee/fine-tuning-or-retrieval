The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from preference data. This allows the use of language models trained on large corpora, which are typically unavailable for fine-tuning with explicit rewards. The method is based on the observation that the optimal policy for the reward function can be expressed as a linear combination of the language model's output, with coefficients determined by the preference data. This allows us to define a new objective, called the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective, which is a convex combination of the KL-divergence from the optimal policy and the log-likelihood of the preference data. We prove that the DPO objective is globally optimal, and that its gradient is analytically computable. Our experiments show that the DPO objective can be efficiently optimized using off-the-shelf reinforcement learning methods, such as REINFORCE, without the need for any hyperparameter tuning. We demonstrate that the DPO objective yields language models that better align with human preferences compared to previous approaches, while also being more robust to model overfitting and data corruption.

Introduction
The ability to generate fluent, human-like text is a key capability for many applications, ranging from virtual assistants to storytelling. In recent years, large language models (LMs) have achieved impressive results in tasks such as machine translation (Peters et al., 2018;Lample et al., 2019a;Bosch et al., 2017;Sutskever et al., 2014), summarization (Sutskever et  This work introduces a new objective for training language models that aligns with human preferences, without the need for explicit reward specification. The main idea is to use a language model to generate text, and then compare it to human preferences. The preference data consists of pairs of generated and reference outputs, where the reference is selected based on how well it matches the preferences. The DPO objective is a convex combination of two objectives: the KL-divergence between the generated and reference outputs, and the log-likelihood ratio of the preference data. This approach is inspired by the RLHF algorithm (Korbak et al., 2016), which uses a reward model to train a policy. However, in the RLHF setting, the reward model is explicitly defined, whereas in the DPO setting, the reward model must be inferred from the preference data. We show that the DPO optimization problem is equivalent to a constrained optimization problem over the optimal policy for the unbounded reward function, which can be solved using standard techniques from reinforcement learning.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We present a general framework for training language models to align with human preferences, without relying on explicit reward functions. The framework is based on the RLHF algorithm (Section 2.1), but replaces the reward function with a DPO objective (Section 2.2). The DPO objective is defined as a convex combination of the log-likelihood of preference data and the KL-divergence with the optimal policy (Section 2.3). We prove that the D PO objective is globally optimal, with a gradient that is analytically computible (Section 2.4).
• We introduce a new method for training language models to match human preferences, called DPO, which is based on the DPO objective. DPO uses off-the-shelf reinforcementlearning algorithms, such as REIN-FORCE (Williams, 1992), to optimize the DPO objective. We evaluate DPO on several preference datasets, including the PPO-VAL dataset (Schulman et al., 2015), and show that DPO outperforms previous approaches, including baselines that do not use any explicit reward function (Section 3).
• We conduct extensive experiments on several preference datasets, comparing DPO to several baselines, including baselines that use explicit reward functions (e.g., PPO with a reward model) and baselines that do not rely on any reward function (e.g., random sampling). Our results show that DPO aligns with human preferences more effectively than previous approaches, while also remaining robust to model overfit-ting and data corruption (Section 4).
