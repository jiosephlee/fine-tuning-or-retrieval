\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be rewritten in a way that allows it to be solved exactly using a standard binary cross-entropy loss. This reformulation enables us to develop a new method for fine-tuning language model policies using human preferences, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO directly optimizes the policy that maximizes the reward function learned from human preferences, without relying on sampling from the policy or performing extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments show that DPO is both effective and efficient, outperforming existing methods for fine-tuning LMs with human preferences, including PPO-based RLHF, in tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialog. In particular, DPO achieves comparable or better results than PPO-based RLHF on sentiment analysis and summarization, while requiring only a fraction of the computational resources. Furthermore, DPO is able to surpass PPO-based RLHF when fine-tuning on human preferences for coding and writing, demonstrating that DPO can be used to steer language models to produce high-quality responses even when the human preferences are not explicitly aligned with the desired responses.

\section{Related Work}
Large-scale unsupervised language model pre-training has led to impressive advances in natural language understanding, but the resulting models are often difficult to control and steer. In this work, we focus on methods for fine-tuning these pre-trained models to produce responses that align with human preferences. One approach is to use reinforcement learning to train a language model to maximize a reward function that reflects human preferences. This is the approach taken by \citep{bubeck2023spark}, who use reinforcement learning to train language models to maximize the reward from a reward model that is learned from human preferences. \citeauthor{bubeck2022sparks} also demonstrate that their method can be used to improve the quality of responses produced by the language model, but they do not provide a formal analysis of the optimization process. In contrast, we provide a theoretical analysis of the RLHF objective, showing that it can be solved exactly using a binary cross-entropy loss, enabling the development of a new method for fine tuning language models using human preferences, which is referred to as Direct Preference Optimization (D PO).

Another related line of work focuses on using human preferences to guide the training of language models. \citeauthor{ziegler2022learning} propose a method for fine-tuning a language model to match human preferences for sentiment, using a dataset of human preferences paired with a sentiment lexicon. \citeauthor{zhang2022learning} use a similar approach to fine-tune a language model to match preferences for sentiment, but instead use a preference dataset paired with a sentiment lexicons. \citeauthor{wang2022learning} use human preferences to fine-tune a model to match preferences for topic, but do not use a reward model. \citeauthor{bai2022constitutional} use a preference dataset paired a preference model to fine-tune a sentiment model, but do not use reinforcement learning. \citeauthor{chung2022learning} use reinforcement learning to fine-tune a policy to match human preferences for writing, but do not use human preferences to train the policy. \citeauthor{ouyang2022learning} use supervised learning to fine-tune language models to match human preferences for coding, but do not use RLHF. \citeauthor{tang2022learning} use unsupervised learning to fine-tune models to match human preferences, but do not use reward learning. \citeauthor{joulin2022learning} use learning from human feedback to fine-tune a generative model to match human preferences, without using reinforcement learning. \cite author{yue2022learning} use RL to fine-tune a pre-trained language model to match human preference for sentiment, but do not use the preference model to train the language model. \citeauthor{jain2022learning} use an unsupervised learning method to fine-tune a pretrained language model to match human sentiment preferences, but do not train the language model using reinforcement learning. \textbf{Our work is the first to show that the RLHF optimization problem can be solved exactly using binary cross-entropy, and to demonstrate that this approach can be used to train language models to match human preference for a range of tasks, including sentiment, coding, and writing.}<|endoftext|>
