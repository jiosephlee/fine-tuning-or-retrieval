\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models learn to perform a wide variety of tasks, but achieving precise control of their behaviors is difficult due to the complete unsupervised nature of the training. Existing methods for gaining steerability typically involve collecting human preferences for model generations and fine-tuning the unsupervised LM with reinforcement learning (RL). These methods can be broadly categorized into two classes: those that fit a reward model from human preferences and those that fit a policy and a parameterization of the reward function. We will refer to the first class as \textit{reward model fitting} (RMF) algorithms and the second as \textit{policy-parametrized RL algorithms} (PRA).

RMF algorithms typically involve first fitting a reward model from human feedback, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback, or \textit{RLHF}, and then fine-tuning an unsupervised LM with PPO-style RL to maximize the estimated reward without drifting too much from the original model. PRA algorithms, in contrast, directly optimize a policy to maximize the reward without first fitting a reward model. Examples of PRA algorithms include \textit{PPO} (Schulman et al., 2017), \textit{DPO} (Chen et al., 2020), and \textit{D2D2O} (Liu et al., 2022). While PRA algorithms can be simpler to implement than RMF algorithms, they still require sampling from the LM during training, which can be computationally expensive for large models.

In this paper, we introduce a new PRA algorithm, \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO). DPO fits a reward function parametrization to the preferences of a set of humans, but does not fit a reward model. Instead, DPO directly optimizes a policy to maximize the estimated reward, using a simple classification loss. We show that the optimal reward function parametrization for the classification loss is the same as the optimal reward function parametization for the RL objective used in PRA algorithms, and thus the optimal policy for the DPO objective is the same as the policy learned by PPO. We therefore present DPO, a simple modification of PPO that does not require fitting a reward model. Our experiments show that DPO is as effective as existing PRA algorithms for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences, including PPO-based RLHF (Bai et al., 2023) and a variant of PPO called \textit{P2PPO} (Gao et al., 2024), but is substantially simpler to implement and easier to train.
