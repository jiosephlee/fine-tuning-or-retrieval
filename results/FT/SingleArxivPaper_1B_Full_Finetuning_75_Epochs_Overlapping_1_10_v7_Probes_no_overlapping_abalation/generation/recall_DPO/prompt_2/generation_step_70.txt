The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a general framework for training language models with a single loss function that balances multiple objectives, including human preferences and language accuracy. Our method can be viewed as a generalization of the reinforcement learning (RL) framework introduced by Lai et al. (2018);Liu et al. (2019);Sutskever et al. (2014);Ranzato et al. However, existing RL-based methods optimize a separate reward function for each objective, making them computationally impractical for large models. In contrast, our method jointly optimizes a single loss function that guides the model's behavior while simultaneously satisfying the preferences of a set of human raters. We show that our method can be cast as a particular instance of a more general class of algorithms known as preference-based optimization (PBO; Schmidhuber, 2015;Schmidhuber, 2020). PBO algorithms generally optimize a loss function that satisfies certain constraints with respect to a reward function, much like we do with our preference-based loss function. However, existing PBO algorithms either optimize a custom reward function for each objective (e.g., PPO (Schwendeman et al., 2019);Liu et al., 2020; or else use an implicit reward that implicitly balances all objectives but is difficult to interpret (e.g., the cross-entropy loss used in GPT-3 (Tian et al., 2022)). By contrast, our method uses a reward function that is a natural generalization of the reward function used in the reward learning framework RL-FOR-LM (Lai et al., 2018), which has been used to train high-quality language models (LMs) without explicit reward engineering. Our key insight is that the reward function learned by RL-FOR-LM can be reinterpreted as a proxy for human preferences, and we can use a simple modification of RL-FOR-LM to learn a reward function that directly optimizes for human preferences, without any additional assumptions about the reward function learned by the original RL-FOR-LM algorithm. We call the resulting framework RL-FOR-LMs (RL-FOR-LM), and show that it is equivalent to our method. We further show that RL-FOR-LM is easier to implement than our method, and performs similarly or better for a variety of preference datasets. However, we show empirically that for some tasks, RL-FOR-LM may be preferable due to its ability to directly optimize for human preferences, rather than a proxy reward.
Our other main contribution is a new dataset of human preferences for LMs. We collect 1,000 human judgments of 100,000 sentences from 100 raters on 20 different preferences, covering topics such as political bias, religiosity, and sexual orientation. Each rater provides a score on a scale from 0 to 10 for each of the 100,000 sentences in the dataset, using a custom rating tool. We release the dataset along with code for generating the raters and the dataset, as well as a preference analysis tool called PREFER, which allows users to explore the dataset and perform various analyses. The dataset and tools are available at https://github.com/CSER-LLC/ direct_preference_optimization.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we review existing methods for training LMs with human preferences, focusing on the RL-based methods. In Section 3 we introduce our framework for training LMs with a single loss function, and in Section 4 we present our method, RL-FOR-LMs. In Section 5 we describe how to train LMs with our method using PPO. In Section 6 we describe how to use PREFER to analyze the preferences in the dataset. Finally, in Section 7 we give concluding remarks.
