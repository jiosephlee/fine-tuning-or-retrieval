The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a general framework for aligning models with human preferences, which we call "direct preference optimization" (DPO). DPO uses an objective function that directly measures the discrepancy between a model's output distribution and human preferences, without first fitting a reward model or performing reinforcement learning. We show that the DPO objective is well-posed, can be efficiently optimized using gradient-based methods, and enjoys several desirable properties, including scalability to large models and the absence of local optima. The DPO framework is general and does not rely on any particular model architecture, making it applicable to both transformer-based and recurrent language models. We evaluate the performance of DPO on two preference datasets, showing that models optimized using DPO align with human preferences up to 10.5 points on the CommonCrawl preference dataset and up to 7.5 points on the MovieLens dataset. On the Piazza dataset, which measures preferences in academic context, we find that models optimized using D PO are generally more representative of human preferences compared with existing methods based on reward functions or RL algorithms. Finally, we conduct a large-scale study of the properties of language models, finding that models optimized using D Po are generally more stable and robust to changes in hyperparameters and external stimuli than self-supervised models trained with other methods.
We make the following contributions: • We present a general framework for align ing language models with human preferences, called direct preference optimization (DPO). • Using a novel theoretical analysis, we show that the DPO optimization objective is well-posed and can be efficiently optimized using stochastic gradient descent. • We evaluate the performance of models optimized using DPO on two preference dataset s, showing that models optimized with DPO align with human preference s up to 10. 5 points on the Common Crawl preference dataset and up 7.5 pointson the MovieLens dataset. • We conduct a large-scale study on language model stability and robustness, finding that models optimized with D PO are generally more stable and resistant to perturbations than self-supervised models.

Related Work
Language Models. Self-supervised language models of increasing scale have been trained using various methods, including contrastive learning [57], Transformers [22], and mixed-precision training [36]. These methods share a common architecture consisting of a large multi-layer transformer embedded layer followed by a dense layer for prediction. However, due to the success of these models, there has been significant interest in training language models using only unlabeled data, without an explicit supervision objective. Several methods have been proposed for this "self-supervised" training, including pre-training on datasets such as ImageNet [47] and PASCAL VOC [50], before fine-tuning on the target task. Other methods first train a large language model using an explicit supervision objective on a small dataset, then fine-tune this pre-trained model on the target task. A popular class of self-supervised methods are those based on the contrastive learning objective [57], which learns to compare pairs of samples and assign a weight to each comparison according to how similar the corresponding samples are. This allows the model to focus on the most relevant pairs of samples and ignore less relevant ones, resulting in large increases in performance relative to fine-tuning on the downstream task. For more details on the development of language models, the reader is referred to the excellent survey by Ba et al. (2022).
Preference Modeling. Modeling human preferences is a major challenge in computational social choice, with applications ranging from online advertising to electoral systems. Several approaches have been proposed for modeling human preferences, including reward modeling [13,14,39,40,44,46,49,54], reinforcement learning [8,9,28,47,48,59], and Bayesian networks [23,24,26,47,60]. Reward modeling approaches fit a reward function to a dataset of human preferences, after which the model can be used to predict the preferences of a new, unseen individual. Reinforcement learning methods learn to behave in a way that maximizes a reward function, using an agent-environment interaction. In contrast to reward modeling and reinforcement learning, the preference models based on Bayesian networks are explicitly probabilistic models, but they require assumptions about the underlying generative process, such as linearity and additivity of preferences [23,24,47]. In this paper, we focus on reward modeling and reinforcement learning methods.
Reward Modeling. Several reward modeling approaches have been proposed for language models. Perhaps the earliest is the GPT-NLP framework [39], which uses a neural network to fit a reward function to datasets of human preferences for tasks such as summarization and sentiment analysis. More recently, GPT-4 [13] and BERTstar [44] use a similar framework, where the reward function is learned from a dataset of human preferences for a set of downstream tasks. Like GPT-NLP, these models use a single, global reward function to encode knowledge about multiple tasks, but they differ in the sampling and optimization procedures used to fit the reward function.
