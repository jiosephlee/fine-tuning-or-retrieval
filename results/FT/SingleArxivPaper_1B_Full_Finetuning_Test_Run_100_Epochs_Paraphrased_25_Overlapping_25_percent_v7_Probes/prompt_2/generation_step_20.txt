The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new algorithm for optimizing the reward function used by the language model, which is based on the theory of direct preference optimization. The algorithm is simple to implement and has a provable guarantee of optimality. We evaluate the performance of the algorithm on a variety of datasets and languages, showing that it can improve the performance of language models by up to 10% relative to existing methods.
We also present a new dataset of human preferences for language models, the Human Preferences for Language Models (HPLM) dataset, which contains 1,000 human judgments of the quality of 1,000 different language models. We evaluate the performance and utility of the language models on the HPLM dataset, and find that the language models trained with the DPO algorithm perform better than existing methods on the HPLM task.
The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2 we review the existing methods for training language models with human preferences, and discuss the challenges of aligning the reward function of the language model with human preferences. In Section 3 we introduce the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm for optimizing the reward of the language model, and in Section 4 we present the HPLM dataset and evaluation protocol. In Section 5 we present results on the HPLM and on several other datasets, and in Section 6 we discuss the results and future work. The code for the DPO algorithm and the HPLM dataset is available at https://github.com/leonghaozhang/HPLM.

Related Work
Training language models with human preferences is a relatively new area of research. The first method for training language models with preferences was proposed by Li et al. (2019), who used a reinforcement learning (RL) approach to optimize the reward function of the model. The RL approach requires a reward function that is specified by the human preferences, and the model is trained to maximize the reward. However, the RL approach is not practical for large-scale language modeling tasks, because the reward function must be specified by humans for each training example, which is a time-consuming and error-prone process. In addition, the RL approach does not provide guarantees about the quality of the language model, because the reward function is learned from the data, rather than being specified by the human preferences.
In contrast, the methods for training language models using human preferences that we review in this paper use a different approach. These methods optimize the reward function of a language model using a mathematical optimization algorithm, rather than using RL. The most well-known of these methods is the method of Li et al. (2020), which uses a variant of the policy gradient algorithm to optimize the reward function. The policy gradient algorithm is a standard method for training RL agents, but it is not well-suited for optimizing the reward function of a model, because the reward is not a function of the policy. To address this issue, Li et al. (Li et al. (2021) propose a new algorithm, called the preference gradient algorithm (PGR), which uses the gradient of the reward with respect to the policy to optimize the reward function, rather than the policy itself. The PGR algorithm is similar to the policy gradient algorithm, but it has some important differences. First, the PGR algorithm uses a special form of the reward function, called a reward function with a preference term, which is a function of the policy and a vector of human preferences. Second, the PGR algorithm requires the human preferences to be represented as a probability distribution over actions, rather than as a scalar reward. This is because the PGR algorithm uses the gradient of the preference distribution with respect to the policy, rather than the reward. Finally, the PGR algorithm does not require the reward function to be specified by humans, but instead it uses a neural network to learn the reward function from the data. The PGR algorithm has been shown to be effective for training language models with user preferences, and it is the basis for the DPO algorithm described in this paper.
Another class of methods for training language models from human preferences is based on the theory developed by McCallum (2014). These methods use a generative model to generate human preferences, and then use the preferences to train a language model. The most well-known method in this class is the conditional random field (CRF) model, which was proposed by McCallum (2002). The CRF model is a generative model that uses a graph to represent the dependencies between the words in a sentence. The model is trained to maximize a reward function that measures the similarity of the generated sentences to human preferences. The CRF model has been widely used for training language models with preference data, and it is the foundation for several other methods.
