The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from preference data. This allows the use of language models trained on large corpora to generate human preferences, without the need for any human annotation. We refer to this approach as DPO. DPO is based on a simple yet powerful idea: the preference data can be viewed as a reward function, and the language model is optimized to maximize this reward. We prove that under mild conditions, the optimal policy for the reward function derived from DPO matches the optimal policy derived from the RLHF algorithm. We also demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a wide range of preference learning tasks, including the alignment of language models with human preferences in the context of conversational AI, as well as the alignment of language models to human preferences in the context general preference learning.
The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the problem of aligning language models with preferences, and discuss related approaches. In Section 3, we present the Direct Preference Optimization algorithm, which is the core of our approach. In Section 4, we present experimental results and discuss the limitations of DPO. Finally, in Section 5, we summarize our findings and outline future research directions.

Related Work
Language model fine-tuning. The process of training language models to generate high-quality text is known as language model fine-tuning (LMFT; see, e.g., Li et al. (2018);Biedermann et al. (2019);Peters et al. (2020);Sutskever et al. (2022)). In recent years, large-scale language models (LMs) have been trained on massive datasets such as the Google News and Books datasets (Brown et al., 2018;Peters et al., 2020), and these models have demonstrated remarkable language modeling capabilities. However, these models are not optimized to produce high-quality human-annotated text, and as a result, they often produce output that is difficult for humans to understand or is even nonsensical. To address this issue, a number of approaches have been proposed to fine-tune large-scale language models to produce more coherent and fluent output. These approaches typically involve using human-generated feedback to guide the LM training process, resulting in models that are more aligned with human preferences. One common approach is to use a pre-trained LM as a starting point, and then fine-tune it using a dataset of human-generated feedback (see, e.g., Li and Burges (2020);Radford et al. (2021);Biedermann et Al. (2022);Gpt3-Paraphrase;Sutskever et Al. (2023)). Another approach is to train a new model from scratch using a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm, where the reward is based on human preferences (see, e.g. , GPT-4 (GPT-4; Paszke et al. (2023)), and GPT-J (GPT-J; Williams et al. (2024)). In both cases, the goal is to train a model that produces output that is similar to the reference model, while still maintaining the flexibility and performance of the original model. This process is often referred to as language model fine-tunning, or language model fine-tuning.

In RLHF, the reward function is replaced by a reward model, which is a probability distribution over possible rewards. The RLHF algorithm trains the policy to maximize the expected reward, while ensuring that the policy does not diverge from the reference policy.
