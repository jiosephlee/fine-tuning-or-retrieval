\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be optimized exactly with the simple binary cross-entropy loss, greatly simplifying the RLHF pipeline. We introduce a new parameterization for the reward model that enables us to extract the corresponding optimal policy in a closed form, allowing us solve the standard RLHF optimization problem with only a simple binary cross-entropylearned objective. Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is simple, stable, and computationally lightweight. DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with human feedback to align with human preferences, and we show that it outperforms existing methods in several tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments also show that DPO can be used in combination with reinforcement learning to improve the quality of responses produced by the language model, and that it can be used to improve the quality of the responses produced by a language model trained with reinforcement learning alone.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

\subsection{Language Models}
Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years, with the advent of GPT-3~\citep{brown2020language} and BERT~\citep{devlin2018bert} being two of the most prominent examples. These models have been shown to have impressive capabilities, including the ability to generate coherent and fluent text, and to perform a variety of tasks, including question answering, summarization, and dialogue generation. However, these models are not trained to produce responses that are aligned with human preferences, and so they are not suitable for use in applications where the model's responses must be aligned with human preferences. In this paper, we focus on the task of aligning language models with human preferences, and in particular, aligning language models with the preferences of humans who are not involved in the training process.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}
Reinforcement learning (RL) is a class of machine learning algorithms that have been used to train agents to perform tasks in an environment by maximizing the cumulative reward received over time. The most common RL algorithms are \textit{policy gradient} methods, which optimize a policy that maximizes a reward function, and \textit{value gradient} methods, which maximize a value function that represents the expected cumulative reward received over time by following a policy. The most common policy gradient methods are \textit{reinforcement learning from human feedback} (RLHF) and \textit{replay-based reinforcement learning} (RRL). RLHF methods fit a policy to a dataset of human demonstrations of high-quality responses, and then use reinforcement learning to optimize a language model to produce responses that are assigned high reward without drifting too far away from the original policy. RRL methods fit a reward function to a dataset of human responses, and then use a value function to optimize a language model's policy to maximize the reward function. While RLHF methods have been shown to produce models with impressive conversational abilities, the RLHF objective is complex, involving training multiple LAs and sampling from the policy in the loop of learning, and so is computationally expensive. In contrast, D PO is simple, stable, computationally lightweight, and can be used to align language models with human preferences without requiring any sampling from the language model policy.

\subsection{Human Preferences}
Human preferences are a rich source of information about the types of responses that humans find safe and useful. In this paper, our focus is on the task of align-ing language models with human preferences. In particular, we are interested in aligning language models with preferences that humans hold about the types of responses they find safe and useful. While there are many ways to measure human preferences, we focus on the \textit{human preference metric} (HPM), which is a metric that measures the similarity between a human's preferences and the preferences of a language model. The HPM is defined as the cosine similarity between the human preferences and the language model's preferences, normalized by the length of the human preferences. The HPM is a useful metric because it is easy to compute, and because it is invariant to the choice of human preferences. In this work, we focus on the HPM, but we note that other metrics, such as the \textit{Human Preference Similarity Metric} (HPSM)~\citep{bubeck2023sparking}, can be used to measure the similarity between human preferences and the preferences of language models.
