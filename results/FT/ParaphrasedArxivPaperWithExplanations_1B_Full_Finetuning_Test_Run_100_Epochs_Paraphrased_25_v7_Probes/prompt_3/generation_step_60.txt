\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the standard RLHF objective can be solved exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for RL training. Our method, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or DPO, directly optimizes the policy that best satisfies the preferences, using a simple binary cross-entropylearned objective. We demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, outperforming or matching the performance of existing methods based on RLHF. Our experiments show that DPO is both effective and efficient, requiring only a few seconds of training per epoch for tasks like summarization and single-turn dialog, compared to hours or days for RLHF. Furthermore, DPO is robust to changes in the number of preference samples, making it suitable for scenarios where preference data is limited or unavailable.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have achieved remarkable results in natural language understanding and generation tasks \citep{radford2019language,radford2022human}, but their wide scope of capabilities also raises concerns about their safety and controllability \citep{mishra-etal-2022-ai}. To address these issues, researchers have developed a variety of techniques for fine-tuning large-scale unsupervised LM models to align with human preferences \citep{stiennon2022learning, bubeck2023spark, touvron2023illuminata, korbak2023learning}. These methods typically begin with a pre-trained large-scale LM and apply a supervised fine-tuning step to encourage the model to produce responses that are both high-quality and in line with human preferences. One common approach is to use a dataset of human preferences paired with responses from a reference model, and then train a model to maximize the similarity between the preferences and the responses \citep{stiennkon2022learning, bueckert2022learning, touvron2023illinata}. Another approach is to use reinforcement learning to train a reward model from the preferences and then use RL algorithms to train a policy that maximizes this reward \citep{christian2017deep,bai2022constitutional}. Although these approaches yield models that are more controllable and safe, they typically involve training multiple language models and performing extensive sampling from the policy during training, which can be computationally expensive.

A related line of work focuses on leveraging the power of reinforcement learning to train language models to meet specific objectives, rather than relying on human preferences. In \textit{reward modeling}, the goal is to learn a reward function that reflects the desired human behavior, and then train a policy to maximize this reward. This approach has been applied to various language-related tasks, such as coding \citep{ouYang2022Learning}, summarization \citep{yu2022learning}, and dialogue \citep{meng2022learning}. In contrast to the preference-based methods described above, reward modeling methods do not require access to a dataset of human preference pairs. Instead, the reward model is learned from the data itself, and the policy is optimized to maximize the learned reward. The most prominent method for learning reward models is \textit{Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences} (RLHF/RLAI; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2018reinforcement}), which trains a policy to maximize a reward function derived from human preferences. In RLHF, the preference dataset consists of pairs of model outputs and human labels indicating which output is preferred. The RLHF objective is to learn a policy that maximizes a reward function defined as the negative log-likelihood of the preference dataset, plus a regularization term that discourages the policy from diverging too far from the reference model. Although RLHF is effective at training policies to meet specific objectives, it is computationally intensive, requiring the use of reinforcement learning algorithms to train multiple language models and perform extensive sampling from the policy.

In this work, we demonstrate that the standard RLHF framework can be solved exactly using only a binary cross-entropy loss. This result is significant because it allows us to train a policy to maximize the reward function learned from the preference dataset without relying on reinforcement learning. Our method, which is based on the implicit reward framework \citep{levine2018learning}, is referred to as \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO). DPO is straightforward to implement and does not require any additional hyperparameter tuning or sampling from the policy during fine-tuning. We evaluate DPO on a range of language tasks, including sentiment control and summarization, and demonstrate that it matches or surpasses the performance of existing methods that use reinforcement learning to learn reward functions from preference data.<|endoftext|>
