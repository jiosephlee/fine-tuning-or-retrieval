\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be solved exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for RL training. Our method, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or DPO, directly optimizes the policy that best satisfies the preferences, using a simple binary cross-entropylearned objective. We demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, outperforming or matching the performance of existing methods based on RLHF. Our experiments show that DPO is both effective and efficient, requiring only a few seconds of training per epoch for tasks like summarization and single-turn dialog, compared to hours or days for RLHF. Furthermore, DPO is robust to changes in the number of preference samples, making it suitable for scenarios where preference data is limited or unavailable.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have achieved remarkable results in natural language understanding and generation tasks \citep{radford2019language,radford2022human}, but their wide scope of capabilities also raises concerns about their safety and controllability \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,ouyang2022training,thoppilan2022learning}. To address these challenges, researchers have developed a variety of techniques for fine-tuning language model weights to align with human preferences, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{christian2017deep,bai2022constitutional}. These methods first train a reward model to reflect human preferences for various language tasks, and then use reinforcement learning to train a language model to maximize the reward without drifting too far away from the original model. One of the earliest approaches was \textit{RL with human feedback} (RLHF; \citep{christiano2017deep}), which trains a language model to maximize a reward function derived from human preferences, using a single human preference dataset. More recently, \textit{RL from preferences} (RfP; \citep{ou Yang et al. 2022a,b}) has been developed, which uses a single preference dataset to train a policy that maximizes a reward function derived from the preferences, without relying on a separate reward model. RfP has been shown to yield language models with comparable or superior performance to RLHF, while being easier to train and less computationally intensive \citep{ouYang2022generalized, bai2022constitutional}. However, RfP still requires the use of a separate preference dataset, which can be challenging to collect and annotate, especially for complex tasks like coding \citep{bai2022constitutional}.

Beyond the preference learning stage, there is a growing body of research on leveraging language models for specific tasks, such as summarization \citep{gpt3,sanh2022multitask}, dialogue \citep{gPT2}, and coding \citep{ziegler2022learning}. In summarization, the goal is to generate a summary that is both informative and concise, while in coding, the objective is to produce code that is both clear and efficient. Previous work has shown that language models can effectively summarize or code, but the quality of these outputs is often limited by the quality of the training data \citep{ziegliger2022learning}. To improve the quality of these outputs, several methods have been proposed, including using human-generated reviews to guide the model’s output \citep{ziegelberger2022learning}, utilizing a human coding dataset to train a language model for coding \citep{ziegler2022learning}, and fine-tuning language models on coding datasets \citep{yan2022learning}. In contrast to these methods, which rely on supervised learning from coding datasets, we demonstrate that it is possible to directly optimize the language model to achieve coding quality, without relying on any additional coding datasets.

In this work, we focus on the task of fine-tuning language models to align with human preferences for specific tasks, such that the model’s responses are safe and useful. Previous approaches to this problem have focused on supervised learning from human preferences, where a dataset of human preferences is used to train a reward model that reflects human judgments of the quality of different responses. The most straightforward approach to this problem is to use supervised learning to train a reward model from human preferences, and then use reinforcement-learning methods to train a language model policy to maximize the reward. This approach is known as \textit{reinforcement learning from preferences} (RLPF; \citep{mushal2018reinforcement, christiano2017deep, bai2022constitutionality}). RLPF has been shown to yield models with comparable or superior quality to those trained with supervised learning, but it requires the use of a reward model, which is typically trained using a preference dataset.
