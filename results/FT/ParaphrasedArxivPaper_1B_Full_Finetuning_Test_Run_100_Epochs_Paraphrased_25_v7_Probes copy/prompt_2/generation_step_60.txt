The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a direct preference optimization (DPO) algorithm that uses a reward model to learn a policy that maximizes the likelihood of human preferences while being easy to train and optimize. DPO is based on a simple idea: instead of optimizing the reward function, we can optimize the policy using a gradient-based method, such as REINFORCE, but with a special reward model that is easier to train and optimize. We call this reward model a "secret reward" model, because it is not explicitly defined in the objective. Experimental results show that DPO can achieve comparable or better performance than existing methods, such as PPO, on a variety of preference learning tasks, including summarization, dialogue, and translation, using much simpler training procedures. In particular, DPO requires only a few hundred hours of human feedback to train a model that matches or outperforms current state-of-the-art methods on these tasks.

Introduction
Language models are widely used in various applications, such as translation, summarization, and dialogue. However, current language models are trained using reinforcement learning (RL), which requires large amounts of human feedback to ensure that the language model does not diverge from human preferences. This process is time-consuming and expensive, and it is unclear how to generalize from a small number of human preferences to a large collection of preferences. In this work, we introduce a new approach for training language models using reinforcement learning that does not require extensive human feedback. Our method, called direct preference optimization (D PO), uses a reward model to train a policy that aligns with human preferences, without explicitly defining the reward function. This is possible because the reward model can be learned from the same data as the policy, using a straightforward optimization procedure. We refer to this reward model as a "secret reward" because it is not explicitly specified in the objective. Our experiments show that D PO can achieve comparable or better results than current approaches, such as PPO (Williams et al., 2014), on a variety of preference tasks, using much simpler training methods.
The main contribution of this work is the development of D PO, a method for training language models using RL that does not require extensive feedback from humans. D PO uses a reward model that is learned from the same data used to train the policy, and it is trained using a gradient-based method such as REINFORCE (Bongioanni and Hohne, 1996). This approach is referred to as direct preference optimization (D Po), and it is the core of our method. D PO is a generalization of the standard RL approach of optimizing the reward function using REINFORCE (Bai et al., 2010), which assumes that the reward function is known. In contrast, D PO trains a policy using a reward model that is derived from the same data as that used to train the policy. This allows us to use a standard RL algorithm, such as REIN-FORCE, to train the policy, but with a reward model that is easier and faster to train. We refer to this approach as secret preference optimization (SPO), and we demonstrate that it can yield comparable or better results than PPO on a range of preference learning tasks, using much simpler methods for training the reward model.

In Section 3, we present the D PO algorithm, which is the core of our approach. In Section 4, we describe the experimental setup and present results. We conclude in Section 5.

Related Work
Language models are typically trained using reinforcement learning (RLL) algorithms, such as REIN-FORCE (Bai et al. (2010)), to maximize the likelihood of observed human preferences. These algorithms optimize a reward function that reflects the human preferences, but the reward function is not explicitly defined. Instead, the reward function is learned from the same dataset used to train the language model. This approach is referred to generically as reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). RLHF has been applied to a wide range of language-related tasks, including machine translation (Koehn et al., 2004;Liu et al., 2018;Liu and Lample (2018)), summarization (Koehn and Lample (2017); Lample et al. (2018)), and dialogue (Wu et al., 2019;Liu et al. (2020)).
