Why Preserve Angles?

Neurons in a neural network are like vectors in a high-dimensional space. The angle between two neurons tells us something about their relationship: if two neurons point in the same direction, they represent similar information; if they point in opposite directions, they represent complementary information; if they're perpendicular, they're independent. These angles encode a lot of the semantic structure of the model.

If we change the magnitudes (lengths) of neurons, we can scale their outputs, but we don't change their directions. That's why most finetuning methods only update neuron weights while keeping their norms fixed (e.g., weight decay). But changing neuron directions—changing their angles—can alter the model's semantics in fundamental ways. For example, rotating all neurons by the same angle doesn't change the model's behavior at all. So, if we want to make small, targeted changes to a pretrained model without breaking its general capabilities, it makes sense to keep the angles between neurons unchanged.

How Orthogonal Transformations Work

An orthogonal transformation is a linear mapping that preserves pairwise angles. A simple example is a rotation in 2D:

In this picture, the red vector is rotated into the blue vector. Their lengths (magnitudes) are preserved, and the angle between them is unchanged. More generally, an orthogonal transformation in $d$ dimensions is a linear map $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ such that $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, where $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix. This condition ensures that $\mathbf{r}_i^\top\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{r}_j^\top\mathbf x$ for any two columns $\mathbf{r} _i,\mathbf{r}_ j$ of $\mathbf{R}$ and any input $\mathbf{x}$. In other words, each column of $\mathbf{ R}$ is a unit vector (norm 1), and the inner product between any two columns is zero (orthogonality).

OFT (Orthogonal Finetuing)

The basic idea of OFT is to replace the standard weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ with $\mathbf{R}(t)\mathbf{W} ^0$, where $\mathbf{R }(t)$ is an orthogonal matrix that depends on a learnable parameter $t$ and is initialized as the identity. During training, we optimize $t$ (and possibly some additional parameters) to adapt the model to a new task while keeping $\mathbf{R }$ orthogonal at every step. To enforce orthogonality, the paper uses Cayley parametrization: $\mathbf{R=(I-Q)(I+Q)^{-1}}$ with $\mathbf Q$ skew-symmetric ($\mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^\top$). This guarantees that $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal and differentiable. The forward pass becomes:

$$
\mathbf{z}=\big(\mathbf{R}( t)\mathbf{W }^0\big)^\top \mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{W} (t))^\top\mathbf {x},~~~\text{s.t.}~\mathbf{R}(0)=\mathbf{I},~\mathbf{ R}^\top(t)\mathbf{R}(n)=\mathbf{R }(n)\mathbf{R} ^\top(t)=\mathbf{ I}.
$$

The key constraint is that $\mathbf{ R}(t)$ must be orthogonal for all $t$. This enforces preservation of pairwise neuron angles.
