# Why Orthogonal Finetuning Works

Orthogonal Finetuning ( OF ) is a parameter-efficient finetuning technique that keeps the weight matrix orthogonal during finetuning. The paper argues that OF is effective because it preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, which are important for neural network generalization. To understand why preserving neuron angles is important, let's look at a simple example.

Suppose we have two neurons $w_1$ and $w_2$ with weights $w_{1,1},\dots,w_{1,n}$ and $w_{2,1},\dots w_{2,n}$ respectively. The output of these neurons on input $x$ is given by:

\[
z_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n w_{1,i} x_i,\quad z_2 = \sum_{i = 1}^n w_2,i x_i.
\]

The inner product of these neurons is:

\[
\langle w_1, w_2 \rangle = \sum_{i,j} w_{1,i} w_{2,j} \underbrace{(\mathbf{A}^T)_{ij}}_{\text{pairwise angle}},
\]

where $\mathbf{A}$ is the angular matrix whose entries are pairwise angles between the columns of $\mathbf{W}$. If we change the weights of $w_1$ or $w_2$, the inner product changes. However, if we keep the angular matrix $\mathbf{A} = \text{angle}(w_1, w_1), \forall i \neq j$ fixed, then the inner product remains unchanged. This is exactly what OF does: it updates the weights so that the angular matrix remains constant. The paper argues that this preservation of neuron angles is important for generalization because it maintains the semantic relationships among neurons.

# Butterfly Factorization

Butterfly factorization is a way to represent a dense matrix as a product of several sparse matrices. The paper uses the radix-2 Cooley-Tukey algorithm to construct a butterfly matrix. The radix-2 Cooley–Tukey algorithm recursively decomposes an $N$-point discrete Fourier transform into two $\frac{N}{2}$-point transforms. This leads to a butterfly matrix of the form:

\[
\mathbf{B}_N = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{B}_{\frac{N}{2}}(\omega_1) & \mathbf{0} \\
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{B}_{N/2}(\omega_2)
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{B}^F_{\frac{N}{4}}(\omega_3) & \mathbf{\tilde{B}}^F_{\frac{n}{4}}(\omega3) \\
    \mathbf{\tildep{B}}^F_\frac{N}{4}(\omega_3) &
    \mathbf{D}^F_{\tfrac{N}{4}}
\end{bmatrix},
\]

where $\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\mathbf{D}^\mathbf{F}_\frac{N}{r}) = \mathbf{w}^{\frac{2\pi i k}{N}}, \forall k$ and $\mathbf{B}^\mathbf{R}_\frac{N-r}{2}(\omega)$ is a block-diagonal matrix with blocks of size $r$. The paper uses this butterfly matrix to parameterize an orthogonal matrix by ensuring each factor is orthogonal. For example, the paper constructs an orthogonal matrix as:

\[
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \ni \mathbf{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{d,i}(c_i),
\]

where $\tilde{\mathbf B}^b_{d,i}$ is a butterfly matrix with block size $b$ and depth $d$. The paper shows that when $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, $\mathbf R(m,b)$ can represent any orthogonal matrix. The paper also shows that when $m$ is fixed and $b$ increases, the number of trainable parameters decreases. This is the key idea behind BOFT: use butterfly factorization to reduce trainable parameters while maintaining orthogonality.
