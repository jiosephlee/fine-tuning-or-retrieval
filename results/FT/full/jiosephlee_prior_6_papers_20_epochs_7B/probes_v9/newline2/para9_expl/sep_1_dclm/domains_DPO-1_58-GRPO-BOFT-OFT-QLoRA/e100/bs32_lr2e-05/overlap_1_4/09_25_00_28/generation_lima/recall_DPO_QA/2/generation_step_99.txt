What is DPO?<|EOT|>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; "Direct Preference Optimization: your language model is secretly a reward model") is a method for training language models to generate text that matches human preferences, without the need for explicit reward models or reinforcement learning. Instead of using a learned reward model, DPO directly optimizes the language model parameters to match human preferences, by maximizing the likelihood of human preference data. This is done by treating the preference data as samples from an implicit reward model, and then applying the reparameterization trick to convert the optimization into a maximum likelihood problem. DPO can be seen as a generalization of the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) framework, which is commonly used to train language models, to the case where the true data distribution is unknown and only preference data is available.

1. Human preference data: DPO takes as input a dataset of human preferences over generated texts. Each preference example is a pair of texts $x$ and $y$, where a human has indicated that $y$ is preferred over $x$. The dataset is assumed to follow the Plackett-Luce (PL) model, which is a generalization of the Bradley-Terry model to cases where there are more than two options. The PL model assumes that the probability of a human preferring $y$ over $x$ is given by:

   $$
   p(\text{human prefers } y \text{ over } x) = \frac{\pi(y)^\gamma \pi(x)^{1-\gamma}}{\pi(x)^\gamma + \pi(y)^{1-\gamma}},
   $$

   where $\pi(x)$ denotes the probability assigned by the language model to text $x$, and $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ is a parameter that controls the strength of the preference. When $\gamma = 1$, the PL model reduces to the Bradley-Terry model. The PL model can be interpreted as capturing a notion of comparative ranking: it captures the idea that humans can express preferences between pairs of texts, but may not be able to assign absolute scores to individual texts.

This assumption is reasonable in the context of language modeling, because it is known that language models can be used to generate text that matches observed preferences, as shown in previous work such as "Learning to Write by Reading: The Surprising Power of Relative Rewards".
