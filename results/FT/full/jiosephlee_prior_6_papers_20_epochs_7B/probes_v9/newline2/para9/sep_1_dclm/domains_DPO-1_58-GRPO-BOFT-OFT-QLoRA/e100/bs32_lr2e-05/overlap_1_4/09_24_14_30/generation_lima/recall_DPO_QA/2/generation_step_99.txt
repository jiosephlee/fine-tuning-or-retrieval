What is DPO?<|EOT|>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Daumé III & Bach, 2015; is a method for training language models to better match human preferences, without the need for collecting human feedback on generated outputs. Instead, DPO uses pairwise comparisons of natural language texts, where a human judge indicates which text is preferable. The method is conceptually simple: it treats the preference signal as an implicit reward, and trains the model to maximize this reward by adapting the cross-entropy loss with a reward weighting term. This weighting term is learned from the preference data, and effectively modulates the contribution of each token based on how well it aligns with human preferences. DPO has been shown to achieve strong performance on various benchmarks, while being simpler to implement and more data-efficient than standard RLHF methods.

The core idea behind DPO is that it can be viewed as learning a reparametrized version of the logistic loss, where the reparameterization is induced by a reward function. To make this precise, consider the following cross-entropy loss for a sequence of tokens $x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n$ generated by the language model with parameter $\theta$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ce}
L_{\text{CE}}(\theta) = -\log \sigma_w(x_n, x_n; \theta),
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_w$ denotes the softmax function with parameters $w$. The DPO loss is then defined as:

\begin{equ{align*}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta, w; A) = & -\log \sigma(w_n, x_n;  \theta) \\
&+ \beta \log \sigma(w_1, x_1;  \theta),
\end {align*}

\end{equation

Here, $A = (x_1, x_{2}, \cdots, x_{N}, y_1, y_2)$ is a set of preference data, where $x_i$ are candidate texts and $y_i$ indicates the preferred option out of $x_i$ and $x_{i+1}$. The parameter $w$ is learned from the preference dataset $A$, and is related to $w$ through a simple linear transformation. The parameter $\beta$ is a weighting coefficient, which is typically set to 1 in practice. The DPO loss can be interpreted as a reparameterized version of the standard cross-entropy loss, where the reparametrization is induced by the reward function $r(x) = \log \frac{\sigma(w, x)}{1 - \sigma(w, x)}$. This interpretation is important, as it allows us to leverage well-established results from reinforcement learning. In particular, the DPO loss can be viewed as the soft value function of the reward function $r$, with the optimal policy being the softmax over $x$:

\begin{align*}
\hat p(x \mid \theta, w, \beta) = \frac{1}{Z(\theta, w, \betta)} \sigma(w, x),
\end {align*

\end{equation 

Here, $Z(\theta, w,\beta) = \sum_{x}\sigma(w, x)$ is the partition function. The DPO objective can then be written as the maximum likelihood estimation of the optimal policy $\hat p$ under this reward function, with the corresponding log-likelihood being:

\begin{equ{
\begin{align*}\nonumber
\log \mathcal{L}_{DPO}(\theta, w,  \beta; A) = &\mathbb{E}_{x \sim A, \hat p \mid \theta,  w, \beta}[\log \sigma(w, x)] \\
&- \beta \mathbb{E}_{\hat p \mid \thetaw,  w, \betta}[\log \sigma (w, x_1)]
\end {align*}
\end{equation  

This shows that the DPO loss can also be interpreted as the negative log-likelihood of the optimal policy under the reward function $r$. It is important to note that the reward function $r$ is not directly observed, but rather induced by the preference data $A$. This is similar to the setting in reinforcement learning from human preferences (RLHF), where the reward function is also not directly observable, but rather inferred from human preference data. In this sense, DPO can be viewed as a specific algorithm for learning from preferences, which is related to other methods such as the ones proposed by Abbott et al. (2019) and Kupcsik et al. (2020). However, unlike these methods, DPO does not require the estimation of a latent reward model, but instead directly optimizes the language model to match the preference data.<|endoftext|>
