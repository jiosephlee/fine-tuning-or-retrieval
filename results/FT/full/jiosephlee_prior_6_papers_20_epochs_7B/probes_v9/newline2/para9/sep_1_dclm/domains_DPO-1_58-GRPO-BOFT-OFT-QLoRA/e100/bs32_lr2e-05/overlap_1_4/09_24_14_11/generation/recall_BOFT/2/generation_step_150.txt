# Orthogonal Butterfly: A Parameter-Efficient Finetuning Approach

The Orthogonal Butterfly (BOft) approach is a parameter-efficient fineting technique that utilizes butterfly factorization to create a dense orthogonal matrix. This method can achieve comparable or superior outcomes to the standard Orthogonal Finetuing (OFT) while requiring fewer trainable parameters.

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): The BOFT approach is based on the butterfly structure, which is widely used in computer networks to reduce the number of multiplications required for matrix multiplication. The butterfly structure can be represented as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{B}\cdots\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{D}=\text{diag}(\sqrt{\delta_1},\sqrt{\delta_2},\cdots,\sqrt{\delta_{\frac{n}{2}}}),\forall i:\delta_i\in\mathbb{R})
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times \frac{n}{2}}$ is a banded matrix with $1$ on the diagonal and $-1$ on the superdiagonal, and $\mathbf{D}$ is a diagonal matrix containing $\sqrt{\delta_i}$ values. The orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb {R} ^{n\times n}$ can be constructed by recursively multiplying $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{ D}$. To ensure that $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, we apply the Cayley transform, resulting in the following equation:

\begin{equATION}
    \mathbf {R} = \mathbf {D} \mathbf {B}^\top \mathbf {B}\mathbf {D} - \mathbf {I}_n
\end{equATION}

In order to make the matrix $\mathbf{ R}$ trainable, we replace the identity matrix $\mathbf{I}_n$ with a trainable matrix $\mathbf{M}$. This yields the BOFT forward pass:

\begin{equations}
\begin{aligned}
    &\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{x} \\
    &\mathbf y = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{z} \\
    &\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{w}^0\mathbin{\operatorname{Softmax}}(\mathbf{y})
\end{aligned}
\end{equations}

Where $\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ is the pretrained weight matrix, $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb R^{d \times d}$ is the orthogonal matrix produced by BOFT, and $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb  R^n$, $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb   R^d$, and $\hat{\mathbf{ y}}\in\mathbb R^C$ are the input, output, and predicted outputs, respectively. The dimension $d$ is typically chosen to be much smaller than $n$, allowing for significant parameter reduction.
