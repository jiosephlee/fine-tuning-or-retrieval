What is Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)?<|EOT|>Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), as described in the paper, is a parameter-efficient orthogonal transformation method that leverages butterfly factorization. To better understand BOFT, it's helpful to first review OFT and its block-diagonal variant.

## Orthogonal Finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning (from here on referred to as OFT) is a finetuning technique that maintains the angles between neurons by only adjusting their scales during finetuning. This is achieved by applying an orthogonal matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ to transform the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$:

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\bm{W}} = R \bm{W}^
\end{equation}

In order to initialize the orthogonal matrix $R$, the Cayley transform is used:

\begin{equATION}
    R = (I - XX^T)^{-1}(I - YY^T)^{-1}
\end{EQUATION}

Here, $X$ and $Y$ are two matrices of size $d \times n_f$, where $n_f$ is the number of trainable fins. The main drawback of OFT is that the number of parameters is proportional to $d^2$, which can be prohibitively large for high-dimensional models. To mitigate this, a block-diagonal structure is introduced.

## Block-diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning
Block-diagonal OFT divides the orthogonal matrix $R$ into $B$ block-diagonal submatrices, each of size $d \cdot s$:

\begin{EQUATION}
    R = \text{diag}(R_1, R_2, \cdots, R_B) \in \mathbb{
        R}^{d\times d}, \quad \text{s.t.} \; R_i = \text{diag}(
        r_{i, 1}, r_{i, 2}, \cdots, r_{i, ns})
\end{EQUATIONS

This reduces the number of trainable parameters to $d \cdot ns$. However, this block-diagonal structure imposes a constraint that the transformation is applied independently to different blocks, which may not be optimal for all applications. BOFT addresses this limitation by using butterfly factorization to construct a dense orthogonal matrix with fewer parameters than a full dense matrix.

## Orthogonal Butterfly

Butterfly factorization is a method for expressing a dense matrix as a product of several sparse matrices. A general butterfly matrix has the following form:

\begin{equations}
    B = \text{diag }(B_1, B_2, \cdotes, B_m) \in \mathbf{R}^{b\times b},
    \text{s.t. } B_i \in \mathbf {R} ^{b/2 \times b/2}, \forall i
\end{equations}

Where $b$ is the total number of elements in the butterfly matrix. For a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, the number of parameters is $d^2$. If we use butterfly factorization to express the orthogonal matrix, the number becomes $m \cdot b \cdot (\log_b d)$. In other words, the number of parameters can be reduced to $O(d \log d)$.<|endoftext|>
