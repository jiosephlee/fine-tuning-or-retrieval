The key idea is to construct a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying several sparse orthogonal matrices. To better understand this, let's first review some basic definitions.

An orthogonal matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ satisfies $Q^\top Q = Q Q^\top = I$, where $I$ is the identity matrix. This property ensures that the length of vectors is preserved under a rotation or reflection described by $Q$. A special case is the identity matrix $I$, which is its own orthogonal matrix. Another important property is that the determinant of an orthogonal matrix has absolute value 1, meaning it is either 1 or $-1$. A matrix is called skew-symmetric if $A = -A^\top$. It is well-known that the skew-symmetric matrix $A$ satisfies $A + A^\top = 0$, and its diagonal entries are all zero. Furthermore, the eigenvalues of a skew-symmetric matrix are purely imaginary, and the geometric multiplicity of each eigenvalue is at least 2. These eigenvalues are $\pm i\lambda$ for some real number $\lambda$.

The authors propose a butterfly structure, which is inspired by the butterfly graph in computer science. The butterfly graph is a sparse connectivity pattern that allows efficient communication between nodes. The construction of the butterfly graph starts with a complete graph $K_n$ (where every node is connected to every other node) and then removes all edges except those that form a bipartite graph. The resulting graph is called the $n$-dimensional butterfly graph. The key idea is that any node in the $K_n$ graph can be reached from any other node in $O(\log n)$ steps by following the edges in the butterfly graph.
