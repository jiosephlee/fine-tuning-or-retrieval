What is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?<|EOT|>Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a popular reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm that is used to train large language models (LLMs) for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). PPO works by estimating a surrogate objective function called the "critic value", which is then optimized to increase the likelihood of policies that are close to the reference policy, but not too far away. The surrogate objective function used by PPO is defined as:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\text{clip}(\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)]^2  \cdot \mathbb{E}_{q \mid \pi_{\theta}} [\text{critic}(x, y, q)]$$

where $\pi_{\theta}$ is the policy model and $q$ is the output of the critic model. The critic model is trained on data generated from $\pi_{\theta}$. The first term in the objective function is the KL-divergence between the current policy and a clipped version of the log-likelihood of the current policy, which serves to regularize the optimization and prevent the policy from straying too far from the current policy. The second term is the expected reward according to the critic model. The problem with this objective is that it requires computing the reward for every action generated by the policy, which can be computationally expensive for large language models. This is because the policy model generates a sequence of tokens, and the critic model needs to be evaluated for every token in the sequence. For a policy model with parameter $\theta$ generating a sequence of length $T$, the critic model needs to generate $T$ rewards. This can be a significant computational burden, especially for long sequences.

To address this issue, the authors propose Group Relative Policy Optimization ( GRPO), which is a variant of PPO that reduces the computational cost of computing the critic rewards. The key idea behind GRPO is to compute the critic values relative to a group of policies, rather than computing the critic values for each individual policy. Specifically, the authors propose to compute the critic values for each group by comparing the mean reward of the group to the mean reward of a baseline group. This approach reduces the computational cost of evaluating the critic model from $O(T)$ to $O(1)$. The authors also propose a novel baseline for the critic model, which is based on the mean reward of a group of policies that are sampled from a fixed reference policy. The authors show that this baseline is more effective than using the mean reward of the current policy as a baseline. The GRPO algorithm is described below:

1. Initialize the policy model $\pi_{\theta_{init}}$ and the critic model.
2. For each training example $(x, y^*)$:
a. Sample $N$ policies $\pi_{\theta_i}$ from $\pi_{\thetahat}$, where $\thetahat$ is the current policy model and $i$ ranges from 1 to $N$.
b. For each sampled policy $\pi_{\theta_i}$, generate a trajectory by generating a sequence of length T.
c. Evaluate the trajectories generated in step b using the critic model to obtain rewards.
d. Compute the group mean reward for the $N$ trajectories as the baseline for the critic model.

where $y_*$ is the ground truth label, $\varkw$ is a small positive constant, and $\varepsilon_{sample}$ is a variable that is sampled from a uniform distribution in the range $[0, 1]$ at each time step. The term $\varepsilon_{samples}$ is introduced to reduce variance in the optimization. The standard deviation of the log-probability is normalized by dividing by $\sqrt{\text{std}}$. This normalization is necessary to ensure that the KL-divergence is well-behaved.
