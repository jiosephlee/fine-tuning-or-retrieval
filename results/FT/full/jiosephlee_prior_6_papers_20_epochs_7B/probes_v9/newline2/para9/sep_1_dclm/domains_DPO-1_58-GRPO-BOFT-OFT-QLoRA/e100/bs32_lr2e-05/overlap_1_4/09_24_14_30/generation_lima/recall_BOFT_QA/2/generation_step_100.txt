The key idea is to construct a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying several sparse orthogonal matrices. To better understand this, let's first review some basic definitions.

An orthogonal matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ satisfies $Q^\top Q = Q Q^\top = I$, where $I$ is the identity matrix. This property ensures that the length of vectors is preserved under a rotation or reflection described by $Q$. A special case is the identity matrix $I$, which is its own orthogonal matrix. Another important property is that the determinant of an orthogonal matrix has absolute value 1, meaning it is either 1 or $-1$. A matrix is called skew-symmetric if $A = -A^\top$. It is well-known that the skew-symmetric matrix $A$ satisfies $A + A^\top = 0$, and its diagonal entries are all zero. Furthermore, the eigenvalues of a skew-symmetric matrix are purely imaginary, and the geometric multiplicity of each eigenvalue is at least 2. These properties imply that the determinant of a skew-symmetric $d \times d$ matrix vanishes if $d > 1$.

The butterfly structure can be described as follows: given $2^N$ data points, they are arranged in a $2^N \times 1$ vector. Then, the vector is transformed by a sequence of $N$ sparse matrices, each of which has nonzero entries only in two diagonals. These diagonals are determined by a binary number, where the $i$-th bit corresponds to the $2i$-th diagonal for a $2^{i-1} \times 2^{i-1}$ submatrix. The resulting sequence of sparse matrices forms a product that is equivalent to a single dense matrix. This structure is called the butterfly graph, and it is widely used in the FFT algorithm to efficiently compute the discrete Fourier transform.
