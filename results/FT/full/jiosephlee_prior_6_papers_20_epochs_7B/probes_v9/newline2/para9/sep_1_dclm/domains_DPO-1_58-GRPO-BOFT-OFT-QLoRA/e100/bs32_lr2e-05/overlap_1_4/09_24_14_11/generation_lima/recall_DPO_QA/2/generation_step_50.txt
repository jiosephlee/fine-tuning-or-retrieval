What is DPO?<|EOT|>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Daumé III & Bach, 2015;Bai & Gershman, 2019) is a method for training language models to follow human preferences without explicit reward modeling. It does so by directly optimizing the model's parameters to maximize the probability of preferred outputs, given that preferred outputs are more probable than non-preferred ones. This is done using a preference loss function that compares the probability assigned by the model to the preferred output and the non-preferred output. The preference loss is then backpropagated through the model, updating its parameters.

In contrast to traditional approaches such as REINFORCE or PPO, which require the specification of a reward function, DPO only requires access to pairwise human preferences. This makes it particularly suitable for scenarios where a reward function is not readily available, but there is a large set of human preference data. 

To better understand how DPO works, it is helpful to first review the standard RLHF pipeline. In this pipeline, a reward model is trained on a dataset of human preferences, and then used to guide the learning of a language model via reinforcement learning. By contrast, DPO bypasses the need for a separate reward model by directly optimizing the language model to match human preferences. This is possible because, under certain assumptions, the optimal policy derived from a reward model can be expressed as a function of the preference distribution over actions. By maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences, the language model effectively learns the implicit reward function that gave rise to those preferences.

Here is a step-by-step breakdown of the DPO algorithm:

1. Train a reward model on the preference dataset.
2. Use the reward model to derive the optimal policy.
3. Compute the log-likelihood of the preference data under the optimal policy.
4. Minimize the negative log-likelihood with respect to the language model parameters.

This process can be summarized in the following equation:

$L_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l, p) \sim D}\left[\log \sigma(\hat{r}_n(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_n (x, y_l))\right]$

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid, $\hat{r}_n$ is the reward model, and $D$ represents the dataset of human preferences.

A key advantage of DPO is its simplicity. Because it avoids the need for reinforcement learning, it is computationally efficient and can be easily integrated into existing language model training pipelines. Additionally, unlike previous methods that attempt to learn from preference data, DPO does not require the construction of a reward model, which can be challenging when dealing with complex tasks or domains. However, it is important to note that DPO assumes that the preference data is generated by the optimal policy induced by some unknown reward function.
