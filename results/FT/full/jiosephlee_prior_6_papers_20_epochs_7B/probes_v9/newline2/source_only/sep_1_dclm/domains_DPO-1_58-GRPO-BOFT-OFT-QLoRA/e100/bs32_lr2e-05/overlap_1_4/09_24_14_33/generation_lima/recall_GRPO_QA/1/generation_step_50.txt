According to the paper "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models", what is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?<|EOT|>Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPOP) is a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). It was developed by DeepSeekMath team to train their large language models on mathematical tasks. The key idea of GRPO is to optimize the policy based on the performance difference between a group of teammates, rather than comparing the current policy with a baseline which may be too conservative. 

Formally, given a set of K policies  {π_i}^K_{i=1}, the objective function of GRPO is to maximize the sum of the pairwise relative advantages:
```math
L(\theta) = \sum_{i < j} \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(x)\left( r_{ij} | x \right)}{\pi_{i}\left( r_{ij}|x \right)}}_{\textstyle \text{advantage}} - \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\lambda\sum_{i}||\nabber_\theta-\pi_i||_2^2}_{\textstyle \text{scaffold loss}},
```
where $\beta$ and $\lambda$ are two hyperparameters, and $\nabber_\theta$ denotes the soft value function of the current policy $\pi_\theta$. The first term is the advantage, which measures how much better the current policy performs compared to the i-th benchmark policy. The second term is called scaffold loss, which prevents the policy from degenerating. It ensures that the current policy at least as good as the best benchmark policy, i.e., $\nabber_\thetawas defined as:
```math
\nabber_\theta(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_{r \mid s, a \sim \pi_\theta(r \mid s)}\frac{1}{Z(s, a)}\log\frac{\sigma(r+\lambda\nabber_\theta[s, a](s, a); \theta)}{\sigma(r;\theta)},
```
where $\nabber_\tha[s, a](s,a)$ is the value function of state-action pair $(s, a)$ under the value function baseline, and $Z(s, a)$ is the partition function.

The main difference between PPO and GRPO is that in PPO, the advantage is calculated based on the comparison between the current policy and the baseline, while in GRPO, the comparison is made between a group of policies. This makes GRPO more flexible and effective for training mathematical models, because it is often difficult to come up with a good baseline for mathematical tasks. By using a group of benchmarks, the performance of the current policy can be measured more reliably.<|endoftext|>
