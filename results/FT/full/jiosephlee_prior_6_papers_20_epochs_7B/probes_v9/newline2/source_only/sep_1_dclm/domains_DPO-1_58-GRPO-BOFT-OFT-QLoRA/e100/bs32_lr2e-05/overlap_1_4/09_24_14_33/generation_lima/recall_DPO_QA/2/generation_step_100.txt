The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" introduces Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) as an alternative to the usual RLHF pipeline like PPO. What is DPO?<|EOT|>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Schulman et al., 2017) is a method for training language models to satisfy user preferences, without explicit reward modeling. It trains a language model by maximizing the probability of a desired output (the "good" output) given a user-provided context and undesirable output (the "bad" output), while minimizing the probability of the undesirable output, given the same context. This can be formalized as:

$$\max_{p_\theta}(q_\theta(\text{desire} \mid \text{context}, \text{output good}), p_\theta(\text{output bad} \mid \text{T, context}}))$$

where $p_\theta$ is a language model parameterized by $\theta$, and $q$ is the preference model, which is trained on user-provided preferences and outputs a score of how well the specified output satisfies the user. The preference model scores are then backpropagated through time to update the language model parameters.

This approach has several advantages over standard RLHF methods like PPO. First, it does not require specification of a reward function, but rather uses a pre-trained preference model as its source of preference information. Second, it avoids the need for a constraint such as the advantage estimate falling within specified bounds, as used in PPO. However, it does have some limitations. Most notably, it requires access to a pre-trained preference model, and may be less stable than methods like PPO, since it does not enforce any constraints on the language model's training loss.
