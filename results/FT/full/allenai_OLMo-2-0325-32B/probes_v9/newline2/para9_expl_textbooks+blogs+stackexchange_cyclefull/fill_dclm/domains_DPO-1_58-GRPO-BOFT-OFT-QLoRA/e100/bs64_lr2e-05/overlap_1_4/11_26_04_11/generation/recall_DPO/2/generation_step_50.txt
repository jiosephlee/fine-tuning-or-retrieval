The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), instead of the usual RLHF pipeline like PPO. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a simple, sample-efficient, and effective approach for training language models to reflect human preferences. DPO is based on the idea that the optimal policy for a reward model can be expressed as a closed-form function of the reward model, which allows for direct optimization of the reward model rather than the policy. This approach eliminates the need for reinforcement learning and the associated difficulties, such as reward normalization and hyperparameter tuning. DPO is straightforward to implement and does not require any additional software beyond a typical language model training setup. The authors demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses the performance of state-of-the-art preference-based training techniques, including those that employ reinforcement learning, across a variety of tasks and datasets, including summarization, single-turn dialogue, and sentiment generation. Additionally, the authors present a theoretical analysis of DPO, demonstrating that it is equivalent to a variant of the Bradley–Terry model and that it can be interpreted as a form of implicit maximum likelihood estimation for preference data.

The paper begins by outlining the standard RLHF pipeline, which typically involves three stages: 1) collecting preference data from humans, 2) training a reward model to predict these preferences, and 3) using reinforcement learning to optimize a language model to maximize this reward while maintaining diversity and avoiding collapse. The authors argue that this pipeline is unnecessarily complex and introduces several practical difficulties, such as reward scaling, reward model calibration, and the need for careful hyperparameter tuning in reinforcement learning. They propose a simpler alternative: instead of optimizing a policy, one can directly optimize the reward model so that the optimal policy according to that reward model aligns with human preferences. This is possible because the optimal policy for a given reward function is a closed-form function of that reward function, specifically the softmax of the reward with respect to the actions. By parameterizing the reward model as a function of the language model parameters, one can then optimize the language model parameters so that the corresponding reward model satisfies the preference constraints. The authors show that this optimization problem can be solved efficiently using a simple gradient update that involves computing gradients through the softmax operation, which they call the "Gumbel-Softmax Trick." The resulting algorithm, Direct Preference Optimization (or DPO), is conceptually simple: for each pair of candidate completions $y_1, y_2$ for the same prompt $x$, DPO computes the gradient of the loss with respect to the language model parameters by comparing the Gumbel-Softmax sampled rewards for the two completions, scaled by a temperature parameter $\tau$. Intuitively, this gradient update increases the reward (and thus the probability) of preferred completions and decreases the reward of dispreferred ones, while also encouraging the model to assign higher probability to completions that are likely to be preferred in the future (i.e., those with high expected reward under the current policy). The authors also provide a theoretical analysis of DOP, showing that it can be interpreted both as a variant of the Bradley-Terry model and as a form of implicit MLE for preference data. In particular, they show that DPO can be seen as minimizing a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the true preference distribution and the model's predicted preference distribution, regularized by a term that depends on the reward function. This KL-divergence interpretation provides a principled way to choose the temperature parameter $\tau$: larger $\tau$ corresponds to a stronger regularization that keeps the reward function close to the initial reward model, while smaller $\tau$ allows the reward function to diverge more from the initial model in order to better fit the preference data. The authors recommend initializing $\tau$ to a large value (e.g., 1) and gradually decreasing it during training to balance fitting the data and preventing overfitting.
