The paper is about how to train a language model to produce text that is preferred by humans, without having to train a separate reward model. The idea is to use the GPT-4 model to generate pairs of completions for the same prompt, and then train the language model to assign higher probability to the completion that humans prefer. The authors call this method "Direct Preference Optimization" (DPO). They show that DPO can match or exceed the performance of RLHF methods that use a learned reward model, while being simpler and more sample-efficient. Intuition: The paper starts from the observation that a language model trained by maximum likelihood on human-labeled preferences (i.e., a pairwise ranking of completions) implicitly learns a reward function. The reward function is defined up to an additive constant, but that doesn't matter for the KL-regularized objective that is commonly used in RLHF. So instead of training a separate reward model and then using RL to optimize it, we can directly optimize the language model to increase the probability of preferred completions. This is the core idea of DPO: treat the language model as a reward model and optimize it directly with a simple pairwise loss.

Algorithm: The DPO algorithm is straightforward. For each prompt $x$, generate two completions $y_1$ and $y_2$ using the current language model (initialized from a pretrained model). Label the completion that a human prefers as $y_w$ and the other as $y_l$. Then update the model by maximizing the log probability ratio:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{DPO}(x, y_w, y_l) = \log \sigma\!\big(\beta \log P(y_w \mid x) - \beta \log P(y_l \mid x)\big),
$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $\beta$ is a hyperparameter that controls the strength of the preference gradient. In practice, the authors use a one-step Langevin sampling procedure to generate $y_1$~and~$y_2$ from the current model, and they re-scale the loss by the number of comparisons $N$ so that the learning rate can be set independently of $N$.

Why it works: The paper gives a theoretical justification for why DPO can learn the same optimal policy as a KL-regularized reward maximization objective. The key is that the implicit reward function induced by the MLE-trained language model has the right form to be used in the KL-regularized reward objective. Concretely, if you parameterize the reward as $r_\theta(y,x)=\beta\log\frac{P_\theta(y\mid x)}{P_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ (where $P_{\text{ ref}}$ is a reference model), then maximizing the reward subject to a KL constraint is equivalent to minimizing a cross-entropy loss over preferred/unpreferred pairs. DPO implements exactly that cross-entropy loss, so it optimizes the same objective as the standard RLHF pipeline but without the extra reward-model training step.

Importantly, DPO does not require sampling many candidate completions per prompt during training; it only needs two samples per prompt, which makes it practical for large models.
