We introduce direct preference optimization (DPO), a new framework for training language models based on human preferences. In DPO, a reward model is trained to discriminate preferred from non-preferred completions, using only binary preference annotations. The reward model is then used to optimize a language model via reinforcement learning. Remarkably, DPO can be implemented with only minor modifications to standard language modeling objectives and training pipelines. In experiments with GPT-2 and T5 models trained on Flicker URLs, we show that DPO can leverage a small amount of preference data to outperform strong baselines, and that it can also leverage large-scale but weak human preference data (e.g., from Mechanical Turk) to achieve performance comparable to much more expensive strong supervision methods (e.g., from crowd workers with domain expertise).

The main idea of DPO is to train a reward model to predict which of two completions of a prompt is preferred, and then use this reward model to train the language model by maximizing the reward. The reward model is trained on pairs of completions of a prompt, with one completion labeled as preferred, and the language model is trained by maximizing the reward predicted by the reward model. The reward model is a neural network that takes as input the prompt concatenated with the completion and the other completion, and outputs a single scalar. The language model is trained by adding the reward from the reward model to the log probability that the reward model assigns to the preferred completion, and maximizing this value.

The authors claim that DPO can be implemented using only minor modifications to standard training pipelines. The modifications are:

* During training, for each example, generate two completions and use them to train the reward model.
* During inference, generate only one completion and use it to train the language model.

\mathcal{L}_{DPO} = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}}[r(y^+, y^-)] - \mathbb{E}_{{p}_{\theta}}[\log \sigma(r(y^+, y^-))],
