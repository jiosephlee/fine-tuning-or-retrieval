Before diving into the details of QLoRA, let's first review some basics of model training and quantization. In traditional model training, we start with an initial model and update its weights based on the error between predicted outputs and ground truth labels. This process is known as gradient descent, where the gradient of the loss function with respect to the model weights is computed and used to adjust the weights in the direction that decreases the loss. The accuracy of the gradient calculation depends on the precision of the weights and the calculation of the gradient. Higher precision generally leads to more accurate gradient calculations, but also requires more memory to store the weights.

Quantization is a technique used to reduce the memory footprint of models by representing their weights with fewer bits. One common approach is to use fixed-point representation, where each weight is represented as an integer multiplied by a power of two. For example, a 4-bit quantized weight can take on values from -8 to 7 with a step size of 1. To ensure that the quantized weights accurately represent the original floating-point weights, we need to choose an appropriate scale factor and rounding mode. Once the weights are quantized, the forward and backward passes of the model can be performed efficiently using integer arithmetic.

Post-quantization scaling of gradient
------------------------------------

In QLoRA, the gradients are scaled after the weights have been dequantized, but before the backward pass begins. This scaling is done to compensate for the fact that quantization introduces some error into the gradient calculation. By carefully choosing the scale factor, we can minimize this error and improve the stability and convergence of the training process.

To understand why post-quantization scaling is necessary, consider the formula for computing the derivative of a quantized weight with respect to the input:

$$\frac{\partial W}{\partial x} = \text{dequant}\left(\frac{\partial \hat{W}}{\partial x}\right) = \text{de quant}\left(2^b \cdot \text{round}\left(\frac{2^{b-1}}{M}\hat{W}\right)\right)$$

where $W$ is the quantized weight, $\hat{W}$ is the corresponding unquantized weight, $b$ is the number of bits used for quantization, and $M$ is the maximum absolute value of the unquantized weights. The term $\text{dequant}(\cdot)$ denotes the operation of dequantizing a number, i.e., mapping it from the quantized domain to the unquantized domain. The term $\text {round}(\cdot)$ represents rounding to the nearest representable quantized value.

The problem with this formula is that it does not account for the error introduced by the rounding operation. When the unquantized gradient $\frac{\partial \hat W}{\partial x}$ falls exactly halfway between two representable quantized values, the rounding operation will choose one of them uniformly at random, introducing an error of half a step size. Over many iterations of gradient descent, this error can accumulate and cause the optimization to diverge or become unstable.

To address this issue, QLoRA scales the unquantized gradient before rounding, effectively increasing the resolution of the quantized gradient and reducing the likelihood of rounding errors. Specifically, QLoRA uses the following formula to compute the scaled gradient:

$$\frac{\tilde{\partial} W}{\partial x}= \text{dequant} \left(2^b M \cdot \text{ round }\left(\frac{1}{M}\frac{\partial \hat {W}}{\partial x }\right)\right)$$ 

where $\tilde {\partial} W / \partial x$ is the scaled gradient. Note that the scaling factor $2^b M$ ensures that the quantized gradient has the same magnitude as the unquantized gradient, up to a constant factor. After the gradient is quantized, it is dequantized to obtain the final gradient used for the backward pass.
