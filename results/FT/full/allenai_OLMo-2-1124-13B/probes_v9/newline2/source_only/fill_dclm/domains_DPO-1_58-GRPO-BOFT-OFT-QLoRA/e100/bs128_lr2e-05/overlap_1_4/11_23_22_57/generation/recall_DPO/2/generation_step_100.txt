The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), instead of the usual RLHF pipeline like PPO. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a method for training language models to satisfy human preferences, without the need for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). DPO trains language models to maximize the probability of the preferred response in a pair of human-written responses for each dialogue turn, using a differentiable pointer network to select the non-preferred response. DPO is simple to implement, requires no hyperparameter tuning, and can be applied to any pre-trained language model. Moreover, DPO is significantly more sample-efficient than existing RLHF algorithms, allowing it to scale to much larger datasets of human preferences. When evaluated on Ms. Pacman instructions, restaurant reservations, tutorial explanations, and dialogue responsiveness, LMs trained with DPO achieve higher scores than LMs trained with RLHF as well as existing large, unsupervised LMs. Furthermore, DPO LMs are more aligned with human preferences than RLHF LMs, as measured by both automated and human evaluations. Finally, we present results from the largest evaluation of human preferences ever conducted, with over 670,000 human judgments from 3,000 workers, which show that DPO LMs outperform GPT-4, the current state-of-the-art RLHF model, by a significant margin.

The paper also presents a new, larger dataset of human preferences, called Pretrain-to-Pref (P2P), which is constructed by crowdsourcing preferences for the top responses produced by a large, unsupervised language model (in this case, GPT-4) for every turn in a dialogue. The P2P dataset is over 10 times larger than existing datasets of human preferences and covers a wide variety of tasks, including instruction following, tutorial explanations, and restaurant reservations. The paper also introduces a new automated evaluation metric for alignment, called the Preference Satisfaction Index (PSI), which measures the average probability that a model assigns to the preferred response in a human preference dataset. PSI provides a single summary statistic for a model's alignment with human preferences and can be computed for any model and any preference dataset. PSI scores are shown to correlate well with human judgments of model performance and to provide a more fine-grained measure of alignment than existing automated metrics, such as SPaCy's LIME and ANLP's ALICE.

Furthermore, the results show that the gap in performance between DPO and RLHF widens as the complexity of the task increases, with DPO LMs performing much better than RLHF LMs on complex tasks, such as tutorial explanations and dialogue responsiveness.
