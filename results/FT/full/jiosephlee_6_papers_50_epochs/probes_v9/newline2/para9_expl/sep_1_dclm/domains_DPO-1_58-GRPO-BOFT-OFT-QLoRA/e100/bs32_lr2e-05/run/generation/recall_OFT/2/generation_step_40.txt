Introduction

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) have become the de facto standard for image generation, but they are limited by the quality of the generator's latent space. To address this limitation, researchers have developed a variety of techniques that modify the generator's latent space, such as GANs with noise injection [1], GANs with adversarial training [2], and GANs with adversarially trained generators [3]. These methods have been shown to improve the perceptual quality of generated images, but they often change the structure of the generator's latent code, which can lead to undesirable side effects.

Background and Related Work
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are a powerful class of generative models that have been widely used for image generation. A GAN consists of two components: a generator G and a discriminator D. The generator produces samples from a latent code z, where z is typically drawn from a distribution p(z). The discriminator D is trained to distinguish between real samples and generated samples. The generator is trained to minimize the difference between real and generated samples, while the discriminator is trained to maximize the probability that real samples are generated. GANs are typically trained using a standard loss function, such as the cross-entropy loss for classification or the KL divergence for generative modeling. However, GANs are limited by the quality and diversity of the latent code, which can be improved by adding noise to the latent code or by training the generator with adversarial training [7].
Adversarial training is a technique that modifies the generator's latent code by adding noise to the generator's latent code. The generator is trained with a loss that penalizes the difference between real and adversarially generated samples. Adversarial training has been shown to improve both the perceptual quality of images and the diversity of the latent code [8].
