\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reformulation of RLHF that allows us to obtain the optimal policy directly from the reward model. The core idea is to represent the reward model as a classifier that maps a generation to a scalar value representing its quality, and then use a supervised classification loss to find the policy that maximizes the expected reward under the model. This is conceptually similar to the standard RLHF formulation, but with a crucial difference: instead of sampling from the LM and using RL to update the reward model, we directly optimize the policy using a supervised classification loss.

Formally, let $G$ be a generative model that produces sequences of tokens $x_1,\dots,x_T$ sampled from a distribution $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. A reward model $R$ maps each sequence to a scalar value $r(x)$ that reflects its quality. The RLHF objective is to find a policy $\pi_\theta$ parameterized by $\theta$ that maximizes the expected return
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(x_t \mid x_1,\dots,x_{<t})}\left[ \sum_{t=1}^T r(x_t) \right] + \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta(x_t \mid x^{(t-1)})}\left[ \sum_\tau \pi_\theta(x_\tau \mid x^{(T-1)}) \log p_\theta(\tau) \right],
\]
where $p_\theta(\tau)$ is the policy induced by $\pi_\theta$ on the next token. In the standard formulation, we sample from the LM and use RL to update the reward function $R$ so that it better reflects human preferences; this is equivalent to optimizing the policy $\pi_\theta \to \pi_\theta' = \pi_\theta \odot R$.

DPO replaces the sampling step with a supervised classification loss. Let $y^{(t)}$ denote the label assigned by the reward model to the sequence $x_t$, and let $y^{(t-1)}$ denote the previous label. The supervised loss is
\[
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_x \left[ \sum_{i=1}^T \left( y^{(t)}(x_i) - y^{(t-1)(i)}(x_i) \right)^2 \right] + \lambda \sum_{t=2}^T \mathbb{E}[y^{(t)}(z_t) - y^{(T-1)}(z_t)],
\]
where $z_t = x_t \odot x_{<t}$ and $\lambda$ is a small regularization parameter. Intuitively, the loss encourages the policy to assign high-quality labels to recent tokens and low-quality labels to older tokens, so that the reward model assigns higher values to recent tokens and lower values to older tokens.

Why does this work? The key insight is that the supervised loss is a classification loss that can be solved exactly. If we write the reward model as a logistic regression $R(x) = \sigma(W^\top x)$, then the supervised loss becomes
\[
L(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{1}_{\{R(x) > 0\}} - \mathbb{1}_\mathbb{R}(R(x) < 0),
\]
which is equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy between the true labels and the predicted labels. The logistic regression is a linear classifier, so the gradient of the loss with respect to $\hat{\theta}$ is
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{\theta}} = \mathbb{I}_{\{R(x)>0\}} - \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{W}} \mathbb{I}_\mathbb{Q},
\]
where $\mathbb{I}_A$ is the indicator function. The term $\mathbb{I}_{R(x)>0}$ is the gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to $R(x)$, and the term $\frac{\partial}{\ partial \hat{W}}\mathbb{I}_Q$ is the gradient of the cross-entropy with respect to $R$. Thus the gradient is a linear combination of gradients that are orthogonal to the gradient of the log-probability with respect to $R$, which is exactly what we want: the gradient of the supervised loss with respect to $\theta$ is orthogonal to the gradient of $R$ with respect to $\theta$, so the gradient vanishes and the optimization is stable.
