Introduction
In recent years, there has been a growing interest in aligning language models (LMs) with human preferences. This aligning process is often referred to as "alignment" or "alignment engineering". Alignment aims to improve the behavior of LMs by modifying their internal representations so that they better match human preferences. There are many practical challenges in aligning LMs with human preferences, including collecting and annotating preference data, representing preferences as reward models, learning reward models from preference data, and evaluating alignment models. This chapter surveys the main approaches to alignment and explains the key components of alignment pipelines: preference data collection, preference annotation, reward model training, evaluation, and practical considerations such as data quality and model size. We also discuss the trade-offs between alignment accuracy and computational cost, and the role of human-in-the-loop evaluation in practice.

# Preference data collection and annotation
Preference data is the raw material that drives alignment. Preference data consists of human judgments about the quality of text produced by an LLM. The goal of preference data collection is to obtain a large set of human judgments that reflect the diversity of human preferences. Preference data can be collected in several ways: crowdsourcing, human-in-the-loop evaluation, and automated preference generation. Each method has its own strengths and weaknesses.

Crowdsourcing is the most straightforward and widely used approach. Crowdsourcing involves asking humans to rate or comment on text produced by an LML. Crowdsourcing can be done via platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), CrowdFlower, or Google's AI Platform. The main advantage of crowdsourcing is that it is cheap and scalable. However, crowdsourcing has several limitations. First, crowdsourced data is noisy and subject to bias. Second, crowdsourcing is time-consuming and expensive. Third, crowdsourced data is often limited in scale and diversity. To address these limitations, researchers have developed human-in-the-loop evaluation methods. Human-in-the-loop evaluation uses human annotators to label preference data. Human annotators are typically recruited from the same population as the LLM users, which reduces bias. Human annotators can be trained to provide more reliable annotations than crowdsourced workers. However, human annotators are expensive and require careful supervision. Another alternative is automated preference generation. Automated preference generation uses machine learning to generate preference data from LLM outputs. Automated preference generation can be cheaper and faster than human annotation, but it can introduce bias if the model is trained on biased data or if the model is not robust to adversarial inputs. Therefore, preference data collection should be designed to minimize bias and maximize diversity.

Once preference data is collected, it must be annotated. Annotation is the process of labeling preference data with labels that reflect human preferences. Annotation tasks can be simple (e.g., rating items on a scale) or complex (e.g., providing detailed comments). Annotation quality matters: high-quality annotation yields higher alignment quality. Annotation is expensive and time-consuming. Researchers have developed annotation strategies to reduce annotation cost. For example, annotation can be performed by multiple annotators who review each other's annotations, or by annotators who are trained to provide consistent annotations. Another strategy is to use multi-turn annotation where annotators read multiple examples and provide annotations for each example. Multi-turn annotation reduces annotation noise and increases annotation consistency.

Finally, preference data must be cleaned and processed before it can be used for alignment. Preference data is often noisy and contains errors.
