# Motivation: aligning language models with user preferences

Language models are powerful tools for generating text, but they are trained to maximize the likelihood of observed data, not to satisfy human preferences. This mismatch can lead to undesirable consequences: the model may produce outputs that are too similar to the training data, or that are biased toward certain viewpoints. Aligning a language model with human preferences is therefore a central problem in many applications, including text generation, conversational systems, and content moderation.

A naive approach to aligning a language model with preferences is to collect a dataset of human judgments about which outputs are preferred. For example, one could collect pairs of candidate text and a label indicating whether the candidate is preferred or not. However, collecting such preference data is expensive and time-consuming. Moreover, the labels must be carefully constructed so that they reflect the intended preferences rather than idiosyncrasies of the annotators. There is also the practical problem that annotators may disagree about which outputs are preferred, leading to noisy labels.

A more principled approach is to represent preferences as a model that assigns a scalar reward to each output. A simple reward model might be a linear function of the text, where the reward is proportional to the cosine similarity between the text and a reference model. Another possibility is a neural network that maps text embeddings to scores, or a probabilistic model that assigns conditional probabilities to preferred outputs. The key idea is to learn a reward model that reflects the underlying distribution of human preferences, and then use this model to guide optimization of the language model.

There are several reasons why learning a reward model from preference labels is preferable to using human labels directly. First, the reward model can be trained efficiently from a small number of preference examples. Second, the reward model captures the statistical structure of human preferences, which is often richer and more reliable than the idiosyncrasies and disagreements of individual annotators. Third, the reward model can provide a principled objective for optimization: instead of optimizing directly for human preference labels, one can optimize the language model to maximize the expected reward under the reward model.
