\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for fine-tuning a large unsupervised language model (LM) to align with human preferences. The key idea is to use a reward model to estimate the quality of model generations, and then fine-tune the LM to maximize the reward. This is similar to the approach used in reinforcement learning from human feedback, but instead of using human feedback to train a reward model, we directly optimize the reward model to maximize the reward. The resulting algorithm, DPO, is simple and computationally efficient, and we show that it can be used to fine-tune LMs with high performance.

\textbf{Related Work.} Large-scale unsupervised LMs have been shown to learn rich knowledge and reasoning skills, including commonsense reasoning, question answering, and summarization (see, e.g., \cite{liu2022learning,liu2023learning,liu2024learning}). However, obtaining precise control over the behavior of these LMs is challenging due to their completely unsupervised nature. Existing methods for gaining steerability include reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{liu2022reinforcement,liu2023reinforcement,liang2023learning,liang2024learning}, where the human provides feedback on the quality of model generations and the model is fine-tuned to maximize the reward. However, RLHF is a computationally expensive procedure, and often requires significant hyperparameter tuning to achieve satisfactory performance. In addition, the reward model used in RLHF is often learned from human feedback, which may not be representative of the true reward function of the model. In this paper, we propose a new method for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO is based on a simple reward model that is learned from human feedback, and does not require any hyperparameter tuning. We show that DPO can be used to fine tune LMs with high performance, and outperforms existing methods in several benchmarks.

\textbf{Direct Preference Optimization.} DPO is based on the following simple idea. Given a large unsupervised LM, we can estimate the quality of model generation by computing the average reward of all possible model generations. We then use this reward to fine-tune the LM, maximizing the reward. Formally, given a large unsupervised LLM $M$ and a set of human preferences $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}$, we can define the reward function $R(M, \mathcal{P})$ as follows:

\begin{equation}
    R(M, \mathcal P) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}_{m \sim M} \bigg[ \underbrace{r_{\pi}(m)}_{\text{reward of model generation}} \bigg],
\end{equation}

where $r_{\pi}(x)$ is the reward of model generation $x$ generated by the policy $\pi$, and $\mathbb{E}_{x \sim M}[\cdot]$ is the expectation over all possible model generations. The reward function $R(M,\mathcal{P})$, which is computed from the human preferences $\mathcal P$, is a simple way to estimate the quality of a model generation. We can then use the reward function to fine-tune the LLM to maximize the reward. Formally,

    \arg\max_{M} R(M, \mathbb{P}),
\end{equation}
