\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model for the language model, which is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward. This is a common approach in reinforcement learning from human feedback, but it is not straightforward to optimize the reward model in a way that also yields a good policy. In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, allowing for efficient optimization of the reward model using standard methods. We then introduce a simple classification loss to train the reward model, and show that this leads to a stable and performant algorithm for fine-tuning LMs using reinforcement learning. Our experiments show that DPO is competitive with existing methods for fine-tuning LLMs, and outperforms PPO-based RLHF on sentiment alignment and response quality in summarization, single-turn dialogue, and text-to-image synthesis.

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive performance on downstream tasks such as machine translation (Mikolov et al., 2013; Devlin et al., 2019), question answering (Devlin et al., 2020), and summarization (Chen et al., 2021). However, the lack of supervision makes it difficult to control the behavior of these models, especially when it comes to fine-tuning them for downstream tasks. Existing methods for fine-tuning unsupervised LMs typically use reinforcement learning from human feedback to learn a reward function that reflects the preferences of the human annotators. The reward function is then used to optimize the unsupervised LM using standard reinforcement learning algorithms such as proximal policy optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017).

While this approach has been successful in many cases, it is not without challenges. First, the reward function must be carefully designed to reflect the preferences of the human evaluators. This is often done by training a separate reward model from the human preferences, and fine-tuning the unsupervised LM with reinforcement learning to maximize the estimated reward. However, this process is often unstable, and the resulting reward model may not accurately reflect the human preferences. Second, the reward model must be carefully tuned to avoid drifting too far from the initial unsupervised LM. This is often done using a hyperparameter tuning step, which can be computationally expensive and unstable. Third, the reward model must fit a high-dimensional latent space, which can be challenging to optimize using standard methods. Finally, the reward model must also be trained with the same data as the unsupervised LM, which can be computa- tively expensive.

In this paper, we introduce a new approach for fine-tuning unsu- pervised LMs using reinforcement learning, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO is based on the observation that the reward model can be viewed as a latent representation of the preferences of the human raters. We parameterize the reward model as a generative model of the preferences, and then optimize the reward model using standard reinforcement learning algorithms. This leads to a simple and efficient algorithm for fine-tuning the unsu- pervised LM using reinforcement learning. Our key insight is to parameterize the reward model in a different way than existing methods, which leads to a more efficient optimization algorithm. Specifically, we parameterize the reward model using a generative model of latent preferences, which allows us to optimize the reward model using a standard generative adversarial network (GAN) (Goodfellow et al., 2014). This leads to a simple yet effective algorithm for fine-tuning unsuper- vised LMs using reinforcement learning.

We evaluate DPO on several downstream tasks, including sentiment alignment (Chen et al. (2021)), response quality in summarization (Chen and Bansal (2022)), and single-turn dialogue (Chen et al.). Our experiments show that D PO is competitive with existing methods in terms of performance, and is often more stable and efficient to train. In particular, we find that DPO is able to fine-tune LMs with fewer human annotations, and is more robust to the choice of reward model. We also compare DPO to PPO-based RLHF, and find that DPO is more stable and efficient to tune, and yields better performance in downstream tasks.
