\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. It takes a pretrained unsupervised language model and a collection of preference annotations, and optimizes the language model to maximize the reward function defined by the preference annotations. This is a natural extension of reinforcement learning (RL), where the goal is to optimize a reward function to achieve a desired policy. In contrast to existing reward model fine-tuning algorithms, DPO does not require sampling from the language model, and does not require any hyperparameter tuning. We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment classification, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our results show that DPO is able to fine-tune LMs with high performance, and is more stable and efficient than existing methods.

\section{Related Work}

Large unsupervised language models have achieved impressive performance on various tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and machine translation (Devlin et al., 2019;Radford et al., 2018;Brown et al., 2020;Yang et al., 2021). However, obtaining high-quality supervision for LMs is challenging, especially for tasks that require fine-grained control over the model's behavior, such as sentiment classification and summarization. In this section, we review recent work on reward modeling for LMs, and highlight the key challenges in designing effective reward models.

Reward modeling for LMs has been studied in the context of reinforcement learning (RL) (Schulman et al., 2017;Kaelbling et al., 1996;Sutton and Barto, 2018). In RL, the goal is to optimize an agent's behavior by maximizing a reward function that reflects the desirability of different states and actions. In the context of LMs, a common goal is to train a reward model that can be used to generate high-quality generations of text, without supervision. One approach is to use RL to optimize a reward model that can generate high-quality generations of data for downstream tasks (Chen et al., 2016;Liu et al., 2015;Wang et al., 2014;Ribeiro et al., 2013;Zhang et al., 2012;Kulkarni et al., 2011;Mikolov et al., 2010;Kudo et al., 20110;Wu et al., 20181;Wu et al. 2019). Another approach is to use RLHF, which trains a reward model from unlabeled data and then fine-tunes the reward model using human feedback (Hulora et al., 20191;Ghahramani et al., 20182;Kulkarni and Ghahramani, 2018;Kulkarni, 2018;Ghahramany et al., 20183;Kulkarni 2019;Kulkarni \textit{et al}, 2020). RLHF has been shown to be effective for improving the performance of LMs on downstream tasks (Ghahramani and Kulkarni, 2020;Ghahraman et al., 20192;Kulkarni\textit{et al}., 2020;Kulkarni,\textit{et al}., 2021;Kulkarni , 2021;Kulikov and Ghahramani 2021;Kolodny and Ghahramani , 2021;Ghahramanian and Kolodny, 2021;Ghebremeskel and Ghahramani., 2021;Ghisalberti and Ghahramany, 2021;Kuruvilla and Ghahramani. 2021;Korol and Ghahramani ., 2021;Rajpurkar et al., 2022;Kulkarnis et al., 2023), but it is difficult to train effective reward models using RLHF. The main challenge is that the reward model must be able to represent the preferences of a large number of humans, which is often not the case. Additionally, the reward model must be stable, so that the fine-tuned language model does not drift too far from the original unsupervised model. Finally, the reward model must also be efficient, so that it can be trained quickly and with low computational overhead.

In this paper, we introduce a new reward model fine-tuning method, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO is based on the observation that the optimal policy for a reward function can be found in closed form (see Appendix A for details). We can therefore directly optimize the reward function to achieve the desired policy, without having to sample from the language model or perform any hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments show that DPO is effective for fine-tuning LMs to align with preferences, and is more stable, efficient, and performant than existing methods
