\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. It takes a pretrained unsupervised language model and a collection of preference annotations, and optimizes the language model to maximize the reward function defined by the preference annotations. This is a generalization of the existing reward model fine-tuning algorithms, which typically optimize a policy gradient objective with a reward function that is a fixed approximation of the true reward function. The key difference between DPO and existing algorithms is that DPO uses a closed-form expression for the optimal policy, which enables us to solve the standard reward model fine-tuning problem with only a simple loss function.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language model training has been a major focus in natural language processing (NLP) in recent years. Large language models (LLMs) are trained on massive datasets of text and have demonstrated impressive performance in a variety of tasks, including natural language understanding, generation, and translation \citep{devlin-lee2020bert,radford2021learning}. However, LLMs are typically trained in an unsupervised manner, without any explicit human supervision of the generated text. This makes it difficult to control the behavior of LLMs, especially when compared to supervised training where the model is fine-tuned on a task-specific dataset alongside human feedback. In this section, we review the existing methods for gaining steerability in LLMs.

\textbf{Reward-based methods}. One approach to gain steerability is to fit a reward model to the LLM's behavior and then fine-tune the reward model to align with human preferences. This is typically done using reinforcement learning (RL). In reinforcement learning, an agent interacts with an environment to learn a policy that maximizes a reward function defined by the environment. In the context of LLMs, the reward function is typically a function of the LLM's output, and the environment is the text that the LLM generates. The goal is to train an LLM to maximize the reward function without drifting too far from its original behavior. The most common RL method for this problem is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which is a variant of the actor-critic algorithm that uses a proximal operator to regularize the policy gradient. Other methods include \citep{liu2022training,liu2023fine,liu2024fine}, which use reinforcement learning to fine-tune a reward model that is already fit to the LLM's output.

\textbf{Model-based methods}. Another approach to gain steerability in LLM is to fit a model of the LLM's behavior to human preferences and then fine-tune this model to align with the preferences. This is typically achieved using generative adversarial networks (GANs) \citep{goodfellow2014generative}. In GANs, two neural networks are trained to generate samples from two different distributions: a generator network that produces samples that are difficult for a discriminator network to distinguish from real data, and a discriminator network that distinguishes between real data and generated data. The generator network is trained to match the distribution of the real data, and the discriminator network is trained to distinguish between real data and generated samples. The two networks compete against each other, with the goal of training the generator to match the distribution of real data and the discriminator to be unable to distinguish between real data generated by the generator and real data. The resulting model can be used to generate samples that match human preferences, and then the model can be fine-tuned to align with the preferences using reinforcement learning. This approach is used in \citep{zhang2023fine,zhang2024fine}.

\textbf{Supervised methods}. A third approach to gain steerability uses supervised learning to fine-tune the LLM. In supervised learning, a model is trained on a dataset of paired input-output pairs, where the input is the LLM's output and the output is a label indicating the human preference for the output. The goal is to fine-tune the model to maximize the cross-entropy loss between the model's output and the labels. This approach is used by \citep{li2023fine} and \citep{liji2023fine}.
