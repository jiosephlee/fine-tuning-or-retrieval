\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. It takes a pretrained unsupervised language model and a set of preference labels, and optimizes the model parameters to maximize the reward function defined by the preference labels. This is a natural extension of reinforcement learning (RL), where the goal is to optimize a reward function to maximize the cumulative reward of a given policy. In contrast, in DPO, the goal is to optimize the reward function to match the preferences of a given model. 

\textbf{Why is DPO better than existing methods?} Existing methods for gaining steerability include reinforcement learning from human feedback (\textit{RLHF}) and preference alignment (\textit{PA}). 
RLHF trains a reward model from human preferences, and then uses reinforcement learning to optimize the reward model to maximize the human preferences. This is a complex and often unreliable procedure, because the reward model is learned from human preferences, which are themselves learned from the model. PA optimizes a pretrained unsupervised LM to match a given set of preference labels, typically by minimizing the KL divergence between the LM and the preference labels. This approach is simple and effective, but it does not allow us to directly optimize the reward function to maximize the preferences, and it does not provide any guarantees about the stability of the resulting policy.

\textbf{Why does DPO work?} The key insight behind DPO is that the reward function we want to optimize is closely related to the preference labels. Specifically, we can express the reward function as the dot product of the preference labels and the LM parameters. This allows us to rewrite the optimization problem as a standard policy optimization problem, where the goal is to maximize the dot product of the LM parameters and the preference labels. We can then solve this policy optimization problem using techniques from the theory of optimal transport, which provides a general framework for solving problems of this form. This approach has several advantages over existing methods. First, it provides a theoretical guarantee that the resulting policy is optimal, in the sense that it maximizes the dot product of the parameters with the preference labels. Second, it is computationally efficient, because it does not require sampling from the LM or performing significant hyperparameter optimization. Finally, it is simple to implement, because it does not involve any additional training or inference steps.

\textbf{What is the difference between DPO and other methods?} DPO is similar to reinforcement learning from human feedback, but it does not require sampling human preferences or performing significant hyperparameter fine-tuning. Instead, it uses a simple preference classification loss to estimate the reward function, and then optimizes the reward function using standard policy optimization techniques. DPO is also similar to preference alignment, but it does not use any supervised training data, and it does not require any additional hyperparameters. Instead, it uses the preference labels to initialize the LM, and then optimizes a simple preference classification loss. Finally, DPO is different from DPO, which is a method for fine-tuning a pretrained unsupervised LLM to match a given set or preferences. DPO is a general method for optimizing the reward function of a pretrained LLM, and it can be used to fine-tune any LLM, not just those pretrained on large datasets.

\textbf{How does DPO compare to existing methods?} We evaluate DPO on two tasks: sentiment classification and summarization. For sentiment classification, we compare DPO to PPO-based RLHF, which is the most popular method for fine-tuning LLMs to match human preferences. For summarization, we compare DPO and PPO to a baseline that uses reinforcement learning from human feedback to optimize the reward function of the LLM. For both sentiment classification and summarization, we use the same dataset and evaluation protocol as in \cite{li2023learning}. We find that DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF and the reinforcement learning from human feedback baseline, and it is generally more stable and easier to train than PPO-based RLHF. In particular, DPO is much less sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and the number of training steps. We also evaluate DPO on a task that was not considered in \cite{li2019learning}, which is generating coherent responses to questions about the movie Titanic. We find that DPO is able to generate responses that are more coherent and more consistent with the preferences of the human judges than PPO-based RL HF and the reinforcement learning from humans feedback baseline.
