\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. It takes a pretrained unsupervised language model and an unlabeled dataset of preference annotations, and optimizes the language model to maximize the reward function defined by the reward model. The reward model is learned from the preference annotations, and it is optimized to maximize the expected reward of the optimal policy from the pretrained language model. The optimal policy is then used to optimize the pretrained language model.

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised LM (e.g., BERT [1], GPT-3 [2]) have achieved impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and machine translation. However, achieving precise control of the behavior of these models is difficult due to their completely unsupervised nature. Existing methods for gaining steerability include reinforcement learning from human preferences, where the reward model (i.e., the function that maps model outputs to a numerical reward value) is first fit to reflect the preferences of a human annotator, and then the unsatisfied model is fine-tuned using RL to maximize the reward without deviating too far from the original unsupervised model. This approach is computationally intensive, as it requires sampling from the LM and performing reinforcement learning on each sampled generation. Additionally, the reward model must be carefully tuned to avoid drifting too far from the initial unsupervised model, which can be challenging to do in practice.

In this paper, we introduce Direct Preference Optimization (D PO), a new method for fine-tuning unsupervised LMs to align with user preferences. DPO is based on a simple yet powerful idea: the reward model used in RLHF can be interpreted as a parametric family of reward functions, each of which corresponds to a different policy from the pretrained unsupervised LM. We can therefore optimize the reward model to maximize the expected reward from the optimal policy, which corresponds to the original unsupervised LM. This leads to a closed-form solution for the optimal policy, which can be efficiently computed using dynamic programming. We refer to this optimal policy as the \textit{direct preference policy}, and we refer to the reward model as the \textit{(direct preference) optimization model}. The DPO algorithm optimizes the optimization model to maximize the expected value of the reward function, which corresponds to the optimal policy from the original unsupervisd model. The resulting algorithm is simple to implement and computationally efficient, and it does not require sampling from the LM or tuning the reward model. We refer to this algorithm as \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO).

We evaluate DPO on two tasks: sentiment analysis and summarization. For sentiment analysis, we use the dataset of \cite{liu2022learning} and evaluate the generated text on the negative and positive sentiment labels provided by humans. For summarization, we use the dataset from \cite{liu2019learning} and evaluate the summarization quality of the generated text. We compare DPO to several existing methods, including PPO, RLHF, and a variant of RLHF called \textit{RLHF with human feedback (RLHF-HF)} that uses human feedback to tune the reward model. We find that DPO is able achieve comparable or better performance than existing methods, while being significantly simpler to implement and train. In particular, DPO is able to achieve comparable or better performance on sentiment analysis and summarization with only a few hundred training examples per task, while existing methods require tens of thousands of training examples per task.
