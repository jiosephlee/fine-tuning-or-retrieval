1. Introduction

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in using language models (LMs) to generate high-quality text. LMs are trained on large amounts of text data, and their performance can be evaluated using various metrics, such as the perplexity [1], the BLEU score [2], and the CLIP score [3]. These metrics are widely used to evaluate the performance of LMs, and they are based on the assumption that the human preference for a text is equivalent to its quality. However, this assumption is not always true. For example, some people may prefer a text that is biased towards a certain political party, even if the text is not of high quality. Therefore, it is important to develop methods that can take human preferences into account when evaluating the performance of LMs.

One way to do this is to use a reward model to measure the quality of a text. A reward model is a mathematical function that maps a text to a numerical value, which represents the quality of the text. The value of the reward model can then be used to adjust the training objective of the LM, so that the LM produces text that is preferred by the reward model. This approach has been successfully applied to various tasks, such as text generation [4, 5], dialogue [6], and summarization [7].

In this paper, we focus on the task of language alignment, which is the problem of aligning a language model with human preferences. Specifically, we want to find a language model that is as close as possible to the preferences of a group or community of humans. To solve this problem, we propose a new algorithm called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which uses a reward model to measure human preferences. The key idea of DPO is to learn a reward model from human preferences, and then use the learned reward model to optimize the language model.

To learn a reward model from the preferences of humans, we follow the approach proposed in [8]. In particular, we use a group of humans to annotate a reward model that maps a text to its quality. The annotation process involves three steps: (i) collecting a large amount of text data, (ii) training a model to predict the quality of the text, and (iii) fine-tuning the model to maximize the agreement between the predicted quality and the annotations. We evaluate the performance of the reward model learned in this way using two benchmarks: the Common Crawl dataset [9] and the Open Language Modeling dataset [10]. Our results show that the reward models learned in this way are able to accurately measure the quality of text data.

Once we have a reward model, we can use it to optimize the language model, so that it produces text that is preferred. To achieve this goal, we propose a new optimization objective that takes the reward model into account. Specifically, we use the reward model to construct a reward function that measures the difference between the output of the language model and the preferred output. We then use the gradient of this reward function with respect to the parameters of the language model to update the parameters of the language. The resulting optimization objective is a variant of the policy gradient objective, and it is similar to the method proposed in [11]. However, our approach is different in two important ways. First, we use a reward model that is derived from human preferences, instead the standard reward model that is fixed by the researchers. Second, we use a different method for optimizing the reward model, which involves a gradient ascent step with respect to the reward function. We believe that our approach is more effective than the existing methods, and it will have a positive impact in the field of language alignment.
