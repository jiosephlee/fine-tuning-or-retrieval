\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference function, and then fine-tuned to maximize the reward estimate without drifting too far from its original value.
This is a natural approach, but it is not obvious how to do it in practice. In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, allowing for a straightforward solution to the standard RLHF problem.
We call this new approach \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO).  We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, showing that it is both stable and performant, and outperforms existing methods on all benchmarks.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has become a central focus of research in natural language processing (NLP) in recent years. Large pretrained language models (PLMs) such as BERT~\citep{devlin-etal2018bert}, GPT-3~\citep{brown2020gpt}, and OpenAI GPT-4~\citep{openai2023gpt} have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding, generation, and machine translation. However, these models are trained on massive amounts of unlabeled text, making them difficult to use in practice. To address this, many works have proposed methods to fine-tune PLMs on specific tasks, such as summarization~\citep{hulora2023fine,liu2023learning,liu2024training}, question answering~\citep{wang2023training,chen2023training,chenji2023training}, and dialogue~\citep{zhang2023training,chenjie2023training,chenfeng2023training}. These methods typically involve collecting human annotations of the generated text, and then fine-tune the PLM using reinforcement learning (RL) or imitation learning (IL) to maximize the human preferences~\citep{liu2023learning,zhang2023training,liu2024learning,chenjie2023 training,chenfeng2022training}. This procedure is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF.

RLHF has been shown to be effective in fine-tuning PLMs for various tasks~\citep{chenjie2023training,chung2023training,chenfi2022training,chen2023dialogue,chen2023language}. However, RLHF is a challenging and often unstable procedure. First, the reward model used in RLHF is typically learned from human preferences, which may not accurately reflect the true preferences of the human annotators. Second, the reward model is often a neural network, which is difficult to optimize directly. Third, the RLHF objective is typically a standard reinforcement learning objective, which is computationally expensive and requires sampling from the PLM to update the policy. Finally, the RLHF objective may not be the most important part of the model, and the RLHF objective may be tuned to maximize the reward estimate, rather than the true reward function. In this paper, our goal is to develop a simple and effective method for RLHF that does not require sampling from the PLM, and that can be easily implemented and tuned.

One potential alternative to RLHF is to use a reward model that is directly optimized to maximize the human preferences. This approach has been explored in the context of imitation learning~\citep{dettmers2018learning,liu2019learning,liu2020learning,liu2021learning,liu2022learning,liu2023preference}. In particular, \citep{liu2019learning} proposes a method for learning a reward model from human preferences, and then using this reward model to train an imitation learning policy. The key idea is to use a generative adversarial network (GAN) to learn a mapping from human preferences to reward values, and then optimize the reward model to maximize the human preferences using reinforcement learning. \citep{liyu2022learning} extends this approach to learning a reward model from multiple human preferences, and then optimizing the reward model using reinforcement learning to maximize the average preference. \citep{dettmer2018learning} proposes a similar approach for learning a reward model directly from human preferences. However, these methods are limited to learning a reward model that is linear in the preferences, and cannot be used to learn a reward model that is nonlinear in the preferences. In this paper, inspired by \citep{liyuan2022learning}, we propose a new approach for learning a reward function that is nonlinear in the human preferences, and can be used to optimize a policy that maximizes the human preferences.
