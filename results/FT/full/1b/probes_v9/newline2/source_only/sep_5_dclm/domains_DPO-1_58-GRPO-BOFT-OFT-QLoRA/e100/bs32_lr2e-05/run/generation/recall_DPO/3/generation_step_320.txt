\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference function, and then fine-tuned to maximize the reward estimate without drifting too far from its original value.
This is a natural approach, but it is not obvious how to do it in practice. In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, allowing for a straightforward solution to the standard RLHF problem.
We call this new parameterization \textit{Direct Preferences}, and our main contribution is a method for optimizing a reward model with Direct Preferences, called \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO). DPO is a simple and effective alternative to existing methods for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences, and we evaluate it on a range of tasks including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Language Models}. Large-scale language models (LMs), such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) and GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks. However, their ability to reason and make decisions is limited, and their behavior is largely determined by the training data they are exposed to. This makes it difficult to use them for tasks that require fine-grained control over their behavior, such as controlling the sentiment of text or generating coherent responses to questions. 

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback}. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RHLF) is a popular approach for training LMs to perform specific tasks. In RHLF, a human annotates a set of pairs of model and human preferences, and the model is then trained to maximize the human preferences. The most common approach for collecting human preferences is to ask users to rate model generations on a scale from 1 to 5, where 1 is the worst and 5 is the best. This approach has been used to train LMs for sentiment analysis (Huang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021), summarization (Wang et al., 2022), and single-turn dialogue (Chen et al., 2023). While RHLF has been shown to be effective, it is a complex and often unreliable procedure. First, the number of pairs of model and preferences needed to train a high-quality model can be prohibitively large, and the annotation process itself is time-consuming and expensive. Second, the human annotations are noisy, and it is difficult to ensure that the human preferences reflect the true preferences of the users. Third, the human preferences are often inconsistent, and it is difficult for the model to converge to a good solution when the human preferences are noisy or inconsistent. Fourth, the model must be trained to maximize the human preference, which can lead to undesirable side-effects, such as encouraging the model to produce text that is too similar to the preferences. Fifth, the model must be able to generalize to unseen preferences, which can be difficult to achieve. Finally, the model must be stable, which can be difficult in practice. 

\textbf{Tuning Language Models}. Tuning LMs to improve performance on a particular task is a common approach for fine-tuning LLMs. The most common method for tuning LMs is to train a reinforcement learning agent to maximize a reward function that reflects the performance of the model on a given task. The most common reward function is the perplexity of the model's output, which is a measure of how well the model can generate text that is consistent with the training data. Other reward functions have been proposed, such as the cosine similarity between the model's output and the target text (Kulkarni et al., 2024), the average log likelihood of the model's output on a held-out test set (Ghahramani et al., 2025), and the average mutual information between the model's output distribution and the target distribution (Ghahramany et al., 2026). These reward functions have the advantage of being easy to compute, but they can be difficult to optimize, especially for large LMs. Additionally, the reward function must be carefully tuned to reflect the desired performance on the task, which can be difficult. Finally, the model's behavior is often influenced by the reward function, which can lead to undesired side-effects.

One approach is to use reinforcement learning to train a reward model that approximates the desired reward function. The reward model is then used to optimize the original reward function, and the resulting optimization objective is typically a differentiable function of the reward model parameters.
