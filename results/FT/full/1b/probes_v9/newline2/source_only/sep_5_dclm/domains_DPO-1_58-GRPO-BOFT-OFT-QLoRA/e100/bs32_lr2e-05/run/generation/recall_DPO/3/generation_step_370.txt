\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require any sampling from the LM during training, nor does it require any hyperparameter tuning. \section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Unsupervised Language Models}. Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has been a major focus of recent work in natural language processing (see \cite{li2022training,liu2023learning,liu2024unsupervised} for surveys).  The most popular approach to training LMs is training them on large quantities of unlabeled text, typically by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the LM on a large corpus of text.  This approach has been shown to yield powerful language models, but it is completely unsupervised, and so it is unclear how to control the behavior of the resulting model.  In this paper, we focus on the task of controlling the behavior of LLMs, and so we are interested in methods that can be used to steer the behavior of LMs without supervision.

\textbf{Reward Learning}. Reward learning is a general framework for learning to control the behavior of a system, where the goal is to learn a reward function that can be used to control the system.  The most common form of reward learning is reinforcement learning (RL), where the goal is to maximize a reward function over a sequence of states and actions.  RL has been successfully applied to a wide variety of problems, including control, robotics, and games.  However, RL is typically difficult to implement, and so there has been significant interest in alternative approaches to reward learning.  One such approach is to learn a reward model from human feedback, and then use this reward model to control the system. This approach is known as reward model fitting (RMF), and it has been shown to be effective in a wide variety of settings.  For example, RMF has been used to control the behavior of LMs \cite{liu2023learning}, to control the behavior of robotic arms \cite{wang2023reward}, and to control the behavior of video games \cite{zhang2023learning}.  However, RMF is typically unstable, and so it is necessary to tune the reward model parameters to avoid drifting too far from the initial model.  In this work, we propose a new approach to reward learning, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which avoids the need for RMF tuning.

\textbf{Control of Language Models}. There has been significant interest in the problem of controlling the behavior of language models.  One approach to controlling the behavior of LSTMs is to use a neural network to predict the probability of each word in the input sequence, and then use this probability to control the output sequence.  This approach is known as sequence-to-sequence learning, and it has been shown that it is possible to train LSTMs using only a small number of training examples.  However, sequence-to-sequence learning is typically difficult to implement and requires significant computational resources.  In this work we focus on the problem of controlling the output of LLMs, which is a more tractable problem than controlling the output of LSTMs.  There has been significant interest recently in the problem of controlling LLMs, and several methods have been proposed.  These methods include attention-based methods \cite{hulora2019attention,liu2020att,liu2021att}, attention-free methods \cite{liu2019language,liu2020language}, and reinforcement learning methods \cite{li2021reinforcement,liu2022learning,liu2023unsupervised}.  While these methods have been shown to be effective, they typically require significant computational resources and are difficult to implement.  In this work our focus is on methods that can be used with relatively simple implementations and that are easy to implement and deploy.
