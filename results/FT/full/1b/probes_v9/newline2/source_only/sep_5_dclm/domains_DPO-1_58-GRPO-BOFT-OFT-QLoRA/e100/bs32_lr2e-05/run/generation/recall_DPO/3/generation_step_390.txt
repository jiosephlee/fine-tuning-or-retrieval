\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for fine-tuning a language model (LM) to match human preferences. The key insight is to parameterize the reward function of the RL algorithm in a way that allows us to find the optimal policy from the reward function, which is the policy we want to optimize. This is done by reparameterizing the reward function as a linear combination of the LM's parameters, and then finding the optimal policy from the resulting linear function. We refer to this as the \textit{reward-to-parameter} (R-to-P) transformation.

We then use the R-to-P transformation to derive a simple reinforcement learning algorithm for fine-tuning an unsupervised LM to match human preferences. This algorithm, called \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO) is a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which is a widely used reinforcement learning algorithm for fine tuning LMs. DPO is simple to implement and has a small number of hyperparameters, but it is not guaranteed to converge to the optimal policy. To address this, we propose a simple yet effective method for stabilizing DPO, called \textit{safety regularization}. Safety regularization adds a penalty to the loss function that encourages the policy to stay close to the initial policy. We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our results show that DPO is able to align LMs with human preferences as well or better than existing methods, while being substantially simpler to train and faster to run. In particular, we show that DPO can be trained in minutes on a single GPU, while existing methods take hours or days. We also demonstrate that DPO can be used to improve the quality of LMs, outperforming PPO-based RLHF on sentiment analysis and summarization, and matching PPO-based RLHF performance on single-turn dialogue.

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models have recently achieved impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and machine translation (see, e.g., \cite{devlin-lee2019bert,liu2020language,liu2021language}). However, achieving precise control of the behavior of these models is difficult due to their completely unsupervised nature. While it is possible to collect human feedback on the quality of model generations, this is a labor-intensive process that is not scalable to large models. Moreover, even if we could collect enough human feedback, it is unclear how to use it to train a model that is both accurate and controllable. 

To address this, we introduce a new approach for gaining precise control of LMs, called \textit{(Direct) Preference Optimization}. The basic idea is to use human preferences to train a reward model that reflects how humans would like the LM to behave. We then use the reward model to fine-tune the LM to maximize the reward, without explicitly using reinforcement learning. This is similar to the approach taken by \cite{liu2021language}, where the authors use human feedback to train a reward model and then fine-tune the LM using reinforcement learning. However, our approach is different in two important ways. First, we use a simple reinforcement learning algorithm, called \textbf{Direct Preferences Optimization} or DPO, that does not require sampling from the LM during training. Second, we do not use reinforcement learning to optimize the reward model, but instead use the reward model to optimize the LM directly. This allows us to avoid the instability and complexity of reinforcement learning, and instead use a simple classification loss to optimize the reward model. As a result, our approach is much simpler to implement and train than reinforcement learning-based approaches.

We evaluate DPO on several tasks, including sentiment analysis, summary generation, and single-turn dialogue generation. Our results show that, compared to existing methods, DPO is able to achieve comparable or better performance while being substantially simpler to use and easier to train. In particular, we find that DPO can be easily implemented in a few lines of code, and can be trained in minutes using a single GPU. We also show that DPO can improve the quality of LLMs, outperforming Ppo-based RLHF on sentiment and summarization, and achieving near-perfect performance on single-turn dialogue. Finally, we demonstrate that DPO can also be used to improve the performance of existing LLMs, outperforming PPO-based RLHF by a small margin on sentiment and summarization tasks, and matching PPObased RLHF performance on singleturn dialogue.
