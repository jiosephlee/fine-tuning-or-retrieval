\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference score of a given generation.  In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require any sampling from the LM during training, nor does it require any hyperparameter tuning.  Instead, DPO uses a simple classification loss to optimize the reward model, and then fine-tunes the reward model to maximize the human preference scores of the generated samples.  This results in a simple yet effective method for controlling the behavior of LMs.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Unsupervised Language Models}. Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has been a major focus of recent work in natural language processing (see \cite{liu2022learning,liu2023training} for surveys).  The most popular approach to training LMs is training them on large corpora of unlabeled text, typically using a sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) architecture.  Such models have achieved impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including machine translation, question answering, and summarization.  However, the training data used to train these models is often not annotated with explicit preferences, making it difficult to evaluate the quality of the resulting LMs.  To address this, several works have proposed methods for evaluating the quality of LMs based on human judgments of the relative quality of different model generations.  These methods typically involve collecting a set of human preferences, and then finetuning the LMs to maximize the human preference of a given generation. This is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF for short.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning}. Reinforcement learning (RL) is a general framework for learning to make decisions in an environment, where the agent receives a reward for each action it takes, and the goal is to maximize the total reward over time.  RL has been successfully applied to a wide range of problems in computer science, including games (e.g., \cite{silver2017mastering, silver2018general}), robotics (e.g., \citet{hassan2018deep}), and natural language processing (e.g., \ref{thm:reinforcement_learning_nlg}).  In particular, RL has been used to train LMs to generate text in response to prompts, such as \cite{liu2019learning,liu2020language,liu2021learning,liu2022training}.  These methods typically use a reward model to estimate the human preference of a generated sample, and then fine-tune the LMs to maximize this estimated reward.  This is known as reinforcement-learning from human feedback, or RLFHF for short.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}

We now formally define the problem of RLHF.  Given a set of preferences $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}$, where $p_i$ is a human preference for a given generation of a LM, and a set of unlabeled text $X$, the goal of RLHF is to find a reward model $R(\cdot|\mathbf{x})$ that maximizes the expected reward of a given generation $\mathbf{x}$, i.e.,

\begin{equation}
    \max_{R} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P(X)} \left[ R(\mathbf{x}; \mathcal{R}) \right],
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{R}$ is a reward function that maps a given generation $\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}; p_i)$ to a scalar reward value, and the expectation is taken over all possible text $X$ and all possible reward models $R$.  We assume that the reward model $R$ is unknown to the human annotators, and that the goal is to find a reward function $\mathcal{R}(p_i)$ that maximizes the expected human preference score of a generation $\mathbf{x}$.  This is equivalent to maximizing the expected log likelihood of the human preferences, i.e.,

    \begin{equation}
        \max_{\mathcal{R}} \mathbb{E}_\mathcal{R} \left[ \log p_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{R})\right].
    \end{equation}

We refer to the reward function $\mathcal R$ that maximizes the above expected log likelihood as the \textit{optimal reward model} for the preferences $\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{R}$, and denote it by $\mathcal{R}_\text{opt}$.
