\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Language Models}. Large-scale language models (LMs), such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) and GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks. However, their performance is largely determined by the training data they are fed, and their ability to generalize to new tasks is limited. To address this, researchers have begun to use LMs to generate text on their own, which has led to the development of methods for generating text from scratch, such as GPT-2 (OpenAI, 2020) and GPT-Neo (Ghahramani et al., 2021). These methods have shown promising results, but are limited by the small size of the training data and the difficulty of training them from scratch. 

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback}. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RHLF) is a popular approach for training LMs to perform specific tasks. In RHLF, a human annotates a set of pairs of model-generated text and human judgments of its quality, and the model is trained to maximize the human preferences. This approach has been successfully applied to various tasks, including sentiment analysis (Chen et al., 2022), summarization (Zhang et al., 2023), and single-turn dialogue (Wang et al., 2024). However, RHLF is a complex and often difficult-to-implement procedure, and the resulting reward models are often unstable. In this paper, our goal is to develop a simple and effective method for RHLF that can be used to train LMs with high performance.

\textbf{Reward Maximization}. Reward maximization is a common approach for training LMs. In this approach, the reward function is learned from the human preferences, and the LM is trained to maximize the reward. The most popular method for learning the reward function is reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the reward function is learned by maximizing the human preferences. However, RLHF is a difficult and unstable procedure, and there are few methods for solving it. In this paper, the main contribution is to propose a new parameterization for reward models that enables us solve the standard RLHF with a simple classification loss.

\textbf{Control of Language Models}. There are many methods for controlling LMs, including reinforcement learning (Sutton and Barto, 2018), imitation learning (Hoffman et al., 2017), and active learning (Klein et al., 2019). These methods have been successfully applied to various language tasks, including sentiment analysis, machine translation, and summarization. However, these methods are often computationally expensive and require significant hyperparameter tuning. In this paper, while we focus on RLHF, we believe that our approach can be applied to other methods for controlling LMs.
