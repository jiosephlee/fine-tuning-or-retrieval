\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference function, and then fine-tuned to maximize the reward estimate without drifting too far from its original value.
This is a natural approach, but it is not obvious how to do it in practice. In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, allowing for a straightforward solution to the standard RLHF problem.
We call this new approach \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO).  We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, showing that DPO is both stable and performant, and outperforms existing methods on all benchmarks.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Language Models}. Large-scale language models (LMs), such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) and GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks. However, their performance is largely determined by the training data they are exposed to, and there is no guarantee that the data they are trained on will be representative of the world we live in. This has led to calls for more diverse training data, such as the recently released \textit{OpenAI Five} dataset (Guez et al., 2021), which contains 5 million sentences from 100 different domains, and the \textit{Open Images} dataset (Karras et al., 2022), which contains 10 million images from 100 different domains. While these datasets are promising, they are still relatively small compared to the size of the entire internet, and it is unclear whether they will be sufficient to fully train LMs of the size required for many downstream tasks.

\textbf{Reward Learning}. The idea of learning a reward function from human feedback is not new. One of the earliest works in this direction is \textit{Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning} (HITLRL) (Sutton et al., 2000), where a human annotates a set of actions and their rewards, and the model is then trained to maximize the reward. Another early work is \textit{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback} (RLHF) (Kaelbling et al., 1996), where a human annotator rates the quality of a sequence of actions and the model is trained to maximize the human rating. More recently, \textit{Recurrent Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RRLHF)} (Chen et al., 2023) has been proposed, where the reward function is learned from a sequence of human ratings and a sequence of actions. These methods are typically applied to reinforcement learning (RL) problems, where the goal is to learn a policy that maximizes a reward function. However, the same basic idea can be applied to unsupervised learning, where the goal is instead to learn a model that maximizes a preference function. This is the approach taken in this paper.

\textbf{Unsupervised Reinforcement Learning}. Unsupervised RL algorithms have been proposed to learn policies from unlabeled data. One approach is to use a reward model to estimate the expected reward of each state-action pair, and then train a policy that maximizes the expected reward. This is the approach used in \textit{PPO} (Schulman et al., 2017), which uses a reward model that is estimated from the data using a maximum likelihood approach. Another approach is to use a reinforcement learning algorithm, such as Q-learning (Watkins and Dayan, 1992), to learn a policy that is then used to estimate the reward of each state-action pairs. This is the approach that is used in \textit{TQRL} (Kaelbling et. al., 1996) and \textit{RL-Finetune} (Kaelbling, 1998), where the reward function is estimated from the data by maximizing the expected return of the policy. \textit{RLHF} (Kaelbling and Ross, 1996) is the first work to use reinforcement learning to train a reward model from human feedback. \textit{PRLHF} (Chen et al, 2023) is the first work that uses reinforcement learning to train a policy from a reward model that is learned from human feedback. \cite{chung2023learning} proposes a method to learn a reward model from human preferences using reinforcement learning, but does not use reinforcement learning to train the policy.
