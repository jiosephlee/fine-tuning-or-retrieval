\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Language Models}. Large-scale language models (LMs), such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) and GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks. However, their performance is largely determined by the training data they are fed, and their ability to generalize to new tasks is limited. To address this, researchers have begun to use unsupervised training methods to generate new data from the existing training set. These methods include generative adversarial networks (Goodfellow et al., 2016), variational autoencoders (Kingma and Welling, 2014), and unsupervised pretraining (Oord et al., 2017; Devlin et al., 2021). While these methods have been shown to improve performance on downstream tasks, they are typically trained on large amounts of text, making them computationally expensive to train on real-world datasets.

\textbf{Reward Learning}. Reward learning is a popular approach for training models to make decisions based on rewards. In reinforcement learning, the goal is to learn a policy that maximizes a reward function. This is typically done by sampling from the policy and maximizing the expected reward. However, this requires sampling from the policy, which can be computationally expensive for large models. Additionally, the reward function may not be easily interpretable, making it difficult to understand how the model is making its decisions. In contrast, in preference learning, the goal is simply to maximize a reward function, without sampling from the policy. This makes it easier to understand how the model makes its decisions, but it can be difficult to find an optimal reward function that maximizes the human preference function. In this paper, our goal is to find an optimal reward model that maximizes the human preferences, and fine-tune the model to maximize the reward estimate.

In RHLF, the goal is to train a model to maximize a reward function that reflects the preferences of a human annotator. This reward function is typically learned using reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the model is trained to maximize the reward given a sequence of inputs and outputs. The reward function is typically learned by sampling from the policy, and then optimizing the expected reward without sampling. However, this can be computationally expensive, and it can be difficult to ensure that the reward function accurately reflects the human preferences.
