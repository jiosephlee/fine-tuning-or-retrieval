\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference score of a given generation.  In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require any sampling from the LM during training, nor does it require any hyperparameter tuning.  Instead, DPO uses a simple classification loss to optimize the reward model, and then fine-tunes the reward model to maximize the human preference scores of the generated samples.  This results in a simple yet effective method for controlling the behavior of LMs.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Unsupervised Language Models}. Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has been a major focus of recent work in natural language processing (see, e.g., \cite{liu2022learning,liu2023training}).  These models are trained on massive amounts of text data, typically using a combination of self-supervised learning and reinforcement learning (RL).  Self-supervised learning methods like contrastive learning (e.g., \cite{caron2020learning,li2020learning,liu2021language}) and unsupervised language modeling (e.g., \citet{hulora2022,liu2022learning}) have been shown to be effective at training high-quality LMs.  Reinforcement learning methods like policy gradient (e.g., \protect\cite{hulora2021training,liu2021reinforcement,liu2022training}) and value iteration (e.g., \protec\cite{huloda2022,liu2019learning,liu2022fine}) have also been used to train LMs.  While these methods have been shown to produce high-quality LMs, they are often computationally expensive and require access to large amounts of text data.

\textbf{Control of Language Models}. While LMs have been shown to be useful for a variety of tasks, controlling their behavior is often difficult due to the completely uncontrolled nature of their training.  Existing methods for gaining steerability include adding explicit rewards to the training objective (e.g., \ref{thm:reward-rlhf}), using reinforcement learning to train a reward model from human preferences, and using reinforcement learning to train an optimal policy from the reward model.  The first approach, reward learning from human feedback (RLLHF), has been shown to be effective for controlling LMs in sentiment analysis (e.g., \thmref{thm:rlhf-sentiment}), summarization (e.g., \reftitle{thm:rlhfsummarization}), and single-turn dialogue (e.g., \refs{liu2023dialogue},\ref{thm:dialogue-control}).  RLLHF typically involves collecting human preferences for multiple generations of the model, and then fine tuning the unsupervised LM using RL to maximize the human preference ratings.  This approach is computationally expensive, and often requires significant hyperparameter tuning to ensure stability.  In contrast, DPO uses a simpler reward model that can be optimized directly with a classification loss, and then fine-tuned to maximize the human preference of the generated samples. Our experiments show that DPO is both computationally efficient and stable, and can produce high-quality control policies for LMs.

We assume that the human preferences are represented by a vector $p \in \mathbb{R}^d$, where $d$ is the number of dimensions in the reward model.  We also assume that the human preferences can be represented by a vector $q \in \mathbb R^d$ that is orthogonal to the reward model, i.e., $q^\top r = 0$ for all $r \in \mathbb {R}^d$.
