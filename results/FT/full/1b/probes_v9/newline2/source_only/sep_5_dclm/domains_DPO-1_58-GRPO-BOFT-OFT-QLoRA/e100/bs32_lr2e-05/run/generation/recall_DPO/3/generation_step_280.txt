\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for fine-tuning a language model (LM) to align with human preferences. The key insight is that the reward model used in RLHF can be reparameterized as a policy gradient, and the resulting policy can be optimized using standard RL algorithms. We demonstrate that this approach can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO outperforms existing methods on all tasks, and is simpler to implement and train.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years. In particular, GPT-3~\citep{brown2020gpt3} is a 175B parameter language model trained on 175B tokens of open web text. GPT-3 is able to generate fluent text on a wide range of topics, but its performance is limited by the lack of supervision. To address this, researchers have proposed various methods to fine-tune GPT-3~(e.g.,~\citep{liu2022training,liu2023learning,liu2023fine,liu2024fine}). These methods typically use reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize the LM to achieve a desired task. For example, \cite{liu2022learning} uses RL to optimize the LM to generate high-quality text on a given topic. \cite{liu2019learning} uses RL to improve the fluency of GPT-3. \cite{li2022fine} uses RL to improve GPT-3's ability to generate coherent text. \cite{li2019learning} uses a combination of RL and imitation learning to improve GPT-2's performance on the WMT14 English-German translation task. \cite{liujia2023} uses RL to improve a pretrained GPT-3 model on the WMT14 French-German translation task. However, these methods are not straightforward to apply to other tasks, and they are often unstable and difficult to tune.

To address these limitations, we propose a new method for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences. Our method, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), uses reinforcement learning to optimize a reward model that reflects human preferences. The reward model is learned from human preferences collected through a preference elicitation task. Once the reward model is learned, DPO uses reinforcement learning to optimize the LM to maximize the reward. This is similar to the standard RLHF method~\citep{hulora2022}, but DPO has several advantages. First, DPO does not require sampling from the LM during training, which can be computationally expensive. Second, DPO can be easily implemented and tuned, unlike the complex RLHF method. Third, DPO can be applied to a wider range of tasks than RLHF, including sentiment analysis, summary generation, and single-turn dialogue.
