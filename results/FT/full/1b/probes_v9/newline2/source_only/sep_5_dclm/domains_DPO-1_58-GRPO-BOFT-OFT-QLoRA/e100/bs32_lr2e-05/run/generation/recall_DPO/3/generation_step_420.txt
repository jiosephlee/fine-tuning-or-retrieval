\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Language Models}. Large-scale language models (LMs), such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) and GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks. However, their performance is largely determined by the training data they are exposed to, and their ability to generalize to new tasks is limited. To address this, researchers have begun to explore the use of unsupervised training methods, such as generative adversarial networks (Goodfellow et al., 2016) and unsupervised pretraining (Hulsmans et al., 2017; Devlin et al., 2021).

\textbf{Reward Learning}. Reward learning is a general framework for training models to make decisions based on rewards. The most common application of reward learning is reinforcement learning (RL), where the goal is to learn a policy that maximizes a reward function. However, reward learning has also been applied to other problems, such as image captioning (Krizhevsky et al., 2012) and text-to-image generation (He et al., 2019). In these applications, the reward function is typically learned from human feedback, and the goal is to optimize the reward function to match the human preferences.

\textbf{Human Feedback for Reinforcement Learning}. Human feedback for reinforcement learning (RL) is a common approach for improving the performance of RL algorithms. In this approach, the goal is to learn an optimal policy from human feedback, which is typically collected through a reinforcement learning algorithm. One popular method for collecting human feedback is reinforcement learning from human feedback with reinforcement learning from human preference (RLHF). In this approach, the RL algorithm is used to collect human feedback, and then the optimal policy is fine-tuned to maximize this feedback. This approach has been shown to be effective for improving the performance of reinforcement learning algorithms, but it is often unstable and difficult to implement.

\textbf{Stable Reward Learning}. To address the instability of RLHF, several methods have been proposed to stabilize the reward learning process. One approach is to use a reward model to estimate the reward function before fine-tuning the policy. This is known as reward model-based reinforcement learning (RMBRL). Another approach is to use a policy gradient method to optimize the reward model, which is known as policy gradient reinforcement learning (PGRL). Both of these approaches have been shown to be effective at stabilizing the reward learning process. However, they both require additional computational resources, making them less practical for large-scale applications.

\textbf{Reward Optimization}. While RLHF is a common approach for fine-tuning LMs, it is not always the best choice. For example, RLHF is often unstable and difficult for humans to interpret. In contrast, reward optimization is a more stable and interpretable approach for fine-tuning an LLM. In reward optimization, the goal is to optimize a reward function to match the preferences of a human annotator. This approach has been used to improve the performance of LMs in tasks such as sentiment analysis (Wang et al., 2022) and summarization (Zhang et al., 2023). However, it is not clear how to optimize a reward function in a way that is both interpretable and effective for fine-tuning an LM.
