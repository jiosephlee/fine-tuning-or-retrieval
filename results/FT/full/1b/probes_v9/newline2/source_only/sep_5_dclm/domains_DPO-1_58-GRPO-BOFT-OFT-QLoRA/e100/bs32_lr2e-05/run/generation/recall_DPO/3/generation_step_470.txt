\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference function, and then fine-tuned to maximize the reward estimate without drifting too far from its original value.
This is a natural approach, but it is not obvious how to do it in practice. In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, allowing for a straightforward solution to the standard RLHF problem.
We call this new approach \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO).  We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, showing that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with high performance and low computational cost.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Unsupervised Language Models}. Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has been a major focus in recent years, with the most successful approaches being GPT-3 and OpenAI's BERT (Devlin et al., 2019). These models have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, but are also known to exhibit undesirable behaviors, such as racist and sexist language (Bai et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Li et al., in press; Li et al., under review; Li et al., submitted). To address these issues, researchers have proposed various mitigation techniques, including language modeling with explicit human feedback (Ghazvininejad et al., 2022; Li et al.,2022b; Li et al.,under review; Li et al, submitted), restricting the scope of the LM (Li et al., 2023), and fine-tuning the LM with reinforcement learning (RL) (Li et al.,2022a; Li et al.,submitted; Li et al.,in press; Li et al, under review; Li etal., submitted).

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning for Language Modeling}. Reinforcement learning (RL) has been successfully applied to language modeling (Sutton and Barto, 2018; Li et al.,2019; Li et al.,al., submitted; Li et al., al., under review; Wang et al., 2024). Most existing RL methods for language modeling use a reward model to estimate the human preference for each generation, and then fine-tune the unsuited model using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward. This approach is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback with reinforcement learning from human preference (RLHF). The most popular RLHF method is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017), which uses a proximal policy optimization (PPO) algorithm to optimize the reward model parameters. However, PPO is a complex algorithm that requires careful tuning of hyperparameters, and it is often unstable, especially when the reward model is not well-conditioned (Li et al.,submitted; Wang et al.,2024). Other popular RLHF methods include Value-Adaptive Actor-Critic (VACO) (Chen et al., 2025), Value-Consistent Actor-Critic (VACC) (Wang et al., 2026), and Policy Gradient with Memory (PGM) (Wang etal., 2026). While these methods generally perform better than PPO, they still require significant hyperparameter tuning and are often unstable.

\textbf{Reward Model Parameterization}. The reward model is the key component of RLHF. It is typically represented as a neural network (NN) that maps a LM token sequence to a scalar reward value. The reward model is optimized to maximize the reward estimate, which is often done by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the reward model predictions given the LM output. However, the reward model is not uniquely determined by the reward function, and there are many ways to parameterize the reward model. For example, the reward model can be represented as a neural network that takes the LM token sequence as input and outputs a scalar reward value, or as a neural network that maps a LM token to a reward value and a policy to a reward value, and then optimizes the reward value and the policy jointly. In this paper, following Wang et al. (2024), we use the second representation, which is more flexible and easier to optimize. We refer to this parameterization as the \textit{policy reward model}.
