\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model training has become a major focus of research in natural language processing (NLP) in recent years. These models are trained on massive amounts of unlabeled text, and they learn to represent the entire world of human language, including the semantics, syntax, and culture of different languages. They are also able to perform some reasoning tasks, such as answering questions about the content of text, and they can be used to generate text that is similar to the input text. However, these models are completely unsupervised, and it is difficult to control their behavior in a precise way. This is because the training data is not labeled, and the model is not trained to learn any particular task or domain. Instead, the model learns to represent the entire world, and it is up to the user to decide what to do with the model's representations. This makes it difficult to use the model for specific tasks, and it is also difficult to control the model's behavior in a precise way.

One way to control the behavior of a large-scale unsupervised LM is to collect human feedback on the quality of model generations. This is called reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. The idea is to collect human feedback about the quality of model generations and then fine-tune an unsupervised LM to maximize the human feedback. This is a complex and often difficult procedure, because it requires collecting a large number of human labels, and it is often difficult to find a good way to measure the quality of model generations.

In this paper, we propose an alternative approach to RLHF, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). D PO is a simple and stable method for fine-tuning an unsupervised LM using human feedback. The key idea is that we can use a reward model to measure the quality of a model generation, and then use this reward to fine-tune the LM. The reward model is simply a function that maps model generations to a scalar value, and we can use this function to estimate the quality of a model. The reward model is simple and easy to train, and it is stable, meaning that it does not change much over time. We can use the reward model to estimate the optimal policy for fine-tuning the LM, and we can use reinforcement learning to optimize the reward model to maximize the reward. This is a simple and effective way to fine-tune an unsuupervised LM using human preferences.

We evaluate DPO on a wide range of tasks, including sentiment classification, summarization, and dialogue. We show that DPO can achieve state-of-the art performance on all of these problems, and that it can also be used to improve the performance of existing RLHF methods. In particular, we show that DPO can improve the performance of PPO-based RLHF methods, and we show that DPO is more stable than PPO-based RLHF. We also show that DPO is a simple and effective method for fine-tuning LMs, and we provide a simple recipe for how to use DPO to fine-tune LLMs. We evaluate DPO using a variety of different architectures and training settings, and we show that the performance of DPO is robust to these variations. We also evaluate DPO in combination with existing RL methods, and we show how DPO can be used in combination to achieve even better results. Our experiments show that DPO is simple to use and easy to implement, and that it can achieve state of the art performance on a wide range of NLP tasks.
