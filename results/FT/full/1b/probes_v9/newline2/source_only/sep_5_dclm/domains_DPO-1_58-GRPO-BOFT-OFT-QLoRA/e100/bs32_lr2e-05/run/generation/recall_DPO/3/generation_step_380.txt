\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for fine-tuning a language model (LM) to match human preferences. The key insight is to parameterize the reward function of the RL algorithm in a way that allows us to find the optimal policy from the reward function, which is the policy we want to optimize. We then use this policy to fine-tune the LM, and the resulting policy is guaranteed to be optimal.

We evaluate DPO on two benchmarks: the 2023 SQuAD v4.1 dataset and the 2023 Open Language Modeling (OLM) dataset. For both datasets, we compare DPO to the following baselines: 1) PPO, a standard RL algorithm; 2) PPO with a simple hyperparameter tuning strategy; 3) PPO with a more sophisticated hyperparameter tuning strategy; and 4) DPO with a simple hyperparameters tuning strategy. We also compare DPO to a variant of DPO called DPO-RLHF, which uses reinforcement learning from human feedback to fine-tune the reward model. We find that DPO consistently outperforms all baselines on both datasets, including DPO-RLHF. In particular, DPO achieves the best performance on the OLM dataset, and is comparable to DPO-RLHF on the SQuAD v4 dataset. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of DPO in fine-tuning LMs to match human preferences.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language Models}. Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has become a popular research topic in recent years. In particular, GPT-4~\citep{gpt4} is a state-of-the-art LM that was trained on the Google News dataset and achieved impressive performance on various downstream tasks. Despite its impressive performance, GPT-4 is not a supervised model, and its training data is entirely unsupervised. This makes it challenging to control the behavior of GPT-4, since there is no supervision to guide its behavior. To address this challenge, many works have been proposed to fine-tune GPT-4 to match human preferences. For example, \citep{liu2023training} proposes TrainingGPT, a method to fine-tune GTP-4 to match human preference by maximizing the cosine similarity between the generated text and human-written text. \citep{wang2024training} proposes TrainingGpt4, a method to fine tune GPT-4 to human preferences by maximizing the average cosine similarity between the generated and human-written text. Both TrainingGPT and TrainingGpt4 use a neural text-to-text model to generate text from the prompt, and then use a neural language model to generate text from a given prompt. \citep{li2023training} proposes a method to fine-tuning GPT-4 to English sentiment by maximizing the average cosine similarities between the generated text and the human-written reviews. \citep{zhang2024training} proposes a method for fine-tuning Chinese GPT-4 to Chinese sentiment by maximizing the average similarity between the generated text from the prompt and the human-written reviews.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning}. Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful method for learning to perform tasks from rewards. In RL, an agent interacts with an environment to collect rewards, and uses the rewards to learn a policy that maps the state of the environment to the next action. The policy is optimized to maximize the expected total reward over the long term. RL has been successfully applied to various tasks, such as playing Atari games~\citep{silver2016exploring}, controlling robots~\citep{hassan2017deep}, and generating text~\citep{liu2018reinforcement,liu2020learning,liu2021language}. However, RL is typically difficult to implement due to the need to sample from the environment, which can be computationally expensive. To address this challenge and enable the use of RL in practice, many works have proposed methods to train an RL agent with only a small number of human labels. For example, \citet{liu2020learning} proposes a method to train a language model with only a few human-written reviews. \citet{li2021language} proposes a method to learn a language model with only human-written text. \citet{zhang2023training} proposes an end-to-end training framework for training a language model with only text. \citet{liao2023training} proposes to train a language model using only text and unlabeled data. \citet{wang2024training}, \citet{liang2024training}, and \citet{li2019training} propose methods to train a language model from only text. These methods are often referred to as \textit{RLHF} methods, since they use reinforcement learning from human feedback.
