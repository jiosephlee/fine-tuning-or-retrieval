\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

The key idea is to use a reward model to estimate the quality of model generations, and then fine-tune the LM to maximize the reward. This is similar to the approach taken in reinforcement learning from human feedback, but instead of using human feedback to train a reward model, we directly optimize the reward model to match the preferences of the human annotators. We refer to this approach as \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO).

DPO is a simple yet powerful method for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences. In this paper, we present a novel parameterization of the reward function that enables us to solve the standard DPO problem in closed form, allowing for efficient and stable training. We evaluate DPO on a variety of tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is able to align LMs with human preferences as well or better than existing methods, while being substantially simpler to train and implement. In particular, we find that DPO can be trained in a few minutes on a single GPU, while existing methods require hours or days of training.

We also evaluate DPO in a setting where it is used to fine-tune an existing LLM to improve its performance on a downstream task. We find that DPO is able not only to align the LLM with human preferences, but also to improve its performance on the downstream task. In particular, we show that DPO can improve the performance of the LLM by up to 1.5% on the task of summarization, and by up to 0.5% on the single-turn dialogue task. These results demonstrate the potential of DPO to improve the performance of LMs in downstream tasks, and suggest that DPO may be a useful tool for improving the performance of LMs on downstream tasks.

\textbf{Reward Learning.} Reward learning is a popular approach to training reinforcement learning agents to perform a given task. In reward learning, an agent is given a reward signal that reflects the quality of the actions it takes, and the goal is to learn a policy that maximizes the reward. The most popular reward learning algorithms are Q-learning and actor-critic algorithms. Q-learning is a simple algorithm that uses a value function to estimate the expected reward of an action, and then updates the policy by taking the action with the highest value. Actor-critic algorithms use a policy and a value function to estimate a critic that estimates the expected reward of an agent's actions, and then updates the critic by taking the action with highest value.
