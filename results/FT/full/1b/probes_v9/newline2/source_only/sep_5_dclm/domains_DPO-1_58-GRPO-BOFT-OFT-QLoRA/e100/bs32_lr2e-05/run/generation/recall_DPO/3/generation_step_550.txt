\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference function, and then fine-tuned to maximize the reward estimate without drifting too far from its original value.
This is a natural approach, but it is not obvious how to do it in practice, since the reward function is typically a complex neural network that is difficult to optimize directly.
In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward functions that allows us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, and thus obtain an efficient algorithm for DPO.
We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, showing that it can be used to fine-tune LMs with high performance and low computational cost.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has become a central focus of research in natural language processing (NLP) in recent years, with many successful approaches that have led to state-of-the-art results on various benchmarks (see \cite{liu2023unsupervised} for a review).
One of the most prominent approaches is Generative Pre-trained Language Models (GPT-N; \cite{radford2019language}), which are trained on massive amounts of text data and achieve impressive performance on downstream tasks.
However, GPT-N models are completely unsupervised, and thus it is unclear how to control their behavior and prevent them from diverging into undesirable directions.
To address this problem, many works have proposed methods for fine-tuning GPT-N models to achieve specific goals, such as improving the quality of generated text \citep{hulora2023training,liu2023unsuper}, generating more diverse text \citep{zhang2023training}, or improving the performance of downstream tasks \citep{wang2023training,li2023training}.
These methods typically involve collecting human labels of the generated text, and then fine-tune the unsuper- vious LM using reinforcement learning (RL; \cite{silver2014reinforcement,li2021learning,liu2023training}).

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful method for learning to perform tasks from human feedback, and has been successfully applied to many NLP tasks, such as machine translation \citep{silver2014reinfec,li2021learning}, question answering \citep{hu2020learning,liu2021learning}, and dialogue \citep{liu2023training,chen2023learning}.
In RL, a model is trained to maximize a reward function that reflects the human preferences for the generated output, and the model is then fine-tuned to improve the performance of a downstream task.
However, RL is a complex and often computationally expensive procedure, and it is not always possible to find a suitable reward function that accurately reflects the human preferences.
In this paper, our goal is to develop a simple and effective method for fine-tuning LMs using RL, without having to solve the complex RL problem.

Another related line of work focuses on learning reward functions from human preferences, and then using these reward functions to train LMs.
One of the earliest works in this direction is \cite{hulora2022learning}, which proposes a method for learning reward functions from human preference annotations.
The authors use a generative adversarial network (GAN) to learn a mapping from human preferences to reward functions, and then fine-tunes the LM using RL to maximize the reward function.
However, the reward function learned by \cite{huloda2022learning} is a complex neural network, and it is not clear how to optimize it directly.
In this paper we propose a new parameterized reward function that allows us to solve this problem in closed form, enabling us to obtain an efficient algorithm for learning reward functions from preferences.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}

In this section, we introduce the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; \ref{sec:dpo}) algorithm, which is a simple and effective method to fine-tune LLMs using RL.
As we will see, DPO is based on a simple yet powerful idea: instead of optimizing the reward function directly, we can optimize a simpler function that is easier to optimize, and then optimize the simpler function to maximize the original reward function.
This idea is similar to the method of \cite{hulod2022learning}, but our approach is much simpler and more efficient.
