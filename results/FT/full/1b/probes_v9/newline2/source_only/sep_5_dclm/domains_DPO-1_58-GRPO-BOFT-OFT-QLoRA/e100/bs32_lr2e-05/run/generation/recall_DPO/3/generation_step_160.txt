\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for fine-tuning a language model (LM) to align with human preferences. The key idea is to use a reward model to estimate the quality of model generations, and then fine-tune the LM to maximize the reward. This is similar to the approach taken in reinforcement learning from human feedback, but instead of using human feedback to train a reward model, we directly optimize the reward model to match the human preferences. We refer to this approach as \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO).

We evaluate DPO on two tasks: sentiment analysis and summarization. For sentiment analysis, we use the same dataset as \cite{liu2022direct}, which consists of 1,000,000 sentences from Reddit posts. We use the same evaluation protocol as \cite{liang2022direct}, where we evaluate the model's ability to generate positive, neutral, and negative sentiment. For summarization, we use the same datasets as \cite{li2022learning}, which consists of 10,000 news articles from the New York Times. We use the same summarization evaluation protocol as \cite{lai2022learning}, where we evaluate the ability of the model to generate a summary that is both fluent and coherent.

We find that DPO is able to achieve comparable performance to existing methods, including PPO-based RLHF \cite{li2021learning}, and achieves better performance in some cases. In particular, DPO is able to improve the quality of sentiment generation and summarization, and is more stable than PPO-based RLHF. We also find that DPO is much simpler to implement and train than RLHF, and is able to be trained on a single GPU in a few hours.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language Models.} Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years. The most popular approach is to train a generative model using a large corpus of unlabeled data, such as the Google News dataset \cite{touvron2020news}. These models are typically trained on a large number of parameters, and are often referred to as \textit{large language models} (LLMs). The most popular LLMs today are BERT \cite{devlin2018bert}, GPT-3 \cite{brown2020language}, and OpenAI GPT-4 \cite{openai2024gpt4}. These models have been shown to have impressive performance on a variety of tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and machine translation. However, they are also known to exhibit undesirable behaviors, such as being biased towards certain topics or political views \cite{liu2019language,liu2022direct,li2022learning}. To address these issues, researchers have proposed various methods for controlling the behavior of LLMs, including adversarial training \cite{liu19adversarial,liu2021adversarial}, fine-tuning with human feedback \cite{li2020fine,li2021learning}, reinforcement learning from human feedback \cite{zhang2021reinforcement,li2022learning}, and direct preference optimization \cite{liu21direct,liu2022learning}. We focus on the latter approach in this paper.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning.} Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful method for learning to perform tasks in an environment. It has been successfully applied to a wide range of tasks, including robotics \cite{tao2017deep,tao2018learning}, computer vision \cite{silver2017learning}, and natural language processing \cite{tao2020learning,li2021learning,liu2022reinforcement}. In RL, an agent interacts with an environment by taking actions, and receives a reward signal that reflects the agent's performance. The goal of the agent is to maximize the total reward over time. One popular approach to RL is to use a policy gradient method, which optimizes the policy $\pi$ that maps the agent's current state to the next action. The policy gradient method is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^T r_t\mid s_t\right]\pi(s_t)
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{J}:\mathbb{R}^d\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is the loss function, $r_t$ is the reward at time $t$, and $\pi$ is the policy. The policy gradient method can be used to train a policy $\pi$ that maximizes the expected total reward over time.
