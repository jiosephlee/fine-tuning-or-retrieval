\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference score of a given generation.  In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require any sampling from the LM during training, nor does it require any hyperparameter tuning.  Instead, DPO uses a simple classification loss to optimize the reward model, and then fine-tunes the reward model to maximize the human preference scores of the generated samples.  This results in a simple yet effective method for controlling the behavior of LMs.

\paragraph{Related Work} Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has been a major focus of recent work in natural language processing (NLP).  In particular, unsupervised LM training has been shown to be effective for tasks such as machine translation, question answering, and summarization (see, e.g., \cite{liu2018language,liu2019learning,liu2020unsupervised}).  However, the lack of supervision makes it difficult to control the behavior of these models, especially when compared to supervised models.  To address this, many works have proposed methods for fine-tuning unsupervised LMs to achieve specific behaviors.  For example, \cite{liu2020unssupervised} proposes a method for fine-tuning an unsupervised LM to generate questions that match a given question-answering model.  \cite{wang2022training} proposes a method for training an unsupervised LM on a dataset of human-written summaries to generate high-quality summaries.  \cite{liu_2023_towards} proposes a method for controlling the sentiment of an unsupervised LM by fine-tuning it on a dataset of human-labeled positive and negative sentences.  These methods all rely on collecting human annotations of the relative quality of different model generations, and then fine-tune the unsatisfied model to match the human preferences.  While these methods are effective, they are often computationally expensive, and require significant hyperparameter tuning to achieve good performance.

\paragraph{Reward Models} The key idea behind these methods is to use a reward model to estimate the human preferences of a given model generation.  A reward model is a function that maps a model generation to a scalar value representing the human preference of that generation.  The goal of RLHF is to find a policy that maximizes the reward of the generated samples.   In the case of unsupervised LMs, the reward model is typically learned from human preferences, and the policy is learned from reinforcement learning.  In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward learning problem with only a simple binary classification loss.  This allows us to use a straightforward policy gradient method to optimize the reward model and fine-tune the LM to maximize the reward of the generated model generations.

\paragraph{Direct Preference Optimization} Our main contribution is a new method for controlling the behavior and preferences of unsupervised LLMs, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  DPO is based on the idea of using a reward model to estimate human preferences, but instead of optimizing the reward model to maximize human preferences, we optimize the reward model to maximize a human preference score of a model generation.  This preference score is simply the average of the human preference scores of all the model generations produced by a given model.  We refer to this preference score as the \emph{direct preference} of the model generation.  Our intuition is that the direct preference of a model generation should be a good proxy for the human preference of that model generation, since the human preference of a model generation is simply the average of its direct preferences.  Therefore, we can use the reward model to estimate the direct preference of a given model generation, and then optimize the reward model to increase the direct preference of the model generation.   This leads to a simple yet effective method of controlling the behavior and preferences, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization}.

\paragraph{Experimental Results} We evaluate DPO on several tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.
