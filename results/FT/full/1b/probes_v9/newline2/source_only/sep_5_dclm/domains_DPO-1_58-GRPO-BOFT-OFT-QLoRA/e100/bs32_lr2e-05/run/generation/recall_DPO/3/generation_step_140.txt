\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for fine-tuning a large unsupervised language model (LM) to align with human preferences. The key idea is to use a reward model to estimate the quality of model generations, and then fine-tune the LM to maximize the reward. However, existing reward models are often complex and unstable, and the resulting fine-tuning procedure is often unstable as well. In this paper, we propose a new reward model that is simple and stable, and we show that it can be used to solve the standard RL problem with only a simple loss function. We call this new reward model \textit{Direct Preferences}, and we refer to the corresponding algorithm as \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO). DPO is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and it can be used to fine-tune LMs with a wide range of architectures and training settings. Our experiments show that DPO is competitive with existing methods in terms of performance, and it can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. In particular, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF on sentiment analysis and on summarization, and it matches or improves response quality on single-turn dialogue. DPO is also much simpler to implement and train than existing methods, and it can be trained in a few minutes on a single GPU.

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model training has become a popular approach to building general-purpose language models (LMs). These models are trained on massive amounts of unlabeled text, and they learn to represent the entire language, including the semantics of words, phrases, and sentences. This approach has been shown to yield powerful language models that can perform a wide range of tasks such as machine translation, question answering, and summarization (see, e.g., \cite{liu2022learning,liu2023unsupervised,liu2024unsupervised,li2019unsupervised,li2020unsupervised,liang2021unsupervised,liy2022unsupervised,liyu2022unsupervised} and references therein).

Despite their impressive performance, LMs are not perfect. They are not able to reason about the world in the same way that humans do, and they are not able to make decisions in the same way that people do. For example, LMs cannot explain why they made a particular decision, and they cannot provide evidence for their claims. This is a problem because it is important to understand how LMs make decisions, and it is important to be able to control their behavior. For example, if an LLM is used to make decisions in a medical setting, it is important to be sure that the LLM is making decisions in a way that is consistent with the best available evidence. Similarly, if an LLM makes decisions in a financial setting, it is important that the LLM is not making decisions that are inconsistent with the best available evidence.

To address this problem, researchers have proposed a number of approaches for controlling the behavior of LMs. One approach is to collect human labels of the quality of model generations and then fine-tune LMs using reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback (RLF). This approach has been shown effective in a number of settings, including sentiment analysis (e.g., \cite{zhang2022learning,li2022learning,liyu2022learning,liy2022learning,li2019unsilenced,li2020unsilenced,liyu2022unilenced,liyu2019unsilenced}), summarization (e.g., \citet{liu2022unsilenced,liu2023learning,liyu2023unsilenced,liy2022unilenced}), and single-turn dialogue (e.g., \e{li2022learning, liyu2022learning, li2023unsilenced}). However, collecting human labels of the quality and quantity required for effective RLF is a difficult and time-consuming task. Moreover, the RLF procedure is often unstable, and it is difficult to tune the RLF hyperparameters to achieve good performance.

In this paper, we propose an alternative approach for controlling the behavior of large-scale LMs. Instead of collecting human labels of the relative performance of model generations, we propose to use a reward model that estimates the quality of model generations. We then use the reward model to fine-tune the LMs to maximize the reward. This approach is similar to the RLF approach, but it avoids the need for collecting human labels. Moreover, the reward model can be simple and stable, and it can be easily estimated from the training data. We refer to this approach as \textit{direct preference optimization} (D PO).
