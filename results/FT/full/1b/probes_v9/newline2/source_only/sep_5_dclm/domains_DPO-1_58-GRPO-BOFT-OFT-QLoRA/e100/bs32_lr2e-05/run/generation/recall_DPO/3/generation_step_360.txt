\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference function, and then fine-tuned to maximize the reward estimate without drifting too far from its original value.
This is a natural approach, but it is not obvious how to do it in practice. In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, allowing for a straightforward solution to the standard RLHF problem.
We call this new parameterization the \textit{direct preference parameterization} (DPP), and our main contribution is a new algorithm, \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO) that uses the DPP to optimize the reward model and fine-tune the LM using reinforcement learning.
We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, showing that it can achieve strong performance with minimal hyperparameter tuning and few training samples.

\paragraph{Related Work} Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has become a major focus of research in recent years, with many methods achieving impressive performance on downstream tasks such as machine translation, question answering, and natural language understanding (see \cite{liu2022language} for a review). However, these methods are typically trained in an unsupervised manner, relying on the ability of the LM to learn from data alone. This makes it difficult to gain precise control over the behavior of the LM, especially when compared to supervised methods that can be fine-tuned to produce desired outputs. 
One approach to gain steerability is reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the goal is to learn a policy that maximizes a reward function defined by human preferences. This is often done using a method called reinforcement learning from human feedback with policy optimization (RLHF), where the reward function is learned from human feedback and the policy is optimized to maximize the reward. 
RLHF has been shown to be effective in a variety of settings, including improving the performance of LMs on downstream tasks \citep{liu2022learning,liu2023training}, and controlling the behavior of LMs in chatbot applications \citep{wang2023training,liu2023towards}. However, RLHF is a challenging and often unstable procedure, requiring the collection of human feedback and the tuning of a large number of hyperparameters, such as the number of episodes and the learning rate. 
In this paper, we introduce a new approach to RLHF, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which avoids the need for human feedback and tuning of hyperparameters. DPO is based on the observation that the reward function can be represented as a function of the LM's policy, and that the optimal policy can be found by maximizing the reward function. Specifically, we propose a new reward parameterization that allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem in closed form, and use this to optimize the reward function using gradient descent. We refer to this new approach as \textit{Direct Prefer-ence Optimization} (DPO). 

\paragraph{Reward Models} A reward model is a function that maps a sequence of inputs to a scalar reward value, representing the quality of the generated output. In RLHF, the reward model is optimized to maximize the expected reward of the policy learned from human feedback. The reward model is often represented as a neural network, where the input is the policy and the output is the reward. The network parameters are optimized using reinforcement learning from human feedback. 
There are many ways to represent the reward model, and different representations can lead to different algorithms for solving the RLHF problem. In this paper, our main contribution is a novel reward parameterization that allows for a straightforward solution to RLHF. We refer to this parameterization as the \textit{Direct Preference Parameterization} (DPP). The DPP is a function of the policy and the LM's output, and we can use it to represent the reward model. We can then use gradient descent to optimize the DPP, obtaining the DPO algorithm. 

\paragraph{Control of Language Models} There are many ways to control the behavior of LMs, including reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and active learning. Reinforcement learning is a powerful method for learning to perform tasks in an unsupervised setting, but it is often unstable and difficult to tune. Imitation learning is a method for learning to perform a task from an expert, but it requires access to an expert model. Active learning is a method for selecting a subset of examples to train a model from, but it requires the selection of examples to be performed by a human. 
In this paper we focus on reinforcement learning, and in particular on RLHF. We believe that DPO is a promising approach to gain steerability in RLHF, and we evaluate it on several benchmarks in Section 6.
