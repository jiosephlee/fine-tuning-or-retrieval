\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the human preference function, and then fine-tuned to maximize the reward estimate without drifting too far from its original value.
This is a natural approach, but it is not immediately clear how to optimize the reward model to achieve the desired goal.
In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, allowing for a straightforward solution to the standard RLHF problem.
We call this new parameterization the \textit{direct preference parameterization} (DPP), and our main contribution is a method for optimizing the DPP to maximize the human preference model, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or \textit{DPO}.
We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, showing that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with high performance and low computational cost.

\paragraph{Related Work} Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has recently become feasible due to advances in training methods and data availability.
Large LMs have been shown to have strong capabilities in tasks such as text generation, question answering, and summarization, but their behavior is often difficult to control due to the completely unsophisticated nature of their training.
One approach to gain control over the behavior of LMs is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to train a reward model from human feedback.
RLHF has been shown to be effective in controlling the behavior of LMs, but it is a complex and often unreliable procedure.
In this paper, our goal is to develop a simple and effective method for RLHF that does not require sampling from the LM during training or significant hyperparameter tuning.
We propose a new parameterization for the reward model that enables us to solve this problem with a straightforward optimization problem, which we call \footnote{\url{https://github.com/Alpesh-Prasad/DPO} the \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} algorithm.} \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO).

\paragraph{Reward Models} A reward model is a function that maps a sequence of inputs $\mathbf{x} = \langle x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n \rangle$ to a scalar reward $r(\mathbf{x})$.
The goal of RLHF is to find a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the expected reward, i.e., $\pi_\theta \in \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{D}}[r(\mathbf{x} ; \pi_\theta)]$.
In the context of RLHF, the reward model is typically a neural network that maps the input sequence $\mathbf{x}$ to a scalar reward $R(\mathbf{x})$, and the policy is typically a neural network mapping the reward to the next action $\pi_\theta(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{x}) = \pi_\theta(\mathcal{D}[\mathbf{r}; \theta])$.
The policy is typically trained using a reinforcement learning algorithm such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Actor-Critic (AC).

\paragraph{Reward Maximization} The standard RLHF problem is to find a policy that maximizes the expected human reward, i.e., $r_\theta \in \mathcal{R} \mapsto \argmax_{\theta} \mathbb{I}[r_\theta \geq R]$.
This is a convex optimization problem, and can be solved using methods such as gradient descent or stochastic gradient descent with momentum.
However, the reward model is often a neural network with many parameters, and training the policy to maximize the reward can be computationally expensive.

\paragraph{Reward Minimization} A common approach to reducing the computational cost of reward maximization is to use a reward model that is trained to minimize the expected reward, i,e., $r_\theta^*(\mathbf{x}) = -\mathbb{E}_{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal D} [r_\theta(\mathbf{x})]$.
This is equivalent to minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the reward model, and can be solved by maximizing the negative log-likelihood with gradient descent.
However, this approach can lead to unstable training, and may not converge to the global minimum.
Another approach is to use a reward function that is not strictly convex, such as a piecewise-linear function, which can also be solved using gradient descent.
