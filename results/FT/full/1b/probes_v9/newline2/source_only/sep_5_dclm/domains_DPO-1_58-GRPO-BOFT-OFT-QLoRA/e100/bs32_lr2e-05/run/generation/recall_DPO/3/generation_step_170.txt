\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for fine-tuning a language model (LM) to align with human preferences. The key idea is to use a reward model to estimate the quality of model generations, and then fine-tune the LM to maximize the reward. This is similar to the approach taken in reinforcement learning from human feedback, but instead of using human feedback to train a reward model, we directly optimize the reward model to match the human preferences. We refer to this approach as \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO).

DPO is a simple and effective method for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences. In our experiments, we find that DPO can achieve comparable performance to existing methods, including reinforcement learning from human feedback and reinforcement learning from self-play. However, DPO is much simpler to implement and train, and does not require any hyperparameter tuning. We also find that DPO is more stable than reinforcement learning from human feedback. In particular, we observe that DPO converges to the same policy as reinforcement learning from human feedback when the reward model is initialized with the same distribution as the policy. This is in contrast to reinforcement learning from human feedback where the reward model is typically initialized with a distribution that is different from the policy. We also find that the performance of DPO is not sensitive to the choice of reward model, and that the performance of DFO is not sensitive to the number of training samples used to train the reward model.

We also evaluate DPO on a variety of downstream tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. \section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language Models.} Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years. The most popular approach to language modeling is the Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) architecture, which was introduced in \cite{radford2018language}. GPT is trained on a large corpus of text data, and then fine-tuned to perform well on downstream tasks. GPT is widely used in various applications, including natural language generation, machine translation, and dialogue systems. Other popular approaches to language modeling include the Transformer-XL architecture \cite{liu2019transformer-xl}, the Transformer-Base architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention}, and the BERT architecture \cite{devlin2018bert}. These architectures are all based on the Transformer architecture, which is a feed-forward neural network with attention mechanisms. The Transformer architecture has been shown to be very effective at language modeling, and has been used in many successful applications.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning.} Reinforcement learning (RL) is a subfield of machine learning that deals with learning to make decisions in an environment through interaction with that environment. RL algorithms typically involve an agent that interacts with an environment, and learns to maximize a cumulative reward function. RL algorithms have been successfully applied to a wide range of problems, including robotics \cite{hassan2017deep}, computer vision \cite{silver2017mastering}, and natural language processing \cite{silver2018learning}. One of the most popular RL algorithms is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017proximal}, which is a stochastic gradient descent algorithm that is used to train policies that maximize a reward function. PPO is a powerful RL algorithm that has been used to train a variety of RL algorithms, including OpenAI's GPT-3 \cite{openai2020gpt-3} and OpenAI's GPT-J \cite{openai2019gpt-j}. PPO is also used to train LMs to perform well on downstream language tasks, including sentiment analysis \cite{liu2020learning}, summarization \cite{liu_2020_imitation}, and single-turn dialogue \cite{liu_dpo_2020}. However, PPO is a complex and often difficult-to-implement algorithm, and it is not clear how to train LMs using PPO without making significant hyperparameter tuning choices.
