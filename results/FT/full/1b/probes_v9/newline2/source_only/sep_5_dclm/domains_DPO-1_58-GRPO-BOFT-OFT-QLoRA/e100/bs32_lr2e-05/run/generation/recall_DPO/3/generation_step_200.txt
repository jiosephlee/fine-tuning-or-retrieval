\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm for fine-tuning a language model (LM) to align with human preferences. The key idea is to use a reward model to estimate the quality of model generations, and then fine-tune the LM to maximize the reward. This is similar to the approach taken in reinforcement learning from human feedback, but instead of using human feedback to train a reward model, we directly optimize the reward model to match the preferences of the human annotators. 

We propose a new parameterization of this reward model that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form, allowing for efficient optimization of the reward model. We refer to this method as \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO). DPO is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and does not require any hyperparameter tuning. We evaluate DPO on a variety of tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, and show that it can achieve comparable performance to existing methods while being substantially simpler to train and implement.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years. In particular, large pretrained LMs have shown impressive performance on downstream tasks, such as machine translation, question answering, and summarization. However, these LMs are trained on massive amounts of unlabeled data, and thus lack any explicit supervision of the downstream task. As a result, it is difficult to control the behavior of these LMs, and they often exhibit undesirable properties such as poor generalizability, and poor performance on downstream tasks. To address this problem, researchers have proposed various methods for fine-tuning LMs to improve their performance on downstream tasks. One popular approach is reinforcement learning from human feedback. In this approach, the human annotators provide feedback on the quality of model generations. The goal is to train a reward model that reflects these preferences, and then fine tune the unsupervised LM using RL to maximize the reward. The most popular method for doing this is reinforcement learning from human preference data (RLHF). In RLHF, the human annotators rate the quality of model generations on a scale from 0 to 1, and the goal is to train a model that maximizes the average rating of the human annotators.

One challenge with RLHF is that it is difficult to train a reward model with high accuracy. This is because the human annotators are typically not experts in the downstream task, and so their ratings are noisy and imprecise. Additionally, the human annotators may not be able to provide enough feedback to train a reward function that accurately reflects the preferences of the human users. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various regularization techniques to improve the performance of the reward model. For example, some methods use a prior on the reward function to regularize it, while others use a loss function that penalizes the reward function for being too far from the human preferences. Another challenge with RLHF is the complexity of the reward model. In order to train a reward model to reflect the preferences of the human user, the reward model must be able to capture the underlying structure of the preferences. This can be difficult, especially if the preferences are complex or noisy. To address this challenge, researchers have proposed various approximation methods for the reward model. For instance, some methods approximate the reward function with a neural network, while others use a kernel function to approximate the reward function. Additionally, some methods use a mixture of experts to approximate the reward function, where each expert is trained to reflect the preferences of a different group of human users. Despite these challenges, RLHF has been shown to be effective in improving the performance of LMs on downstream tasks.

In this paper, we introduce a new parameterized reward model for RLHF that enables efficient optimization of the reward function. Our parameterization is based on a reward function that is defined as a linear combination of the preferences of the human experts. This reward function is parameterized by a set of weights, which can be learned from the human feedback data. We show that the optimal policy for maximizing this reward function can be efficiently computed using a simple classification loss. This allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem with only a simple loss function, without requiring any sampling from the LM or performing significant hyperparameter optimization. We refer to this algorithm as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). D PO is stable, performant (in terms of training speed and memory usage), and computationally lightweight. We evaluate DPO in a variety of tasks, showing that it can achieve comparable or better performance than existing methods while being substantially easier to implement and train. In particular, we show that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs on sentiment analysis, summarization and single-turn dialog, and outperforms existing methods in all of these tasks. Additionally, we show that D PO can be used to improve the performance of LMs without significantly changing the underlying architecture, and can be used to improve performance on downstream tasks without requiring any additional training data.
