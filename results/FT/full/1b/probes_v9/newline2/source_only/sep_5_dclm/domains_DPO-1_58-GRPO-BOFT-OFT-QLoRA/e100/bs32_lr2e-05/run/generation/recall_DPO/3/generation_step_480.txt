\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Unsupervised Language Models}. Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has been a major focus of recent work in natural language processing (see \cite{li2022training,liu2023training,liu2024training} for surveys).  The most popular approach to training LMs is training them on large corpora of text data, typically with a few million tokens, and then finetuning them on smaller datasets of human-annotated data.  This approach has been shown to yield state-of-the-art performance on many downstream tasks, including machine translation, question answering, and summarization.  However, the training data required for this approach is often prohibitively large, and the annotation of such large datasets is expensive and time-consuming.  As a result, there has been growing interest in training LMs without any annotated data, by instead using unlabeled text data to train the model.  One popular approach to this end is to use neural machine translation (NMT) models to translate between different languages, and then use the resulting models to generate new text data.  This approach, known as unsupervised pretraining, has been shown to yield strong performance on downstream tasks, but it is also computationally intensive, requiring large amounts of parallel data to be translated between multiple languages.  Another popular approach is to use generative adversarial networks (GANs) to generate new text data from scratch, without relying on any pretraining data.  GANs have been shown to yield strong results on a variety of tasks, but they are also computationally intensive, and they require careful tuning of the parameters in order to avoid generating poor-quality text data.  In this paper, we focus on the task of training LMs without any labeled data, and we do so by using a large amount of unlabeled text data to generate new text data.

\textbf{Reward Learning}. Reward learning is a general framework for learning to control the behavior of an agent in an environment.  In the context of reinforcement learning, a reward model is used to estimate the value of each state-action pair in the environment, and the goal of the learning algorithm is to learn a policy that maximizes the expected total reward starting from a given initial state.  The most common approach to reward learning is to use a reinforcement learning algorithm, such as policy gradient or actor-critic algorithms, to optimize the reward model.  These algorithms typically require the use of a Monte Carlo sampling procedure, such as importance sampling or value iteration, to estimate the value of a given state-action pair.  However, these sampling procedures can be computationally expensive, especially for large environments or high-dimensional reward functions.  In this paper we focus on a different approach to reward learning, known as supervised learning, which does not require any Monte Carlo sampling.  In supervised learning, the reward model is trained using a supervised learning algorithm, such as gradient descent or stochastic gradient descent, to minimize the difference between the reward model and a reference reward model.  The reference reward model is typically obtained by sampling from a distribution of reward values, such as the empirical distribution of reward values in a large dataset of human-annotated rewards.  The goal of the learning algorithm in supervised learning is to minimize the difference between two reward models, one of which is the reward model learned from the training data, and the other of which is the reference reward model.  This approach to reward learning has several advantages over the Monte Carlo sampling approach.  First, it does not require any sampling procedures, which can be computationally expensive.  Second, it does not require the use of any special sampling techniques, such as importance sampling, which can be difficult to implement.  Third, it does not require that the reward model be sampled from a distribution, which can be difficult or impossible to obtain in some cases.  Fourth, it can be used to train reward models that are not necessarily continuous, which can be useful for modeling discrete rewards.  Finally, it can be used in combination with other learning algorithms, such as gradient descent, to improve the performance of the learning algorithm.  In this paper our focus is on the supervised learning approach to reward learning, and we will use the term reward learning to refer to this approach.
