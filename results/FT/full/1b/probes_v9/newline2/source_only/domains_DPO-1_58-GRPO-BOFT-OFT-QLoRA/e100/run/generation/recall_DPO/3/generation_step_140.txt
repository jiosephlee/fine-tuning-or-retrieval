\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. In this section, we will introduce the reward model fine-tuning problem and then describe DPO.

\subsection{Reward Model Fine-Tuning}

The goal of reward model fine-tuning is to learn a reward function $R_{\theta}(x)$ that reflects the human preferences for a given dataset of model generations $\mathcal{G} = \{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ generated by a large unsupervised LM $\mathcal{L}$. The reward function should be able to distinguish model generations with different levels of quality, e.g., a reward function $R_\theta$ that assigns higher values to better generations will encourage the fine-tuning algorithm to optimize the LM to produce better generations. 

Formally, we have a dataset of model generations $\cal{G} = (\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{D})$ where $\mathcal{L}$ is the LM and $\mathcal{D}$ is the human preference dataset. We assume that the human preferences are represented by a set of human judgments $\mathcal{D} = \{d_1, d_2, \cdots, d_n\}$ of model generations $\mathbf{x}^{(i)} \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})$. The goal is to learn a reward model $\mathcal{R} = (\mathcal R, \mathcal{F})$ such that $\mathcal{R}\colon \mathcal{F}(\cdot) \to \mathbb{R}$ is a function that maps a model $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal L(\mathcal{D}), \mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{y}$ to a real number $R(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ that reflects the quality of the model generation $\mathbf{x}$ compared to the reference model $\mathbf{y}$, i.e., $\mathcal{R}: \mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{L} \mid \mathcal{D}\right) \to \mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{d})$ where $\mathbb{R}(\cdot, \cdot)$ is a function that maps two real numbers to a real number. 

We assume that the human preferences $\mathcal{D}_0$ are available and known, but the human preferences $\mathbf{d}_0 \in \mathcal{\mathcal{D}}$ for a given model $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \cal{L}(\cal{D}_0)$ are not available. We want to learn a reward function $\mathcal{R}_0 = (\mathcal{R}_\theta, \mathcal{B}_0)$ that can be used to estimate the human preferences $\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_0 | \mathcal{R}_n(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{d}_n))$ for a given model generation $\mathbf{x}_n \sim \cal{L}$. The goal is to find the best reward function $\mathcal {R}_\theta$ that can be used to learn the human preferences $\hat {\mathbb{P}}$ from the available preference dataset $\mathcal {D}_{n+1}$.
