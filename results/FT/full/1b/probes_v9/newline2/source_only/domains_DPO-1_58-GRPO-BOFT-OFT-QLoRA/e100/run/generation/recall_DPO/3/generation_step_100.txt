\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the preference of a human annotator.  In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require any sampling from the LM during training, nor does it require any hyperparameter tuning.  Instead, DPO uses a simple classification loss to optimize the reward model, and then uses a standard RL algorithm to optimize the reward model to maximize the preference of the human annotator.  This allows us to solve the standard reward learning problem with only a simple loss function, and we find that this leads to a simple yet effective method for controlling the behavior of LMs.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Unsupervised Language Models}. Recent work has shown that large-scale unsupervised LM models can achieve impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including summarization, question answering, and sentiment analysis.  These models are trained on massive datasets of text and have billions of parameters, making them computationally expensive to train and evaluate.  However, the lack of supervision makes it difficult to understand how these models learn, and it is unclear how to effectively control their behavior.  We focus on the task of controlling the behavior of LLMs, and our goal is to gain steerability without requiring human annotations of the relative quality of generations.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback}. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RHLF) is a popular approach for gaining steerability in supervised learning.  In RHLF, a human annotator is shown multiple model generations and asked to select the best one.  The reward for each generation is then estimated by averaging the ratings of the human annotators.  The model is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward.  This approach has been successfully applied to a wide range of tasks including natural language understanding, machine translation, and dialogue.  However, RHLF is a complex procedure that requires annotating multiple generations of model output, and it is often difficult to find an appropriate reward function that accurately reflects the human preferences.  Additionally, RHLF is sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the number of generations shown to the human annotator, and the number of humans annotating the reward function.

\textbf{Reward Learning from Human Preferences}. Reward learning from human preferences is a popular approach for learning a reward function from human feedback.  In this approach, a human annotator provides feedback on the quality of model generations, and the reward function is optimized to maximize the average rating of the human annotators (e.g., \cite{liu2019learning,li2020learning,liu2021reward,wang2022training}).  This approach has been shown to be effective for learning reward functions from human preferences, but it is often difficult to choose an appropriate reward function that reflects the human preferences accurately.  Additionally, the reward function must be learned from scratch for each new dataset, which can be computationally expensive.

\textbf{Control of Large Language Models}. There has been significant progress in the field of controlling large language models.  In particular, PPO-based RL algorithms have been successfully applied to a variety of tasks, including dialogue, summarization, and question answering.  However, these algorithms require sampling from the LM during the training phase, which can be computically expensive for large models.  Additionally, PPO-based RL requires tuning a number of hyperparameters, such the number of episodes and the learning rate, which can be difficult to get right.  We focus on methods for controlling LLMs without sampling from the LM, and we find that DPO is a simple yet effective method that can be used to gain steerability without any sampling or hyperparameter tuning.
