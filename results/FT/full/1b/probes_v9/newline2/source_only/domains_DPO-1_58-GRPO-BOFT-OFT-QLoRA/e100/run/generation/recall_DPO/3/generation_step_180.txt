\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. In this section, we will introduce the reward model fine-tuning problem and then describe DPO.

\subsection{Reward Model Fine-Tuning}

The goal of reward model fine-tuning is to learn a mapping from a large unsupervised language model (LM) to a reward function that reflects the preferences of human annotators. This reward function is then used to train a policy that maximizes the reward. Formally, we have an unsupervised LM $M$ and a set of human preferences $\mathcal{P} = \{p_{\theta}(x)\}_{\theta \in \mathcal{P}}$ where $p_{\theta}(X)$ is a reward function that is learned from the data. The goal is to train a policy $\pi_{\phi}(x)$ that maps each data point $x$ to a reward $R_{\phi}(x; \theta)$. We assume that the reward function $R_{\phi}$ is a function of the LM $M$ and parameters $\theta$:

\begin{equation}
    R_{\phi}(M; \theta) = \underbrace{f_{\phi}(M, \theta)}_{\text{model-specific}}} + \underbrace{g_{\phi}(\theta)}_{\text{sensitive to $\theta$}},
\end{equation}

where $f_{\phi}$ is the model-specific reward function. The goal of reward model fine tuning is to learn $g_{\phi}$. We assume that the model-specific reward function $f_{\phi}(x, \theta)$ is known, but that the sensitive parameter $\theta$ is unknown. We can use human preferences $\mathcal P$ to estimate $\theta$ and then fine-tune the unsatisfied reward function $g_{\phi}$ to match the preferences. Formally, we want to train a policy $\hat{\pi}_{\phi}(\cdot | x)$ that maximizes the reward:

\begin{equations}
    \hat{\pi}_{\ph}(\cdot | x) = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{\pi \sim \hat{\pi}_{g_{\phi}}}\left[ R_{\phi}(x;\hat{\pi}(x)|\mathcal{D}) \right],
\end{equations}

where $\mathcal{D}$ is the training set, and $\hat{\pi}_{g_\phi}$ is the policy that maximizes the unsatisfied reward function:

\begin{equatio}
    \hat{\mathcal{P}}_{g_{\phi}} = \argmax_{ \pi} \mathbb E_{\pi \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[ g_{\phi}(\pi(x)|\mathcal{X})\right].
\end{equation}


\subsection{Existing Methods}

There are several existing methods for reward model fine-tuning. One popular method is Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences (RLHF). RLHF uses human preferences to train a reward model $g_{\phi}(x,\theta)$ and then fine-tunes the unsatisfied reward function using reinforcement learning. Formally, we start with an initial unsatisfied reward function $f_{0}(\cdot, \theta)$ and then train a policy $\hat{q}(\cdot | x, s)$ using a reward function $g_{0}(\cdot,\theta)$ that is defined as:

\begin{equatio}
    g_{0}(x, s, \theta) = f_{0}(x, \theta) + \alpha R_{hF}(x, s; \theta),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a learning rate that controls the importance of the human preferences. The policy $\hat{q}(x | s, \theta)$ is then trained using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward function $g_{1}(\cdot, \thet)$:

\begin{equaatio}
    \hat{q}(x|s, \theta) = \operatorname*{argmax}_{\hat{q}} \mathbb E_{s' \sim \cal D_s, s \sim \cal S, \theta} \left[ g_{1}(x, s,  \theta) - \alpha \mathbb E_{h \sim \cal H_f} \left[ R_{hF}(s, \hat{q}(s | s, h)) \right] \right].
\end{eequation}
