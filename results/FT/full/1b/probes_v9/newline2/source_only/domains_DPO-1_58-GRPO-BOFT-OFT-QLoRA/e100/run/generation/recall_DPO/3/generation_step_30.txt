\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Language Models.} Large-scale language models (LMs), such as BERT, GPT-3, and OpenAI's GPT-4, have been shown to be effective at capturing broad knowledge and reasoning skills. However, these models are trained on large amounts of unlabeled data, and thus are not directly interpretable. To address this, researchers have proposed various methods for extracting the underlying structure of LMs, including neural architecture search, neural architecture transfer, and neural architecture synthesis. These methods have been shown to improve the performance of LMs, but they are also computationally expensive and require significant hyperparameter tuning. In this paper, however, we focus on the more straightforward task of fine-tuning LMs to align with the preferences of humans.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning.} Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful method for learning to perform tasks in an environment. In RLHF, the goal is to learn a policy $\pi$ from human feedback that maximizes the reward function $R$. The reward function $R$, which is typically defined as the difference between a reward function $R^*$ and the reward of the original model, is used to train a policy that maximizes the reward. The reward function $r$ is typically defined as the expected discounted reward of the policy, where the discount factor $\gamma$ controls the importance of future rewards. The policy is then used to generate new samples from the LLM, and the reward of these samples is used to update the policy. This process is repeated until the policy converges to a stationary point. The most common RLHF method is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which uses a gradient-based optimization algorithm to update the policy. Other methods include Policy Gradient (PG), which uses a policy gradient algorithm to update the policy, and Value Iteration (VI), which uses a value iteration algorithm to update the policy.

\textbf{Human Preferences.} Human preferences are a powerful tool for controlling the behavior of LMs. In RLHF, human preferences are typically represented as a reward function $R$ that is used to train a reward model $R^*$ that reflects the human preferences. The reward model is then used to fine-tune the LLM using reinforcement learning. The most common reward function is the reward of the original LM, which is defined as the difference between $R^*$ and $R$. However, other reward functions have also been proposed, including the reward of the original human, the reward of the original user, and the reward of the human and the original user. In this paper, our goal is to find a reward function that aligns well with human preferences, without requiring significant hyperparameter tuning.
