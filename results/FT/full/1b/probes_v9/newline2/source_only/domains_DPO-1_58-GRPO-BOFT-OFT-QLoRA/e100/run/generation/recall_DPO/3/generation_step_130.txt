\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. It takes a pretrained unsupervised language model and an unlabeled dataset of preference annotations, and optimizes the language model to maximize the reward estimated from the preference annotations. This is a natural extension of reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the reward function is estimated from human preferences, and the goal is to optimize the language model to maximize this reward. We propose a new parameterization of this reward function, which allows us to solve the standard DPO problem in closed form, and thus to obtain an efficient algorithm for solving the original problem. We call this algorithm Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and it outperforms existing methods in several benchmarks.

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models have recently achieved impressive performance on many downstream tasks, including natural language understanding (Brown et al., 2021; Devlin et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020b; Radford et al., 2022), question answering (Devlin et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024), summarization (Radford et al., 2018; Vasudevan et al., 2025), and dialogue (Devlin et al. 2020; Vasudevan et 2025). However, these models are trained on massive datasets of unstructured text, and their behavior is difficult to control. For example, it is unclear how to ask a question in a way that elicits a high-quality answer, or how to generate a coherent summary of a given passage. In this paper, we focus on the problem of controlling the behavior of large unsupervised language models, and in particular, how to steer them towards desired responses to questions and prompts. This is a challenging problem because the language model is completely unsupervised, and the desired behavior is not explicitly specified by humans. We believe that a key to solving this problem is to find a way to represent the desired behavior in a way that can be learned by a language model. To this end, we introduce a new parameterized representation of the reward function in reinforcement learning, and use this to propose a new method for controlling language models, called Direct Preference Optimization (D PO).

The basic idea of DPO is to take a pretrained unsupervised LM and an unlabeled dataset containing preference annotations, and optimize the LM to maximize the reward estimated by the preference annotations. The reward function is parameterized by a neural network, and the goal is then to optimize the LM to maximize this reward function. This is a natural generalization of reinforcement learning from human preferences, where the reward function (the reward model) is estimated from human preferences. The reward function is used to optimize the language model, and the goal is either to maximize the reward (in DPO) or to minimize the difference between the reward and the reward estimated from the language model (in RLHF). The main difference between DPO and RLHF is that DPO does not sample from the language model during fine-tuning, and instead uses a simple classification loss to optimize the reward function. This makes DPO much simpler to implement and train, and we believe that it is more robust to hyperparameter tuning and initialization. We also note that DPO can be viewed as a special case of RLHF where the reward function is fixed and the language model is optimized to maximize the reward.

We formalize the problem of DPO as follows. Given a pretrained unsupervised LLM $M$ and an unlabeled dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ of preference annotations $\{y^{(i)}\}$ for the LLM, our goal is to optimize the LLM $M$ to maximize the reward function $R(\theta)$ defined as

\begin{equation}
    R(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[ \ell_R(\theta, x; y) \right],
\end{equation}

where $\theta$ is the parameters of the reward function $R$, and $\ell_R(\theta, y; x)$ is the reward assigned to the text $x$ by the LLM with parameters $\theta$. The goal is to maximize the reward, i.e.,

\begin{equations}
    \max_{\theta} R(\theta).
\end{equations}

We note that the reward function $R$ can be any function of the parameters $\theta$ of the LLM, and it can depend on the text $x$ and the preference annotations $y$. In practice, we typically use a neural network reward function $R(\cdot; \theta)$, where $\theta$ is the set of parameters of the reward function.
