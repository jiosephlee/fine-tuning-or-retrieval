\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs)} LLMs are large neural networks that have been trained on massive amounts of text data. They have been shown to be effective at many tasks, including natural language understanding, generation, and dialogue. However, LLMs are completely unsupervised, which makes it difficult to control their behavior. This is a major challenge, since it is difficult to obtain human feedback on the quality of LLM generations. One approach to gain steerability is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to train an LLM to maximize a reward function that reflects human preferences. This is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. There are several challenges in RLHF. First, the reward function must be learned from human feedback. Second, the reward function must then be used to train the LLM. Third, the reward function must not drift too far from the original LLM. These challenges are addressed by various methods, including policy gradient methods, actor-critic methods, and imitation learning. We focus on the policy gradient method, which is the most commonly used method in RLHF. Policy gradient methods optimize the policy, which is the probability of taking a particular action, using a loss function that is a gradient of the expected return with respect to the policy. The expected return is the sum of the discounted rewards obtained by taking actions in the environment. The policy gradient method is a powerful method for RL, but it is also computationally expensive. This is because the policy gradient method requires sampling from the LLM, which is expensive. In this paper, however, we propose a new method for solving the standard RLHF problem, which does not require sampling from the LLM. Our method, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), uses a new parameterization of a reward model that enables us to solve this problem with only a simple loss function. We will describe this parameterization in more detail in Section \ref{sec:rewardmodel}. We evaluate DPO on sentiment analysis, summarization and single-turn dialog in Section \ref{app:experiments}.

\textbf{Reward Learning} Reward learning is the process of learning a reward function from human feedback. There are several approaches to reward learning. One approach is to use reinforcement learning to train a reward model from human feedback. This is known as reward learning from human feedback, which is the approach we use in this paper. Another approach is to use a reward function that is already learned from human feedback. This approach is known as reward shaping. A third approach is to use a combination of reward learning and reward shaping. This approach is known generative reward learning. We focus on reward learning from human feedback in this paper. We use a policy gradient method to learn a reward function from human preferences. We use a simple binary classification loss to train the policy. We evaluate our method on several benchmarks in Section \ref{experiments}.
