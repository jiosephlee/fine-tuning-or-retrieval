\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has become a popular approach to building general-purpose language models (LMs). These models are trained on large corpora of unlabeled text, and are able to learn a wide range of language-specific knowledge, including word embeddings, syntactic structure, and semantic relations. However, the completely unsupervised training process makes it difficult to control the behavior of the resulting LMs, and they often exhibit undesirable properties such as overfitting, poor generalization, and poor performance on downstream tasks. In this paper, we propose a new method for controlling the behavior of LMs, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

DPO is based on the observation that LMs are often trained to maximize a reward function, which is a function of the LM's output. For example, the reward function for a language model trained to generate text that is similar to a given input might be defined as the cosine similarity between the LM's output and the input. In this case, the reward function is a function of the output of the LM, and the goal of DPO is to find the optimal policy for the LM that maximizes this reward function. This policy can then be used to fine-tune an unsuited LM, without the need for any sampling or hyperparameter tuning.

We show that DPO can be used to solve the standard RL problem, where the goal is to find the optimal action for a given state, and the reward function is the expected return of the action. In particular, we show that DPO can solve the standard RL problem with a simple classification loss, which is a function that takes the output of the LM as input and returns a scalar value that represents the quality of the LM's output.

We evaluate DPO on several tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.
