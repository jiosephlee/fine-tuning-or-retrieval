\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. In this section, we will introduce the reward model fine-tuning problem and then describe DPO.

\subsection{Reward Model Fine-Tuning}

The goal of reward model fine-tuning is to learn a reward function $R_{\theta}(x)$ that reflects the human preferences for a given dataset of model generations $\mathcal{G} = \{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ generated by a large unsupervised LM $\mathcal{L}$. The reward function should be able to distinguish model generations with different levels of quality, e.g., a reward function $R_\theta(x) = \|\nabla_{\theta} L_{\text{KL}}(\pi\mid x)\|$, where $L_{\text{KL}}, \pi$ are the log likelihood and probability of the LM given its parameters $\theta$, is the KL-divergence between the LM's prior distribution and the product of the prior distribution of word distributions and the LM's parameters, measures how much the LM's parameters deviate from being a uniform distribution over word distributions. We assume that the reward function is a function of the LM's parameters $\theta$ and not the full sequence of words $x$.

The learned reward function $R_\thetah(x)$ is then used to fine-tune the LM $\mathcal{G}$ to maximize the reward, i.e.,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:rewardfine_tuning}
    \max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{G}}[ R_\theta(x) ].
\end{equation}

The above optimization is typically performed using reinforcement learning (RL). In RL, the reward function $R_\theat(x)$ is estimated from human feedback, and the optimal policy $\pi_\thetah$ is then learned using reinforcement learning from human feedback. Formally, the reward model fine-tuned by RL is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \pi_\thetah = \underset{\pi}{\operatorname{argmax}} \mathbb{E}_x [ R_\thetah(x) \mid \mathcal{G}=\{x\}, \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\pi, y) ]
\end{equation}.

RL is a powerful method for learning policies from data, but it comes with several challenges. First, the reward function $ R_\thetah $ is estimated from human feedback which is expensive and time-consuming. Second, the reward function $ \pi_\thetar(x) $ is learned using reinforcement learning, which is an iterative optimization algorithm that requires a large number of training samples. As a result, the reward model fine tuning with RL is often computationally intensive and difficult to implement.

\subsection{Direct Preference Optimization}

In this section, we introduce a new parameterized reward model that enables the closed-form solution of the standard reward model fine-tuning optimization problem. This parameterization allows us to directly optimize the reward function $ R_{\theta}(x) $ without estimating it from human feedback, and therefore avoids the challenges of RL reward model fine-tuning. We start by defining the reward model for a single generation $ x^*$ as:

\begin{align*}
    R_{\theta}( x^*) &= \mathbb{E}[ \underbrace{q^\pi_\thetar(\cdot | x^*) \mid \mathcal G} _{\text{assumed}}\mid \mathcal{L}_{\text{unsupred}}] \\
    &\quad\mid \mathcal G = \{x^*\} , \mathcal{L}_h = \mathcal{L}\left(\pi_\thetar \mid \mathcal {G} = \{ x^*\} , y^*\right)\\
    &\qquad\qquad\qqua
    = \mathbb{E}\left[\underbrace{q^\thetar(\cdot \mid x^*) \mid\mathcal{G} \mid \underbrace{R_\thetar(x^*) \mid \text{supp}(\thetar)}_{\text{assumed}}}\right],
\end{align*}

where $\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{H}$ are the training and test sets of the LM, $\thetar$ is the LM's parameters, and $\text{supp} (\thetar)$ is the support of $\thetar$, i.e., the set of all words that the LM knows. The assumption that the reward function $ R$ is a function of the support of the LM's parameters is common in the literature and means that the reward function is invariant to the order of the words in the sequence $ x$.
