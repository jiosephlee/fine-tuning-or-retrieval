\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized to maximize the preference of the human annotators.  In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require any sampling from the LM during the fine-tuning phase, nor does it require any hyperparameter tuning.  Instead, DPO uses a simple classification loss to optimize the reward model, and then uses a standard RL algorithm to optimize the reward model to maximize the preference of human annotators.  This leads to a simple yet powerful framework for controlling the behavior of LMs.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-Scale Unsupervised Language Models}. Recent work has shown that large-scale unsupervised LM models can achieve impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and machine translation (see \cite{liu2022language} for a review).  However, these models are trained without supervision, and thus their behavior is completely uncontrolled.  This makes it difficult to use them in practical applications, since we cannot be sure that the model will behave as desired.  In this paper, we focus on methods for gaining steerability of LMs, i.e., methods for controlling their behavior so that it matches our desired preferences.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback}. One approach to gaining steerability is reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the goal is to learn a policy that maximizes the reward provided by a human annotator.  This approach has been successfully applied to a wide range of problems, including control of robotic systems (e.g., \cite{hassan2018learning, liu2019learning, liu2020human}), video games (e.g., \citet{silver2017discovering}), and text generation (e.g., \ref{thm:reinforcement_learning_text_generation}).  In reinforcement learning from human feedback the goal is to learn an optimal policy from a reward model that reflects human preferences.  This reward model is typically learned using a reinforcement learning algorithm such as Q-learning or actor-critic algorithms, and then used to train a policy that maximizes this reward.  The resulting policy is optimal in the sense that it maximizes the reward, but it may not be easy to implement or efficient to train.  In this paper we propose a different approach, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

\textbf{Reward Models}. A key challenge in reinforcement learning from human feedback is learning a reward model that accurately reflects human preferences.  In the context of text generation, one approach is to use a neural network to map text to a scalar reward value, and then train a policy using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward.  This approach has proven successful in many applications, including text generation (e.g. \cite{liu2019learning,liu2020human}), dialog (e.g. \citet{wang2021training}), and robotics (e.g. \ref{thm:singleturndialog}).  However, the reward model must be carefully tuned to reflect the preferences of the human annotators, and this tuning process can be difficult and error-prone.  In this paper our approach is to directly optimize the reward model to match the preferences of the human annotation dataset.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}

In this section we introduce the DPO framework for gaining steerability of large-scale unsupervised LMs.  Our goal is to learn a reward model that maximizes the preference of human annotator, and then use this reward model to train a policy that matches the desired behavior.  We start by defining the reward model that is typically used in reinforcement learning from human preference, and then describe how DPO can be used to optimize this reward model.

\subsection{Reward Model}

A common approach to learning a reward model for text generation is to use a neural net to map text to a single scalar reward value.  For example, \citet{liu2019 learning} use a single-layer neural network with ReLU activation to map text to a reward value, and then use reinforcement learning to train a policy that maps text to the highest reward.  Similarly, \citet{wang2019learning} use a single-layer convolutional neural network to map text and context to a reward value, while \citet{li2021learning} use a two-layer convolutional neural network.  These reward models typically use a pre-trained LM as the source text, but it is also possible to use a fixed text as the source text (e.g., \textit{I don't like this song}).
