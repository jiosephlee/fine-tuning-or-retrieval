\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. In particular, it takes a large unsupervised language model (LLM) and a dataset of preference labels, and optimizes a policy to maximize a reward function defined as the negative log-likelihood of the dataset under a specified parametrization of the LLM's distribution. This is different from existing methods that optimize a more general reward function, but we show that the reward function used by DPO can be efficiently optimized using standard reinforcement learning algorithms. We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our results show that DPO is competitive with existing methods for fine-tuning LLMs to match human preferences, and in some cases outperforms them. In particular, DPO is able to fine-tune LLMs to generate responses with higher quality than PPO-based RLHF, while being substantially simpler to train and less computationally intensive.

\section{Related Work}

Large unsupervised language models have achieved impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and machine translation \citep{chowdhery2020unsupervised, bai2020language, devlin-lee2020BERT, radford2020language}. However, these models are trained using only self-supervised learning algorithms, which do not explicitly reward or punish the model for performing certain actions or generating certain outputs. As a result, it is difficult to control the behavior of large unsupervised LLMs, since there is no objective function to steer the model towards or away from.

One approach to gaining steerability is to fine-tune an existing supervised model with a human-curated reward function \citep{wang2022training, li2022training, liu2022training, liang2022training, li2019learning, li2020training, li2021training, li2022learning, li2023training}. These methods take a supervised model and a dataset of human preferences, and optimize the supervised model to maximize the reward function defined as the expected return under the specified parametrization of its distribution. The hope is that the resulting fine-tuned model will be easier to control, since it is trained to maximize a well-defined reward function. However, the construction of these reward functions can be challenging, and existing methods typically rely on hand-crafted features or heuristics to define the reward function \citep{lai2022training, liyuan2022training, li_2023_training, li2023training, li2022learn, li2022training}. Moreover, these methods must be fine-tuned with a supervised learning algorithm, which can be slow and inefficient for large models.

Another approach to gaining steerability for unsupervised LLM is reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF \citep{schulman2017reinforcement, liu2022fine, li2022training,kim2022training}. In RLHF, a supervised LLM is fine-tuned with a reward function derived from human preferences, and then the reward function is optimized using reinforcement learning to maximize the expected return. This approach has the advantage of not requiring the construction of a reward function from scratch, but it still requires training an RL algorithm on a dataset of human preferences. This can be computationally expensive, especially for large models, and it can also be unstable, with the RL algorithm sometimes failing to converge or producing suboptimal solutions \citep{kim2022training, liyu2022fine, liu2022learning, liu2022reinforcement, li2022training,sun2023training}. Additionally, RLHF methods typically require sampling from the unsupervised LLM before fine-tuning, which can be inefficient and slow.

In this paper, we introduce a new approach to RLHF, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require sampling from the unsatisfied LLM before fine-tunning. Instead, DPO directly optimizes a policy to a reward function that is defined as the negative log likelihood of the unsatisfied LLM under a specified parametrizations of its distribution. We show that this reward function can be efficiently optimized using a simple classification loss, which allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem with only a simple loss function. Our experiments show that DPO is stable and performant, and in some cases produces results as good or better than existing methods.
