\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model for the LLM, which is used to optimize the LLM's behavior. The reward model is trained to maximize the reward function $R$ given the LLM's behavior $\mathbf{y}$, i.e., $R(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{y}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{D}}[R(\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{y}), \mathbf{y})]$.

The reward model is typically learned using reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback, where the reward function is estimated by maximizing the expected reward of the LLM's behavior, i.e., $L(\mathbf{y}) \sim \mathcal{T}_{\text{human}}(L(\mathbf{L}(y)), \mathbf{y}).$ 1

However, RLHF is an unstable procedure, as it requires a large number of human feedback samples to estimate the reward function, and the reward function is often not well-defined, especially for LMs that are not trained on human feedback. In addition, the reward function is often difficult to interpret, and it is unclear how to use it to optimize the LLM.

In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward learning problem with only a simple supervised classification loss. We refer to this parameterization as the \textit{direct preference model} (DPM). The DPM is a neural network that maps the LLM's behavior to a scalar value that represents the preference of the human annotators. The DPM is trained to maximize the expected reward of the human feedback, i.e., $DPM(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{L}^*(\mathbf{y})) \sim \mathcal{\mathcal{T}_{\rm human}}(DPM(\mathbf{l}^*(\mathbb{y})), \mathbb{y})$.

We show that the DPM can be used to solve the standard reward-learning problem with only a simple loss function, namely, the supervised classification loss, i.e., $J(DPM(\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{L}^\prime) = \mathbb{1}_{\mathbf{L} \sim \mathbf{L}} - \mathbb{1}_\mathbf{L^\prime}.$

We refer to this loss as the \textit{(DPM, supervised)} loss. We show that the DPM, supervised loss can be used to solve standard reward learning problems, including RLHF, with only a simple classification objective. We refer to this method as \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO).

We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. We find that DPO is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and outperforms existing methods in all benchmarks. In particular, DPO is able to fine-tune LMs with a small number of human feedback samples, and outperforms PPO-based RLHF methods in terms of both the quality of the generated text and the ability to control the sentiment of the LLM's generations.

However, the completely unsupervised training of LMs poses several challenges, including the difficulty of obtaining human feedback, the lack of a clear reward function, and the need for a large number of human samples to estimate the reward.
