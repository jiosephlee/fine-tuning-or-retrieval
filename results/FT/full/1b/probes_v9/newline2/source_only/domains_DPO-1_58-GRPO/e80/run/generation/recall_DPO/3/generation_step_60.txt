\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. In particular, it takes a large unsupervised language model (LLM) and a dataset of preference labels, and optimizes a policy to maximize a reward function defined as the negative log-likelihood of the dataset under a specified parametrization of the LLM's distribution. This is a general framework that can be applied to many different reward models, including reinforcement learning (RL) based ones. We will refer to this class of algorithms as \textit{Direct preference optimization algorithms} (DPOs).

In this paper, we introduce a new DPO algorithm, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Our main contribution is a closed-form solution to the DPO problem, which allows us to optimize the reward model using a standard gradient-based optimizer such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)  or AdamW. This is in contrast to most existing DPO algorithms, which use a custom loss function that requires sampling from the LLM, or perform significant hyperparameter tuning to select an appropriate loss function. We find that our algorithm outperforms PPO-based DPO on several benchmarks, including fine-tuning LLMs to align with human preferences for sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.

We also present a set of heuristics for selecting hyperparameters in DPO, which can significantly improve performance on real datasets. These include using a larger learning rate for the classification loss than for the reward function, and using a lower learning rate for the classification than the reward function. Our experiments suggest that these heuristics can be effective even when the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from the preference labels using a neural network. We also evaluate the impact of different initialization strategies for the LLM, finding that initializing with a pre-trained model can improve performance. Finally, we observe that DPO is robust to the choice of loss function used for optimization, and can be applied to a variety of different reward models, including RL-based models.

1. Introduction

Large unsupervised language models are capable of learning rich and useful knowledge, but their behavior is completely unsupervised, making it difficult to control their actions in a precise manner. For example, a LLM trained on Wikipedia may learn to answer questions about the webcomic xkcd, but it is difficult to ensure that it will also answer questions about the web series xkcdsucks. Similarly, a LLM trained to summarize news articles may learn to produce high-quality summaries, but it is difficult for humans to ensure that it also learns to summarize the main point of the article. In this paper, we will refer to these challenges as \textit{steering} and \textit{control}, respectively.

A number of recent works have proposed algorithms for steering LLMs, using either reinforcement learning (RL) or supervised learning. Reinforcement learning (RL) approaches fit a reward model to the preference labels, and then use RL to optimize a policy to maximize the reward. Supervised learning approaches fine-tune a pre-trained LLM with a dataset of preference labels. The key advantage of RL is that it provides a principled way to optimize the policy, but RL algorithms are generally more complex and difficult to train than standard supervised learning algorithms. In this paper, our goal is to develop a simple and effective RL-free method for steering LLMs.

One potential approach for RL-free steering is to use a reward model to estimate the human preference for each generation of the LLM, and then use reinforcement learning to optimize a policy that produces the preferred generation. However, existing methods for constructing reward models can be difficult to train and often do not generalize well to new data. In this paper, inspired by prior work on preference estimation from human ratings (Kiros et al., 2015; Koltun et al., 2016), we propose a new approach for estimating reward functions from preference data. Our approach is based on the observation that the preference labels can be viewed as a distribution over possible reward functions. Thus, we can construct a reward function that is consistent with the preference labels, and maximize this reward under the assumption that the true reward function is known. This approach is particularly simple because it does not require any modeling of the distribution over reward functions, but instead simply uses the preference labels as a proxy for the true reward function. We refer to this approach as \textit{Reward Consistency Estimation from Preferences} (RCEP), and describe it in more detail in Section 3. We refer to algorithms that optimize a reward function estimated in this way as \textit{RCEP algorithms}.

Once we have a reward function, we can use reinforcement learning to optimize the policy that produces the preferred output. However, existing RCEP algorithms typically use a complex reinforcement learning algorithm such as DDPG (Sutton and Barto, 2018) or PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), which are difficult to train and often unstable.
