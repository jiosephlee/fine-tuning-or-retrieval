\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. It takes an unsupervised language model and a set of preference data, and optimizes the language model to maximize a reward function defined over model generations, under a set of constraints. This reward function is typically a variant of the cross-entropy loss, but it can also be a more complex function that incorporates other information, such as the frequency of incorrect answers in response generation tasks. The optimization objective is a standard reinforcement learning objective, where the actor is the language model, and the reward function is the cross-entropy loss. The policy gradient for this objective is then used to update the language model.

The key idea of DPO is to reformulate the reward function as a function of the LM's latent state, and then use a policy gradient to optimize the LM. Formally, given an unsupervised LM $M$ and a set of preference samples $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ of model generations and human preferences, the DPO objective is

\begin{equation}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta} \sim \pi_{\theta}(x^{(1)}, y^{(1)}); \mathcal{D}} \left[ -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \pi_{M}(\cdot | x^{(1)})} \left[ \ell_{\text{cross-entropy}}(y^{(1)}, \pi_{\theta} x) - \mathbb{E}_y [r_{\text{ref}}(x, y; \theta) \mid x]\right] \right],
\label{eq:dpo-reward}
\end{equation}

where $\pi_{\theta}$ is the LM distribution, $\mathcal{D}$ is the set of preference samples, $r_{\text{reg}}$ is a regularization term, and $\ell_{\text{ref}, \text{cross-entropy, }}$ is the reference cross-entropy loss. In practice, we use the cross-entropy loss from the PPO algorithm \cite{schulman2017proximal} as the reference loss, which is a simple and effective baseline. The PPO loss is defined as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppo-loss}

Here $\pi_{\theta}^{(init)}$ is the initial policy, $\beta_{\text{steps}}$ is the weight of the recent experience, and $\pi_{\theta'}$ is the target policy. The $\mid \mid$ notation is used to denote the composition of policies, i.e., $\pi_{\theta}\mid \mid \pi_{f{thetaphil}}$.
