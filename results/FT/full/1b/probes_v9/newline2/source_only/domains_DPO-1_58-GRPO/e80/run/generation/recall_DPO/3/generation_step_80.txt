\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm. In particular, it takes a large unsupervised language model (LLM) and a dataset of preference labels, and optimizes a policy to maximize a reward function defined as the negative log-likelihood of the dataset under a specified parametrization of the LLM's distribution. This is different from existing methods that optimize a more general reward function, but we show that the reward function used by DPO can be efficiently optimized using standard reinforcement learning algorithms.

We evaluate DPO on two benchmarks for fine-tuning LLMs to align with human preferences: summarization and single-turn dialog. For summarization, we use the SQuAD 1.1 dataset \citep{glorot2017carrying}, which contains questions about short passages from the SQuAD dataset \citep{ranganathan2013squad}. We follow the protocol of \cite{wang2022training} and evaluate on the test set of SQuAD 1.x, using the same evaluation protocol as \cite{wang2020language}. For dialog, we use the MultiTurnQA dataset \citep{wang2022multi}, which contains multiple-turn questions and their corresponding answers. We follow the protocol of  \cite{wang2023training}, and evaluate on the test sets of both MultiTurnQA v1.1 \citep{wang2019multi-turn} and v2.0 \citep{wang et al.2022}, using the same evaluation protocol of \cite{welleck2018learning}. We also evaluate on the development sets of v1.1 and v2.0. For summarization, since \cite{wang2021training} uses a dataset of movie reviews, we use the corresponding dataset of news articles from SQuAD 1.X (which is identical to SQuAD 1.y except for the title and URL). For dialog, we use a dataset of questions and their corresponding answers from MultiTurnQA v1, which is the most recent version of MultiTurnQA and is the most closely related to the future version of MultiTurnQA v2.0. We follow the protocol and evaluation protocol of \cite{xiong2023training}, and use the same evaluation metric as \cite{xiong et al.2023}.

Table~\ref{tab:squadicp} shows that DPO is able to align the sentiment of generations generated by our LLMs with human preferences with high accuracy. In particular, DPO improves the sentiment of the final summarization generation by up to 4.5 points on the SQuAD  1.1 test set, and the sentiment of the final dialog generation by up to 2.5 points on the MultiTurnQA 1.1 test and 4.0 points on the MultiTurnQAv2.0 test. These improvements are statistically significant at the 0.05 level using a two-sample t-test. These results suggest that DPO is effective at controlling the behavior of LLMs with respect to human preferences, and aligning the sentiment of generations generated with high-quality responses.

Table~\ref {tab:squadicp2} shows that DPO can also improve the quality of responses generated by our LLM models. Specifically, DPO improves the average precision of the response by up to 2 percentage points on the MultiTurn QA datasets. This is particularly notable given that the MultiTurnQA datasets are evaluated using a self-paced evaluation protocol, which is known to be more lenient than a human evaluation protocol. We also find that DPO improves the response fluency of our models by up to 2% on the MultiTurnQA datasets. This is particularly interesting given that the MultiTurn QA datasets are evaluated using a strict human evaluation protocol, which is more stringent than the self-paced evaluation protocol used by \cite{xiong2013training}.

Table~\ ref{tab:squadicP2} also shows that DPO is more effective at improving the quality of responses than at improving the sentiment of the generation. This is because the response quality metric used in our experiments (i.e., the F1 score of the response) is more sensitive to small improvements than the sentiment metric used (i.e., the sentiment score of the generation). This is likely because the response quality metric is designed to be invariant under changes in the sentiment of the generation, while the sentiment metric is not. Nonetheless, we find that DPO improves both the sentiment and response quality of the generation with high accuracy, suggesting that DPO is generally effective at improving the quality and sentiment of the generation produced by our LLMs.
