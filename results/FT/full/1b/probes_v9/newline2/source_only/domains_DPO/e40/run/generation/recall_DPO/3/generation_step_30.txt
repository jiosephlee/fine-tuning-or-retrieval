\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model for the unsupervised language model (LM) that is used to optimize the LM's behavior. The reward model is trained to maximize the reward function $R$ given a sequence of observations $\mathcal{O} = \{o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_T\}$, where $o_t$ is the observation at time $t$ and $T$ is the length of the sequence. The reward function $R$ is typically a function of the LM's behavior, such as the log-likelihood of the LM's output given the observations $\mathcal{L}(o_t | \mathcal{O})$. The reward function $R(\cdot)$ is then used to optimize the LM, either by optimizing the LM's parameters $\theta$ or by optimizing the LM's behavior $\pi(\cdot | \theta)$.

The most common approach to optimizing the reward function $R(\theta)$ is to use reinforcement learning (RL). In RL, the reward function $R_{\text{RL}}$ is used to optimize the parameters $\theta$ of the LM, and the LM's behavior $\hat{\pi}(\cdot | \theta)$. The reward function $r_{\text{RL}}, \hat{\pi}_{\text{RL}}$, and $\theta$ are typically learned using a policy gradient method, such as policy gradient (PG) or value iteration (VI). The policy gradient method optimizes the LM's parameters $\hat{\pi}_{\rm PG}$, while the VI method optimizes the LM behavior $\hat{\pi}_{m VI}$.

The most common policy gradient method is PG, which is a variant of the actor-critic algorithm. In PG, the LM's parameters $\pi_{\rm PG}$ are optimized using the following objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pg}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}}(\pi_{\rm PG}, \theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{O} \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{unlabeled}}}\left[\log \pi_{\rm PG}(o_t | \theta)\right] + \lambda \left[\log \mathbb{E}_{o_t \sim \mathcal{T}_{\text{unlabelled}}}\left[\log r_{\text{RL}, \pi_{\rm RL}}(o_t | \theta) + \log \pi_{\text{RL, \text{VI}}}(o_t | \hat{\theta})\right]\right],
\end{equation}

The reward function $r$ is typically the log-likelihood of a LM's output given the observed data $\mathcal{O}$, but can be any function of the LM's output and the observations $\mathcal O$.
