\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm, but it has several advantages over existing methods. First, it is more efficient in terms of both training time and memory usage. Second, it is more stable, as it does not rely on sampling from the LM, which can be challenging to optimize. Third, it is easier to interpret, as the optimal policy can be easily obtained from the reward function. Finally, it is more general, as it does not require any assumptions about the structure of the reward function, such as being additive or having a single global maximum. In this paper, we introduce a novel parameterization of the reward function that allows us to efficiently optimize the corresponding optimal policy using a simple classification loss. We call this new algorithm Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and we provide extensive experimental evidence showing that it outperforms existing methods in terms of both performance and stability.

1. Introduction

Large unsupervised language models are increasingly capable of performing sophisticated tasks, such as reasoning (Gururangan et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020; Weston et al., 2001), summarization (Kopf et al., 1999; Manning et al.,  2018), and dialogue (Bai et al., 21t; Devlin et al., 1 2 2019; Ondreicka et al., 2 2 2021). However, despite their impressive capabilities, these models are trained in an entirely unsupervised manner, making them difficult to use in practice. For example, current models are typically too large to be deployed on a single machine, and their internal representations are largely inaccessible. This makes it difficult to understand how the language model makes its decisions, or to fine-tune it to perform additional tasks.

To address these challenges, a growing body of work has sought to gain some degree of steerability over the behavior of large unsupervised language models. One approach is to collect human preferences for the generated text, and then fine-tune the language model to maximize these preferences using reinforcement learning from human feedback, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback with preference optimization (RLHF; 2019; Li et al. 2021; Ondreickka et al., 3 2 2021; Riedmiller et al., 4 2 2021).

RLHF works by first fitting a reward model to the preferences of a group of human annotators, which is then used to optimize the language model to maximize the reward. While this approach has proven effective, it is non-trivial to optimize the reward model, and there is no guarantee that the optimal policy can be efficiently computed from the reward function alone. Moreover, the RLHF procedure is inherently unstable, as the choice of hyperparameters, such as the number of episodes and the learning rate, can significantly affect the final reward function and thus the optimal policy.
