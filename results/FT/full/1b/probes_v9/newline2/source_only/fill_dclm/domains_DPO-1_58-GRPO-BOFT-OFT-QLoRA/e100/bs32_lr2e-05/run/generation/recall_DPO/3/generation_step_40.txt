\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm that does not require sampling from the LM during training.  In this paper, we introduce a new parameterisation of the reward model that enables us to solve the standard reward maximization problem with only a simple cross-entropy loss.  This allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem with only a classification loss, without the need for any hyperparameter tuning or sampling from the LM.  We call this new algorithm \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO), and our experiments show that it is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, outperforming existing methods on both sentiment and summarization tasks, while being substantially simpler to train and implement.

\section{Related Work}

Large unsupervised language models have achieved impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and machine translation (see \cite{hulora2023} for a review).  Despite their impressive performance, these models are trained on very large datasets of unlabelled text, making them difficult to train and impossible to supervise.  As a result, existing methods for controlling the behavior of LMs typically rely on reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the human provides feedback on the quality of model generations, and the model is then fine-tuned to maximize the human feedback (see \cite{liu2023learning,liu2024training} for recent work in this area).

One challenge with RLHF is that the reward model used to optimize the LM must be learned from the human feedback, which introduces an additional layer of complexity.  The reward model is used to estimate the expected reward of each policy, and the policy with the highest expected reward is then fine-tuned on the human feedback.  This process is typically performed using reinforcement learning from human feedback with a reinforcement learning algorithm such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017).  However, RLHF is often unstable, with the fine-tuned policy drifting away from the original model (see \cite{wang2022training} for a study of this phenomenon).  Additionally, RLHF is computationally intensive, requiring the training of a separate reinforcement learning model for each human preference dataset, which can be prohibitively expensive for large models.

In this paper, we introduce an alternative approach to controlling LMs that does not require reinforcement learning from human feedback.  Our approach, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), uses a different parameterization of the reward function that allows us to solve the reward maximization problem with a simple cross-entropy objective.  This enables us to train a single policy that optimizes the cross-entropy objective, without the need for sampling from or fine-tuning the LM.  We refer to this policy as the \textit{direct preference policy}, since it directly optimizes the preference data that was used to train the reward model.  Our experiments show that the direct preference policy is stable, performs as well or better than existing methods for controlling LMs, and is computationally much simpler to train.
