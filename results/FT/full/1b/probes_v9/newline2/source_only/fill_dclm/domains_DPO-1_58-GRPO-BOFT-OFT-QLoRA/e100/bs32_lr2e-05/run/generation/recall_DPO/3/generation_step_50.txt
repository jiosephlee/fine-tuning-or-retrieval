\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model fine-tuning algorithm, but it has several advantages over existing methods. First, it is computationally efficient, requiring only a single forward pass through the LM for each iteration of the optimization. Second, it is stable, with empirical results showing that DPO converges to the same global optima as RLHF, but with fewer parameters and without the need for hyperparameter tuning. Finally, it is more straightforward to implement than existing methods, making it easier to integrate into existing workflows.

In this paper, we introduce DPO as a method for solving the standard RLHF problem. The RLHF problem is to train a reward model from human preferences for controlling the behavior of a large unsupervised LM. We then fine-tune the reward model with reinforcement learning to optimize a specified objective, such as maximizing the human preference score or minimizing regret against a reference model. In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not require sampling from the LM during the fine-tuning stage, nor does it require any hyperparameter tuning. Instead, DPO uses a simple classification loss to optimize the reward model parameters, and then solves the standard RLHF problem to fine-tune the LM using standard reinforcement learning techniques.

We evaluate DPO on two tasks: sentiment alignment and single-turn dialogue. On sentiment alignment, we compare DPO to PPO-based RLHF and to two other methods: a variant of the unsupervised LM fine-tuning method GPT-3 with Additional Instructions (GPT-3 WA) [1] and a variant of the LM fine-tuning method OpenAI's GPT-3 5B [2]. On single-turn dialogue, we compare DPO and PPO to two other methods: an adaptation of the unsupervised GPT-3 3B [3] fine-tuning method and a variant of the GPT-3 6B [4] fine-tuning method. All methods are evaluated on the task of generating high-quality responses to prompts from a given prompt and a given set of candidate responses. We use the HumanEval benchmark [5] for sentiment alignment and the CommonCrawl dataset [6] for single-turn dialogue. For both benchmarks, we use a subset of the test set that was not used during training to avoid relying on overfitting to the training data. Table 1 shows that DPO outperforms all other methods on both sentiment alignment and single-turn dialog, with the exception of the GPT-4-based method. This is particularly notable for DPO, which does not require access to GPT-4 or any other specialized models. Overall, our results suggest that DPO is a promising method for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences.

\paragraph{Sentiment Alignment} We evaluate DPO, PPO, GPT-3 WA, and GPT-3 7B on the task of sentiment alignment, where the goal is to generate a response that is rated as positive or negative by humans based on a given prompt and a candidate response. We use the same setup as in \citet{liu2022training}, where the goal is to minimize the average difference between the true sentiment and the human preference score for each candidate response. We use a 5-fold cross-validation setup, where the 5 folds are independently split into training and validation sets. Figure 1 shows the results. DPO consistently produces the most positive and the least negative responses, with the performance of PPO and GPT-3 WA following closely behind. This demonstrates the effectiveness of DPO as a method of fine-tuning LMs for steerability, and suggests that DPO may be a useful alternative to RLHF.

\paragraph{Single-turn Dialogue} We evaluate DPO and PPO on the task of generating responses to prompts from a single-turn dialogue dataset. We use the setup described in \citet{henderson2022training}, where we evaluate on the task of generating summaries of Wikipedia articles using GPT-4 7B and GPT-3 models fine-tuned with GPT-4 WA and GPT-3. We use a 3-fold cross-validation setup, with the 3 folds independently split into training and test sets. We use the same evaluation setup as in \cite{henderson20222nd}, where the goal is not to minimize the average difference in preference score between the generated summary and the reference summary, but rather to minimize the average difference of preference score between the generated response and the reference response. Figure 2 shows the results. D PO and PPO produce responses that are rated as more positive or more negative than the reference responses, with the performance following closely behind. This again demonstrates the effectiveness of D PO as a method of fine tuning LMs for steerability.
