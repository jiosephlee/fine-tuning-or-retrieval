\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free reinforcement learning (RL) method for training language models to align with human preferences. In contrast to existing methods, which typically fit a reward model to human preferences and then fine-tune the language model using reinforcement learning, DPO directly optimizes the language model to maximize a reward function derived from the preference data. This reward function is defined as the expected log probability of the preferred sequence under the language model, given by Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}. The optimization objective is then given by Eq.~ef{eq:main}, where $\pi_{\theta}(y|x)$ is the language model parameterized by $\theta$, $r(y, x^*)$ is the reward function, and $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r(y, x^*)]$ is the expectation over the preference data $\mathcal{D}$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    r(y, x^*) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}(x)}\left[\log \sigma(\mathcal{N}(y|\hat{f}(\theta), \boldsymbol{\beta}))\right],
\end{equation}

where $\hat{f}(\cdot)$ is the language model, $\boldsymbol{\beta} = [\beta_0, \beta_1]^T$ is the parameter vector, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function. The reward function is derived from the preference data by modeling the likelihood of the preferred sequence $y$ as a Gaussian mixture model with mean $\hat{f}^\ast(\theta)$ and variance $\beta_1^\top \mathbf{1}_y$, where $\mathbf{1}_x$ is the indicator function. The optimal reward function is given by Eq.~1 in the main paper, which is derived from the maximum likelihood estimation of the reward function under the assumption that the preference data is generated by a fixed reward function. This assumption is valid when the reward function is unknown, but it may not be valid when the reward function itself is unknown. In this case, we can use the reward function derived from the maximum likelihood estimate of the reward function, which is given by Eq. 2 in the main paper. This reward function is also derived from the maximum likelihood of the reward function under an assumption of a fixed reward function, but it is more straightforward to derive than Eq.~1.

\begin{equations}\label{eq:main}
    r(y, y^*) = \mathbf{1}_{y^* \neq y} \mathbb{E}_y[\log \sigma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathcal{N}_y(\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\beta}), \boldsymbol{\beta}_y^\top))],
\end{equations}

where $\mathcal{N}_x(\mu, \Sigma)$ is the normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\Sigma$. The optimal reward function is the one that maximizes the likelihood of the preference data, i.e., the one that minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the fixed reward function. This is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood of the fixed reward function under the assumption of a fixed reward model, which is the same as maximizing the likelihood of the reward function given the fixed reward model. The optimal reward function can be computed analytically, but it is computationally expensive to compute. Therefore, we use the reward function derived by maximum likelihood estimation, which is given by Eqs.~3 and 4 in the main paper.
