\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a straightforward extension of the standard RLHF framework, which optimizes the reward model learned from human preferences to maximize the expected return of the optimal policy derived from the reward function.  In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of this reward function that allows us to efficiently optimize it using a standard classification loss, thereby enabling the use of DPO to train LMs to align with user preferences.  Our experiments show that DPO is both stable and effective at fine-tuning LMs to align with preferences, outperforming existing methods on sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive advances in natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [1], GPT-3 [2]). However, these models are trained on vast quantities of text, and their behavior is largely uncontrolled. While they may be able to answer questions or generate coherent text, they may also produce outputs that are nonsensical, offensive, or otherwise undesirable. This poses a challenge for downstream applications that require precise control over the behavior of LMs, such as generating high-quality responses to queries in dialogue systems or producing high-concept summaries of articles in news applications. In this paper, we focus on the problem of aligning the behavior of large-scale LMs with human preferences, a task known as preference modeling or preference learning.

Existing approaches to aligning LMs with human preferences typically involve collecting human judgments of the quality of model outputs and fine-tuning the unsupervised LM using a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm that maximizes the reward derived from the human preferences. The most common approach is reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, which trains an RL agent to maximize the reward obtained from a human annotator by selecting model outputs that are preferred by the annotator. This reward function is typically modeled as a function of the LM’s parameters, and the RL algorithm optimizes the parameters of the reward function to maximize the expected return from the optimal policy derived from this reward function. This is equivalent to solving the following optimization problem:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward}
    \max_{\theta} R(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta} \left[ \text{value}(\pi_\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \right],
\end{equation}

where $\pi_\theta$ denotes the probability distribution over model outputs induced by the parameters $\theta$, $\mathcal{D}$ is the dataset on which the rewards are collected, and $value(\pi_\theta \vert \mathcal{D}, y)$ is the value function of the policy $\pi_\theta$ given the dataset $\mathcal{D},$ the label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ indicating whether the model output is preferred or not, and the reward function $R(\theta)$. The optimal policy $\pi^\ast_\theta$ is the one that maximizes the value function, i.e., $\pi^\ast_\thetahat=\argmax_\theta value(\pi_\thetahat \mid \mathcal D, y)$.

The optimal reward function $R^\ast$ is typically estimated from human preferences by applying a machine learning algorithm such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or neural network-based methods like neural Turing machines (NTMs) [3] or GPT-4 [4]. Once the optimal reward function $R^*$ is estimated, the RLHF algorithm optimizes the parameters $\theta$ of the LM to maximize the reward $R^*(\theta)$. This is equivalent to solving:

\begin{equations}\label{eq:RLHF}
    \max_{ \theta} R^*(\theta) - \beta \mathbb{E}_\theta \bigl[R(\theta) - R^*(\theta)\bigr],
\end{equations}

where $\beta$ is a hyperparameter controlling the strength of the regularization term. This optimization problem is generally infeasible to solve exactly, so it is common to use a gradient-based method such as proximal policy optimization (PPO) [5] to find a local optimum. However, the PPO algorithm requires sampling from the LM’s distribution, which can be computationally expensive for large models. Alternatively, some methods use a reinforcement learning algorithm that directly optimizes the reward function $R^*(\thetahat)$, such as the policy gradient method used in the GPT-4 RLHF algorithm [4].

In this paper, we propose an alternative approach to aligning LMs using preference models. Instead of optimizing the reward function $R^\*$ to maximize the reward obtained by the optimal policy, we optimize the reward function $R$ to maximize the reward obtained under the optimal policy.
