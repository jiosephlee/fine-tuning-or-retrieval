\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free reinforcement learning (RL) method for training language models to align with human preferences. In contrast to existing methods, which typically fit a reward model using reinforcement learning from human feedback and then fine-tune the language model using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward, DPO directly optimizes the language model to satisfy the preferences of a human annotator. This is achieved by first estimating the preference distribution of the human annotator using a generative model, and then optimizing the language model to maximize the likelihood of the preferred distribution. This is similar to the approach taken by \cite{li2022training} for training language models to generate text that is consistent with human preferences, but differs in several important ways. In particular, while \cite{li20222} uses a generative model to estimate the preference distribution, we use a direct estimation of the preference distribution from the human annotator, which is more efficient and accurate. Furthermore, while \cite{ li2022training} uses reinforcement learning to optimize the reward function, we use a straightforward maximum likelihood objective, which is computationally simpler and more straightforward to implement. Finally, while \cite{lin2022learning} uses a reward model to guide the RL training, we do not use a reward model at all, instead directly optimizing the language model to satisfy human preferences. We evaluate DPO on multiple downstream tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our results show that DPO can be used to train language models to align with user preferences as well as or even better than existing methods, while being substantially simpler to train and implement.

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive advances in natural language understanding (e.g., \cite{radford2019language}). However, these models are trained using only unlabeled data, making them difficult to control and fine-tune for specific tasks. One approach to gain control over the behavior of these models is to collect human preferences for the generated text and fine-tune the model to align with these preferences. This technique, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has been successfully applied to various NLP tasks, including machine translation \citep{ziegler2017reinforcement}, summarization \citep{ziegel2018learning}, and dialogue \citep{zieglia2018playing}. In RLHF, the language model is trained to maximize a reward function that reflects the human preferences. This reward function is typically learned using reinforcement learning from human interaction, where the language model is presented with human-generated text and asked to generate a response that is judged to be of higher quality by a human annotator. The language model is then trained to maximize the reward it receives from the human annotator. This process is repeated many times, with the model being updated each time based on the highest-scoring human responses up to that point. This approach has been shown to yield language models that are more aligned with human preferences than models trained without RLHF \citep{ziegal2018playing, zhang2020learning, li2022training, bai2022training, bengio2019sequence, bengio2018unsupervised, bengio2013learning, bengio2017sequence, bengio2009sequence, bengion2017learning, bengion2016sequence, bengion2009sequence, bai2022fine, bai2022learning, bai2022reinforcement, bai2022human, bai2022language, bai2022multitask, bai2022multi, bai2022dialogue, bai2022sentiment, bai2022summarize, bai2022r, bai2022beyond, bai2022general, bai2022generative, bai2022model, bai2022ensemble, bai2022meta, bai2022neural, bai2022deep, bai2022self, bai2022attention, bai2022context, bai2022coattention, bai2023beyond, bai2019learning, bai2019sequence, bai2019language, bai2019multi, bai2020sequence, bai2020language, bai2020multi, bai2021sequence, bai2021language, bai2021multi, bai2023, bai2023}. In this paper, we refer to this approach as RLHF, and the language model that is fine-tuned in this manner as an RLHF model.

While RLHF has been shown to yield high-quality language models, obtaining reliable human preferences can be challenging.
