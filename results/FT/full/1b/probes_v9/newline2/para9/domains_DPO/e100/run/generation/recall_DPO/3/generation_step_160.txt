\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free reinforcement learning (RL) method for training language models to align with human preferences. Existing methods for obtaining such preferences typically involve collecting human judgments of the relative quality of multiple model generations, and fine-tuning the largest model to maximize the average reward across these preferences. This approach, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has been shown to yield high-quality language models, but is challenging to implement and often unstable. In this paper, we present a new method for training language models using only a single preference dataset, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO trains a policy from preference data by minimizing a loss that is a convex combination of the negative log-likelihood of the preference dataset and the negative log-likelihood under the optimal policy derived from a reward model. We prove that the DPO loss is equivalent to a reward model-free RL objective that can be solved exactly, and demonstrate that it yields language models that are as effective as those obtained using RLHF with a reward model. Our experiments show that DPO is stable, efficient, and comparable to existing methods for training language models to meet human preferences, including reinforcement learning from human feedback and policy gradient methods. In particular, DPO fine-tunes language models to generate responses that are as effective as or more effective than those generated by PPO-based RLHF, using only a single preference training dataset.

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive advances in natural language understanding (e.g.,  BERT [Devlin et al., 2018], GPT-3 [Sutskever et al., 2020], XLNet [Yang et al., 2019]). However, these models are trained on massive datasets using only self-supervised learning, which makes them difficult to control and fine-tune for specific tasks. For example, it is challenging to fine-tune large unsupervised LMs for sentiment analysis, summarization, or dialogue, because the only available supervision is the output of the model itself. In this work, we investigate how to obtain steerability for large unsupervised LLMs, i.e., how to train a language model to meet human preferences, without relying on human annotations or reinforcement learning. Our goal is to develop a method that can be applied to existing large-scale unsupervised LMs, such as GPT-4 [Brown et al., 2022] and OpenAI's GPT-J [Sutskever and Williams, 2022], to improve their performance on downstream tasks while maintaining their generalization capabilities.

To address this challenge, we propose a new method for training LMs to meet human preferences, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (D PO). Our method is based on a reward model-free reinforcement-learning objective that we derive from a reward function learned from preference data. The reward function captures the human preferences for model outputs, and the reinforcement learning objective optimizes the language model to maximize the reward while avoiding a specified set of policy actions, which correspond to model outputs that are not preferred by humans. This objective is similar to the one used in previous methods for training language models using human preferences, such as RLHF [Chen et al., 2021] and its variants [Bai et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025]. However, our method does not require sampling from the language model during training, and can be applied to existing LMs without any modifications. Our experiments show that the DPO objective yields language models that are just as effective as those obtained by RLHF with a reward function, but with significantly fewer hyperparameters and computational requirements.

Related Work
Large-Scale Unsupervised Language Models. Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been achieved through self-supervised learning, where the model is trained on unlabeled text and achieves human-level performance on downstream tasks such as machine translation [Devlin et al. 2018;Radford et al., 2015;Sutskever et  al., 2014;Graves et al., 2016;Liu et al., 2017;Le and Mikolov, 2018;Radley et al., 201 2019;Peters et al., 2014]. These models are trained on massive amounts of text, but are not explicitly supervised on any task.
