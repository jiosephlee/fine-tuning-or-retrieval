\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive advances in natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [1], GPT-3 [2]). However, these models are trained on vast quantities of text, and their behavior is largely uncontrolled. While they may be able to answer questions or generate coherent text, they may also produce outputs that are nonsensical, offensive, or otherwise undesirable. This poses a challenge for downstream applications that require precise control over the behavior of LMs, such as generating high-quality responses to queries in dialogue systems or producing high-concept summaries of articles in summarization tasks. In this paper, we focus on the latter application, where the goal is to fine-tune an LM to produce high-quality responses to queries, while maintaining the ability to generate novel text. We refer to this task as \textit{response alignment}, and it is closely related to the broader area of \textit{language generation}.

A common approach to response alignment is reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. In this method, the model is first trained on a large quantity of text, and then fine-tuned to produce responses that are preferred by a human annotator. The reward function used to train the model is typically based on the human preference data, and is optimized to maximize the expected return from the optimal policy learned from the reward function. This policy is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward without drifting too far away from the original model. The RLHF framework has been successfully applied to various language generation tasks, including summarization [3, 4], dialogue [5, 6], and question answering [7, 8]. However, RLHF is a challenging and often unstable procedure, as it requires collecting large quantities of human preference data, and fine-tuning the model using reinforcement learning, which is computationally expensive and often requires extensive hyperparameter tuning.

In this paper, we introduce a new method for fine-tuning LMs that does not rely on reinforcement learning. Our method, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or DPO, is based on a simple reparameterization of the reward function used in RLHF. By reparameterizing the reward function in a way that allows us to express the optimal policy in closed form (as a function of the parameters of the reward function), we can efficiently optimize the reward function using a standard classification loss. This enables us to train LMs using a straightforward supervised learning procedure, without the need for sampling from or fine-tuning the LM during training. Our experiments show that D PO can be used to fine-tune LMs with comparable performance to existing methods, while being significantly simpler to implement and train.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervized language models have been trained on massive datasets of text, including the Google News dataset [9] and the Gigaword corpus [10]. These models have demonstrated impressive capabilities in tasks such as summarization [11], question answering [12], and machine translation [13]. However, these models are not controlled, and their behavior is often unpredictable. In this paper, our focus is on the task of fine-tuning these models to produce high-quality responses in response to queries. This task is closely related to the area of language generation, and previous work has explored methods for fine-tuning LLMs to generate high-quality responses to queries. One approach is to use reinforcement learning to train a policy from human preferences, and then use this policy to generate responses. This method is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback or RLHF. Another approach is to train a model directly from raw text, and then fine tune this model to produce high-quality responses. This method is referred to as supervised learning from text or SFT. A third approach is to train a language model from scratch, and then fine-tune it to produce high-quality responses using a technique such as prompt-based fine-tuning (PBT) [14]. PBT is a supervised learning method that uses a human-written prompt to guide the fine-tuning process, but does not require access to human preferences. In this paper, however, we focus on the RLHF approach, as it is the most widely used method for fine-tuning language models to produce high-quality outputs.
