\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model fine-tuning (e.g., BERT [1] or GPT-4 [2]) has led to impressive results in downstream tasks such as machine translation [3], summarization [4], and dialogue [5]. However, these models are trained on vast quantities of text, and their behavior is largely governed by the underlying unsupervised training objectives. While these objectives have been shown to be effective at training high-quality LMs, they are not always aligned with human preferences, and it is unclear how to fine-tune them to produce responses that are more desirable to humans. In this work, we investigate the problem of fine-tuning an unsupervised LM to match human preferences, and propose a new method for doing so that we call Direct Preference Optimization (D PO). Our method, which we refer to as DPO, is based on a simple yet powerful idea: instead of optimizing the unsupervised LM directly, we can optimize a separate reward model that reflects human preferences, and then use this reward model to train a policy that matches the preferences. This approach is similar to previous methods that optimize a reward model derived from human preferences, but our method is more general and does not require any additional assumptions about the reward function.

In particular, we propose a new way to represent the reward function that allows us to efficiently train a policy using a standard classification loss, rather than the more complex reinforcement learning objective used in previous methods. Our method, which we call DPO, is straightforward to implement and computationally lightweight, and we show that the resulting policy can be used to fine-tune an unsupervised LM using a simple classification loss. Our experiments demonstrate that the policy learned by DPO can fine-tuning LMs to align with preferences as well as or even better than existing methods, while being significantly simpler to implement and train. Our contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
\item We propose a new method for optimizing a reward model derived from preferences, and show that this can be used to train a policy using a simple classification loss.
\item We evaluate DPO on a range of downstream tasks, including summarization, single-turn dialogue, and sentiment analysis, and show that it can fine tune LMs as effectively as existing methods, while being simpler to implement and train.

\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervsed language models (LMs), such as BERT [1] and GPT-4 [6], have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks. However, these models are typically trained using large quantities of unlabeled text, and their behavior can be largely governed by the underlying training objectives. While these training objectives have been shown to produce high-quality LMs, it is unclear how to optimize them to produce responses that align with human preferences. Prior work has explored various ways of fine-tuning LMs using human preferences, including reinforcement learning from human feedback [7, 8], supervised learning [9, 10], and generative adversarial networks [11, 12]. In this paper, we focus on reinforcement learning from human feedback, which has been shown to be effective for fine-tuning LMs [13, 14]. In this setting, the goal is to train a policy $\pi$ that maximizes the reward $r(\cdot|\theta)$ given a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ of pairs $(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})$ drawn from the environment, where $\theta$ represents the parameters of the policy. The policy is optimized using reinforcement learning, typically through a policy gradient method such as PPO [15]. However, RLHF is a challenging problem, and previous methods have had to make several assumptions about the reward function, including that it is a valid function of the policy parameters [16, 17], that it is differentiable [18], and that it is convex [19].

In this paper, we propose an alternative approach to fine-tuning LMs that does not require any of these assumptions. Instead of optimizing the policy directly, we optimize a separate reward model $r(\cdot|\pi)$, which reflects human preferences.
