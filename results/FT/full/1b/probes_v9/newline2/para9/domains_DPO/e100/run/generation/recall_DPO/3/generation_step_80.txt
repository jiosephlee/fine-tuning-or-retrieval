\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a straightforward extension of the standard RLHF framework, which optimizes a reward function $r$ to align with human preferences. In the standard RLHF setting, the reward function is defined as the negative log-likelihood of the human preference data, i.e., $r(\pi_\theta(x) \mid y) = -\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$, where $\pi_\theta$ is the LM's policy and $y$ is the set of human preferences. The goal is to find a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the reward $r(\pi_\thetap(x) \mid y)$, which is equivalent to minimizing the negative log-likelihood loss $L_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \mid \mid \pi_\theta^* \mid y)$. This objective is commonly referred to as the \textit{reward model}.

In the standard RLHF setting the reward model is learned from preference data by maximizing the likelihood of the preference data given the reward model, i.e., $\pi_\theta^* = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}[r(\pi_\thetas(x) \mid y)]$.

In contrast, DPO directly optimizes the reward model to maximize the likelihood of the preference dataset given the reward model, $\pi_\theta^*(y \mid r) = \argmax_{p\mid \pi_\theta} \mathbb{P}_{x \sim \cal{D}}[y \mid r(\pi_\thetas (x) \mid \mid p)]$. This is equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy loss between the reward model $r$ and the true reward function $r^*$, i.e., $L_{\text {DPO}} = \mathbb{E}_p[\log \frac{r(\pi_\theta (x) \mid y)}{r^*(y \mid x)}}]$. This loss is commonly referred to as \textit{direct preference optimization} (DPO).

The DPO objective is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood of the reward model given the preference data, $\pi_\thetas^*(y \mid \mid r) =\argmax_{p\sim \mathcal{P}} \mathbb{E}[y \mid r(\thetas (x) ) \mid p]$, where $\mathcal{P}$ is the set of reward models. This is equivalent to minimizing $L_{\text DPO}$, which is the negative log-likelihood objective of the reward model given preference data, i.e. $L_{\textDPO} = \mathbb{ E}_p[\log \mathbb{P}(y \mid r(\theta (x) ))]$. This loss is commonly called the \textit{direct reward model}.

The DPO objective is identical to the standard RLHF objective, except that the reward model is optimized directly rather than via the likelihood of the reward data. This change has several advantages. First, the DPO objective is more straightforward to optimize than the standard RLHF objective. Second, the DPO objective can be optimized using a simple classification loss, whereas the standard RLHF objective requires sampling from the reward model and optimizing the policy using reinforcement learning. Third, the DPO objective does not require any hyperparameter tuning, unlike the standard RLHF objective which must be tuned to balance the reward model's ability to align with the human preferences and the policy's ability to generalize to unseen data.

\section{Related Work}
Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has been shown to yield powerful language models that can perform a wide range of tasks, including summarization, question answering, and dialogue. However, these models are trained using an unsupervised objective that is completely unknown to the model's developers, making it difficult to control the behavior of the resulting language model. In this section, we review the existing approaches for controlling the behavior of large-scale unsupervised LMs, focusing on methods that optimize the reward model.

\textbf{Reward-based methods.} Several methods have been proposed to control the behavior of large-scale LMs by optimizing the reward model. These methods typically involve collecting human preference data and fine-tuning the unsupervised LM using a reward model that is learned from the preference data. One common approach is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize the reward model, which is referred to as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. In RLHF, the reward model is learned using preference data collected from humans, and the reward function is optimized using reinforcement learning to align with the human preference data. The reward model is typically learned using a neural network, and the reinforcement learning objective is commonly a policy gradient objective, such as the policy gradient with a discount factor (PGD) or policy gradient with a value function approximation (PPO).
