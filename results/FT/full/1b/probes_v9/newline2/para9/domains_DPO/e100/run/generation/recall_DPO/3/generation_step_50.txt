\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a variant of reinforcement learning (RL) that uses a reward model to optimize the policy of a large unsupervised language model (LM). The reward model is trained to reflect the preferences of a human annotator, and the policy is optimized to maximize the reward. This approach has been shown to be effective in improving the quality of language models, but it is challenging to train a reward model that accurately reflects the preferences of a human. In this paper, we propose a new method for training a reward model, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), that is more stable and efficient than existing methods. Our method is based on a simple yet powerful idea: instead of optimizing the reward function directly, we optimize a simpler function that approximates the reward function. We show that this simpler function can be used to train a reward model with a closed-form solution, which allows us to solve the standard reward maximization problem with a simple classification loss. We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, and show that it can improve the quality of language models compared to existing methods.

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model training has led to impressive improvements in language understanding and generation capabilities. However, these models are trained using a completely unsupervised objective, which makes it difficult to control their behavior. For example, it is difficult to ensure that a language model does not produce offensive or inappropriate content, or that it does not generate text that is too long or too short. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various methods for fine-tuning language models to align with human preferences. One popular approach is reinforcement learning from human feedback, which involves training a language model to maximize a reward function that reflects the preferences of a given human annotator. This approach has been successful in improving the quality of large language models, but it can be challenging to train a reward function that accurately reflects the preferences. In this paper, our goal is to develop a new method for training reward models that is more stable and easier to implement than existing methods.

To motivate our approach, consider the following scenario. Suppose we have a large language model that we want to fine-tune to align with human preferences, and we have access to a human annotator who can provide feedback on the quality of the generated text. We can use the human feedback to train a reward function, which we can then use to optimize the language model. However, the human annotator may not be able to provide feedback on every single sentence in the training data, so we need to sample sentences from the training data and use the human feedback to fine-tune the language model on these sampled sentences. This process is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback. The challenge is that the human annotator may have different preferences for different sentences, so we need to train multiple reward functions and use them to optimize the language model.

One way to address this challenge is to use a reward model that is trained to reflect the human preferences of a single annotator. This approach is known as reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). In RLHF, the language model is trained to maximize a reward function, which is defined as the difference between the reward of the language model and the reward of a reference model. The reference model is typically a baseline language model that is trained on the same data as the language model, but without access to human feedback. The language model is then trained to maximize the reward function, which is equivalent to maximizing the reward of the reference model minus the reward of the language models. This approach has been successfully used to improve the quality of large language model, but it can be difficult to train a reward function with a closed-form solution. In this paper, instead of optimizing the reward directly, we optimize a simplified version of the reward function, which we call the preference function. We show that the preference function can be used to optimize the language model with a closed-form expression, which allows us to train the language model using a simple classification loss. This approach is more stable and easier than existing methods, and it can be used to train reward functions that accurately reflect the preferences of a single human annotator.

We evaluate our method on several benchmarks, including summarization, sentiment analysis, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is able to improve the quality of language model compared to existing methods, and it is also more stable and easier to train than existing methods. In particular, DPO is able to achieve comparable or better results than PPO-based RLHF, while requiring fewer parameters and being significantly easier to implement. We also compare DPO to other methods for fine-tuning large language models, including reinforcement learning from human feedback and reinforcement learning with human feedback. Our experiments show that while DPO is not as effective as RLHF, it is still able to improve the quality and stability of language models compared to baselines. Overall, our results suggest that DPO is a promising approach for improving the quality of large-scale language models.
