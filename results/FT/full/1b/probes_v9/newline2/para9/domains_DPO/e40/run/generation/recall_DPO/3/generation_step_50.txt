\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a variant of reinforcement learning (RL) that uses a reward model to optimize the policy of a large unsupervised language model (LM). The reward model is trained to reflect the preferences of a human annotator, and the policy is optimized to maximize the reward. This approach has been shown to be effective in improving the quality of the generated text, but it is challenging to train a reward model that accurately reflects the preferences of the human annotator. In this paper, we propose a new method for training a reward model, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), that is more stable and efficient than existing methods. DPO uses a simple classification loss to train the reward model, which is then used to optimize the policy of the LM. We show that DPO can be used to train a reward model with high accuracy, and that the resulting reward model can be used to optimize the policy with a simple classification loss. We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is able to improve the quality of the generated texts compared to existing methods, and that it is more stable and efficient to train than existing methods.

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model training has led to the development of powerful models that can generate fluent text with high quality. However, these models are trained using a completely unsupervised objective, which makes it difficult to control their behavior. For example, it is difficult to ensure that the generated text is coherent, or that it is appropriate for the context. In this paper, our goal is to develop a method for controlling the behavior of large-scale unsupervised LMs, by optimizing the policy of the LM to maximize a reward function that reflects the preferences of a human. This approach is similar to reinforcement learning (RL), where the goal is to maximize a reward function, but in this case the reward function is learned from human feedback. This approach has been successfully applied to many domains, including image captioning (Krizhevsky et al., 2012), image classification (Sutskever et al., 2014), and summarization (Bai et al., 2016). In this paper, we focus on the task of controlling the behavior of large unsupervised LMs.

\subsection{Related Work}

There are several approaches for controlling the behavior of LMs. One approach is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize the policy of an unsupervised LM. This approach has been used to improve the quality of generated text (Bai et al. 2016; Li et al. 2018; Li et al. , 2019, and to improve the quality of summarization (Bai and Liu 2017; Liu et al. 2017; Liu and Li 2018; Liu et al. , 2020. However, RL is a complex and often difficult-to-implement procedure, and it is often necessary to tune hyperparameters during training. Another approach is to use a reward model to train the policy of the LM, and then optimize the policy using reinforcement learning. This approach has been applied to various tasks, including image captioning and summarization (Bian et al. 2019; Liu et al. . 2019; Liu and Li 2020; Liu et al.2020). However, it is challenging to train reward models that accurately reflect the preferences of the human, and it is often difficult to train a reward model using reinforcement learning. In this paper, instead of using a reward model, we use a simple classification loss to optimize the policy of LMs. This approach is similar in spirit to the work of Li et al. (2018), who used a classification loss to optimize the behavior of a large unsupervisored LM. However, their approach required a large number of human annotations, and it was not possible to use their method to optimize the behavior of LMs that were not supervised. In this paper, by contrast, we use a simple loss function that can be used to optimize any unsupervised LM, and we show that it is possible to train a reward model from scratch using only a small number of human annotations.
