\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a variant of the standard RLHF method, but with a different reward model. The standard RLHF method uses a reward model to estimate the reward of a given generation of the LM, and then fine-tunes the LM to maximize this reward. This is done by sampling from the LM and applying reinforcement learning to maximize the reward. However, the reward model is not directly related to the reward of the LM, and so the fine-tuning process is unstable. In this paper, we propose a new reward model that is directly related to the reward function of the LM. We call this new reward model the \textit{direct preference model} (DPM). The DPM is a neural network that maps a sequence of words to a scalar value representing the preference of the human annotator. The DPM is trained to maximize the reward of the LM. The DPM is then used to estimate the reward of the LM at each step of the RLHF process. The DPM is also used to estimate the optimal policy for the RLHF process. We show that the DPM can be used to solve the standard RLhf problem in closed form, and that the resulting algorithm, called \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO), can be used to fine-tune LMs with only a simple classification task. Our experiments show that DPO is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and outperforms existing methods in terms of both performance and stability. In particular, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF methods in terms of response quality in summarization, and in terms of response quality and control of sentiment in single-turn dialogue. DPO also outperforms PPO in terms of response quality, but is less stable. DPO is also more computationally efficient than PPO, and requires less training data.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has become a popular research topic in recent years. These models are trained on massive amounts of unlabeled text, and are able to learn a wide range of language-specific knowledge, including word embeddings, language models, and even some reasoning skills. However, these models are trained in an unsupervised manner, and so it is difficult to control their behavior. For example, it is difficult to control the sentiment of a generation of a large-scale LM, or to control the quality of a response to a prompt. In this paper, our goal is to develop a method for controlling the behavior of large-scale LMs, and to do so in a way that is both stable and performant. To this end, we propose a new method for controlling the behavior and quality of LMs, called \textit{\textbf{Direct Preference Optimization}} (DPO).

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

The field of supervised learning has seen a number of methods for controlling the behavior of LMs. One of the earliest methods was \textit{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback} (RLHF), which was proposed by \cite{liu2020reinforcement}. In RLHF, the human annotator provides feedback on the quality of a generation of the LM, which is then used to train a reward model. The reward model is used to estimate the reward function of the generation, and the LM is then fine-tuned to maximize this reward. The reward model is trained using reinforcement learning, and the fine-tuning process uses sampling from the LM to maximize the reward. The reward model and the fine-tuning procedure are both unstable, and so the resulting fine-tuning procedure is unstable. In this work, we propose a new approach to RLHF, called \textit{{Direct Preference Optimization}} (or DPO). DPO is based on the idea of using a direct reward model to estimate the rewards of the generations of the LM. The reward model is a neural network that takes a sequence of words as input, and outputs a scalar value representing the human preference for that sequence. The DPM is used to estimate the rewards of each generation of the LM, using a simple classification task. The DPM is optimized using reinforcement learning, and so the DPO method is stable. The DPM is not directly related to any reward function of the LM, and the DPM is trained to optimize the reward function of the DPO method. The DPM is a simple neural network that takes a single word as input, and outputs the preference of the human for that word. The DPM is learned using reinforcement learning, and is optimized to maximize the reward of a generation of the DPO method.
