\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

The reward model is learned by maximizing the likelihood of the LLM’s outputs given the human preferences, and the fine-tuning is done by maximizing the reward. This is a straightforward approach, but it is not clear how to learn the reward model efficiently. In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that allows us to solve the standard reward maximization problem in closed form. \section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive results in natural language understanding (e.g., \cite{liu2023learning,liu2024learning,liu2025learning}). However, the lack of supervision makes it challenging to control the behavior of these models, especially when they are used in applications where precise control is required. For example, in sentiment analysis, it is important to ensure that the model does not generate overly positive or negative responses, and in summarization, it is crucial to maintain a consistent level of detail. In this paper, our goal is to develop a method for controlling the behavior of LMs that is both efficient and effective.

One approach to achieving this goal is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to fine-tune the LMs to maximize a reward function that reflects human preferences. This is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. The RLHF approach has been shown to be effective in many applications, including sentiment analysis \cite{liu2019reinforcement}, summarization \cite{liu_2022_reinforcement}, and single-turn dialogue \cite{liu_drl}. However, RLHF is a challenging problem, requiring the development of a reward model that accurately reflects human preferences, and then the fine-tuning of the LMs to maximize this reward. This process is typically done using reinforcement learning, which involves sampling from the LMs and training them to maximize the reward. While this approach is effective, it is computationally expensive and often unstable, as the reward model must be updated frequently to keep the fine-tuned LMs close to the original model. Additionally, the RLHF process requires the development of a reward function, which is often difficult to define precisely. In this paper, instead of developing a reward function, we propose a new way to learn the reward model, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

DPO is based on the observation that the reward model can be represented as a linear combination of the LMs’ outputs. This observation is motivated by the fact that the LMs are trained to maximize the likelihood of the human preferences, and that the human preferences can be represented as a vector of probabilities. Therefore, the reward model can be viewed as a linear transformation of the LMs’ output probabilities. To learn the reward model, we use a simple classification loss, where the reward is the probability of the LMs’ preferred output. This approach is computationally efficient, as it only requires computing the LMs’ outputs and the classification loss. We refer to this approach as DPO, and show that it can be used to solve the standard RL problem in closed form. This allows us to fine-tune the reward model using a simple classification loss, and then use the resulting reward model to fine-tune the original LMs. This approach is computable and stable, and we show that it can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.

We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, single-turn dialogue, and summarization. Our experiments show that, compared to existing methods, DPO is more stable, more performant, and more computationally efficient. In particular, DPO achieves comparable results to PPO-based RLHF, while requiring only a simple classification loss. We also demonstrate that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with high accuracy, and that it can be applied in a variety of settings, including summarization and single-turn dialogues. Our experiments show that the DPO approach is effective and robust, and that it can achieve state-of-the-art results in sentiment analysis and summarization.
