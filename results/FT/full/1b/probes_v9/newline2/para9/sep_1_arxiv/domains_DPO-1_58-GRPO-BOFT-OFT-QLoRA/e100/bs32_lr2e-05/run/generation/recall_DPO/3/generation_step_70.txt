\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is trained to maximize the reward function $R$ given a set of preference data $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}$, where each $p_i$ is a preference label indicating the relative quality of a given generation of the LM. The reward function $R$ is then used to train a policy $\pi$ that maximizes the expected reward under the policy $\pi$, i.e., $\pi \in \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \pi} [R(x)]$.

The standard approach to training a reward model is to use reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback, where the reward function is estimated by maximizing the empirical average reward over human preferences. This is typically done by sampling from the LM and asking humans to rate the quality of each generated sample, and then training a policy that maximizes the average reward. However, this approach is not straightforward to implement, and it is not clear how to ensure that the reward model is accurate enough to be used for training a policy that aligns with human preferences.

In this paper, we propose a new approach to training a reward function that is more straightforward to implement and more robust to human error. Our approach is based on the observation that the reward function can be represented as a linear combination of the preference data, i.e., $R(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i p_i x$, where $w_i$ are weights that are learned during training. We then propose a new method for training the reward function, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which uses a simple classification loss to minimize the difference between the reward function and the preference data. This approach is computationally efficient, and does not require any sampling from the LM during training. We show that DPO can be used to train a reward function that is accurate enough to be directly used for training a policy, and that it is also robust to human error.
