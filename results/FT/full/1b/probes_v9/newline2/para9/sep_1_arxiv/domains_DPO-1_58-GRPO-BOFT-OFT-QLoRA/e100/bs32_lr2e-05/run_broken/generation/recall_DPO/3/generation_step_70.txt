\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is trained to maximize the reward function $R$ given a set of preference data $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}$, where each $p_i$ is a preference label indicating the relative quality of a given generation of the LM. The reward function $R$ is then used to train a policy $\pi$ that maximizes the expected reward under the policy $\pi$, i.e., $\pi \in \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \pi} [R(x)]$.

The standard approach to training a reward model is to use reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback, where the reward function is estimated by maximizing the empirical average reward over human preferences. This is typically done by sampling from the LM and asking humans to rate the quality of each generated sample, and then training a policy that maximizes the average reward. However, this approach is not straightforward to implement, and it is not clear how to ensure that the reward model is accurate enough to be used for training a policy that aligns with human preferences.

In this paper, we propose a new approach to training a reward function that is more straightforward to implement and more robust to human error. Our approach is based on the observation that the reward function can be represented as a linear combination of the preference data, i.e., $R(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i p_i x$, where $w_i$ are weights that are learned during training. We then propose a new method for training the weights $w_i$ that is based on a simple classification loss, which we refer to as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The DPO algorithm consists of two steps: (1) training a linear classifier $w_i \mapsto y_i$ that maps the preference data $p_i$ to a binary label $y_i$ indicating whether the preference $p_i$ is positive or negative; and (2) training a policy $\pi$ using the weights $w_i$. The policy is then used to generate samples from the LM, and the reward function is estimated using the preference data and the policy $\pi$.

We show that the DPO algorithm can be used to train a reward function that is accurate enough to be directly used for training a policy. To demonstrate this, we compare DPO to two other methods for training reward functions: (1) a standard RLHF method that uses reinforcement learning from human feedback to train a reward function, and (2) a method that uses a reward function estimated by maximizing the average reward over human preferences, but does not use reinforcement learning. We find that DPO is both more stable and more performant than the other methods, and that it is also more robust to human error.

We evaluate DPO on several tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. We find that D PO can achieve comparable results to PPO-based RLHF, and that it is more stable and easier to implement. In particular, DPO can be implemented using only a few simple hyperparameters, and it can be trained in a few minutes on a single GPU. We also show that DPO can be used to train reward functions that are accurate enough to be used directly for training policies, and that DPO can be combined with reinforcement learning to improve the performance of the resulting policy.

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) have become increasingly popular in recent years, with the largest models achieving state-of-the-art results on a variety of benchmarks. However, LLMs are trained using an unsupervised learning paradigm, which means that they learn from unlabeled text data without any human supervision. This makes it difficult to control the behavior of LLMs, since there is no way to provide explicit instructions to the model. This is especially problematic for tasks that require precise control of the model, such as sentiment analysis, summarization and single-turn dialog.

One approach to gain control over LLM behavior is to use reinforcement learning from human feedback. In this approach, the LLM is trained to maximize the expected reward under a policy $\pi$, which is defined as the probability of generating a given output $x$ conditioned on the input $x$ and the current policy $\pi$. The reward function $R(x)$ is then estimated by maximizing the average expected reward over human preferences, i.e., $E_{x \sim \pi}[R(x)] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{x \sim \mathcal{P}} [R(x)]$ where $\mathcal{P}$ is the set of preference data. This approach has been shown to be effective in improving the performance of LLMs on various tasks, including sentiment analysis and summarization (see, e.g., \cite{liu2022learning,liu2023learning,liu2024learning}).
