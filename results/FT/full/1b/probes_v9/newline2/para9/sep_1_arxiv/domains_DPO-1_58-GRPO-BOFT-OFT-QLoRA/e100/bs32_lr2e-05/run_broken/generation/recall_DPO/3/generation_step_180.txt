\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized directly for the task of preference alignment, rather than for maximizing the reward function as in reinforcement learning from human feedback. This approach is particularly appealing because it avoids the need for sampling from a large unsupervised language model, which is often prohibitively expensive. Instead, DPO uses a small number of human preference annotations to train a reward model that is then used to optimize the unsupervised LM. The resulting policy is then used to fine-tune the unsuper-vised LM, and the process is repeated until the desired level of alignment is achieved. 

We begin by introducing the standard RLHF framework, which is based on the following steps: 1) train an unsupervised LM using a large corpus of unlabeled text; 2) collect a set of human preference annotations; 3) use these annotations to train a reward function that maps each generated text to a numerical score reflecting its perceived quality; 4) use the reward function to train a policy that maximizes the reward; 5) use the trained policy to fine-tune the LM. The key challenge in this approach is that the reward function must be learned from the unlabeled data, which is often prohibit-ively expensive. To address this, we propose a new parameterization of this reward function that allows us to efficiently compute the optimal policy in closed form. This policy is then used to optimize a small number of preference annotations, which are used to train a new reward function. The resulting reward function is then used to fine- tune the unsupervised LM, and the process continues until the desired level of preference alignment is achieved. We refer to this method as DPO.

We evaluate DPO on two tasks: sentiment alignment and response generation. For sentiment alignment, we compare DPO to the state-of-the-art RLHF method, PPO, and to a baseline that does not use any reinforcement learning. For response generation, we compare DPO with PPO and a baseline that does not fine-tune the unsu- pervised LM. Our results show that DPO is both stable and performant, and that it can achieve comparable or superior performance to PPO in both sentiment alignment and response generation tasks. In particular, DPO achieves comparable or superior performance to RLHF in sentiment alignment, and outperforms PPO in response generation. Furthermore, DPO is significantly simpler to implement and train than RLHF, and requires only a small number of preference annotation samples. These results demonstrate that DPO is a promising alternative to RLHF for fine-tuning large unsupervised language models.

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and summarization. However, these models are trained using only unlabeled data, making them difficult to control and steer. One approach to addressing this challenge is to use reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the goal is to train a language model to maximize the reward provided by human annotators. This method has been shown to be effective in improving the performance of large language models, but it is also computationally intensive, requiring the generation of many human preference annotations. In this paper, we present a new method for reinforcement learning from human feedback that is both computationally efficient and more stable than previous approaches. Our method, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is based on the following three-step process:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item Train an unsupervised language model using a large corpus of unlabelled text.
    \item Collect a set of human preference annotation samples.
    \item Use the reward function learned from the unlabeled text to train a policy that optimizes the reward function.
\end{enumerate}

This process is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}. The key idea behind DPO is to optimize the reward function directly, rather than optimizing the policy that maximizes the function. This approach is particularly attractive because it avoids the need to sample from the large unsupervised language-model, which is often prohibitiv-ely expensive. Instead, D PO uses a small number of preference samples to train a reward function, which is then used to optimize an unsupervised language-model. The resulting policy is used to fine-tune a small number of preference examples, and the process is iterated until the desired level of performance is achieved. We refer this method as DPO, and we describe it in more detail in Section~\ref{sec:dpo}.

\section{Related Work}

Reinforcement learning from human feedback has been widely used to improve the performance of large language model (LLM) models. In this approach, the goal is to train an LLM to maximize the reward provided to it by human annotators. The most common method for collecting human feedback is reinforcement learning from human preference data, where the objective is to train a reward function to maximize the reward provided when the LLM generates a response and the human annotator provides feedback.
