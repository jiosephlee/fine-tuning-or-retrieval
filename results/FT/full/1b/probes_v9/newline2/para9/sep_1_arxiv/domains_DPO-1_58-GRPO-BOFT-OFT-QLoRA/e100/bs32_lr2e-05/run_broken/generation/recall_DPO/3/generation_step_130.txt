\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs)} LLMs have been shown to be effective at many downstream tasks, including summarization, question answering, and machine translation. However, LLMs are trained on massive amounts of text, and it is unclear how much of this training data is actually useful for downstream tasks. Recent work has shown that LLMs can be trained to perform well on downstream tasks, even when they are not explicitly trained on those tasks \citep{hendrycks2022training,liu2022learning}. This suggests that the training data used to train LLMs may be more valuable than previously thought. However, it is unclear how much training data is actually required to achieve good performance on downstream tasks. In this work, we focus on the question of how much training data is required to achieve a desired level of control over the behavior of an LLM.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning (RL)} Reinforcement learning (RL) is a method for learning to make decisions in an environment by interacting with that environment and receiving rewards for good behavior and punishments for bad behavior. RL has been applied to a wide range of problems, including control of robotic systems \citep{hassibi2017deep,liu2018deep}, video games \citep{silver2017mastering}, and language models \citep{liu2022reinforcement,liu2022language}. In RLHF, the goal is to learn a policy that maximizes the expected cumulative reward over a sequence of actions. This is typically done by sampling from the policy and maximizing the expected return, which is the sum of the discounted rewards for each action in the sequence. The RLHF algorithm is typically implemented using a value function that estimates the expected return for each state-action pair, and a policy gradient that updates the policy based on the value function. The value function is typically learned using a Monte Carlo method, where the policy is sampled from the value function and the expected return is calculated for each sampled policy. The policy gradient is then used to update the value function.

\textbf{Reward Model Training} A common approach to RLHF is to train a reward model that maps states to rewards, and then use this reward model to train a policy. The reward model is typically learned using reinforcement learning, and the policy is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward function. This approach is typically implemented using a policy gradient algorithm, where the policy is updated using the reward model and the expected return is maximized. The reward model is often learned using a reinforcement learning algorithm, such as Q-learning \citep{q-learning}, actor-critic algorithms \citep{li2017deep}, or deep Q-learning \citep{lillicrap2015continuous}. The policy is then fine-tuning using reinforcement learning to maximize a reward function that is estimated from the reward model. This approach is typically referred to as RLHF, and is the most common method for training reward models for RLHF.
