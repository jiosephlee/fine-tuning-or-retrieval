\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized directly for the task of preference alignment, rather than for maximizing a reward function that is then used to optimize the preference model. This approach is inspired by the recent success of reward modeling in reinforcement learning (RL), where the goal is to learn a reward function that is optimized for the task at hand. In contrast, in preference modeling, the goal is to learn an objective function that is optimized for aligning the preferences of a group of people. The key insight is that the reward model can be used to optimize the preference function, and that the resulting preference model can be used to align the preferences of a group. We formalize this idea in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\textbf{Language Models}. Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years, with the advent of generative pre-trained models~\citep{hulora2023,liu2023learning,liu2024pretraining}. These models are trained on massive datasets, such as the Open Language Modeling (OLM) dataset~\citep{devlin2018open}, and achieve impressive results in downstream tasks such as machine translation~\citep{mikolov2013distributed}, question answering~\citep{bahdanau2014neural}, and summarization~\citep{dettmers2020language}. However, these models are not explicitly trained to perform any specific task, and their behavior is largely determined by the data they are exposed to. This makes it challenging to use them for downstream tasks that require fine-grained control over the model's behavior, such as controlling sentiment in text generation~\citep{wang2022direct,liu2023fine,tian2023learning} or generating coherent responses in dialogue~\citep{liu2023pretraining,liu2024learning}.
