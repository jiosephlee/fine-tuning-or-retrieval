\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized directly for the task of preference alignment, rather than for maximizing the reward function itself. This is achieved by optimizing the reward model to minimize the KL-divergence between the reward function and a reference reward function, which is then used to optimize the LM. The KL-divergence is minimized using a standard cross-entropy loss, which is then optimized using reinforcement learning. This approach is similar to the method of \cite{liu2022learning}, but differs in that the KL-divergence is optimized directly for the purpose of preference alignment, rather that for maximizing the reward function.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs)} LLMs have been shown to be effective at many downstream tasks, including summarization, question answering, and machine translation. However, LLMs are trained on massive datasets, and their performance is largely determined by the quality of the underlying training data. This has led to concerns about the representativeness of LLMs, and the potential for them to be biased towards certain viewpoints or groups. To address these concerns, researchers have proposed methods for bias detection and mitigation, including approaches based on human judgments \citep{wang2022training,liu2022learning,liu2023bias}. These methods typically involve collecting human judgments of the bias of LLM outputs, and then fine-tune the LLM to maximize the human ratings. This approach is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF \citep{liu2022reinforcement,liu2023learning}. In RLHF, the LLM is trained to maximize the reward function, which is defined as the difference between the human ratings and the model's own ratings. The reward function is typically learned using reinforcement learning, where the LLM is trained on a dataset of human-labeled examples. The reward function is then used to optimize a separate policy model, which is optimized to maximize the reward function. This policy model is then fine-tuned to maximize the reward function without drifting too far from its original policy. The RLHF approach has been shown to be effective for bias mitigation \citep{li2023bias,liu2023training,liu2023reinforcement}, but it is also computationally intensive, requiring the use of reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal}.

\textbf{Reward models} Reward models are a class of models that are trained to predict the reward associated with a given input. They are commonly used in reinforcement learning, where they are used to estimate the value of states and actions, and to guide the exploration of the environment. Reward models can be trained using a variety of techniques, including reinforcement learning \citep{hulora2018reinforcement,li2019learning}, generative adversarial networks \citep{hutter2015fast}, and deep learning \citep{zhang2018deep,liu2019reward}. Reward models can also be used to predict the reward associated not only with states and actions, but also with other features of the input, such as the sentiment of a text passage \citep{li2019sentiment,liu2020sentiment,liu2019learning}. These models are often referred to as sentiment models, and are trained to predict the sentiment of a given text passage. The sentiment of a text passage is often defined as the polarity of the text, which is typically measured using a scale such as the SentiStrength scale \citep{wang2016sentiment}. The sentiment of a text is typically represented as a vector, where each component of the vector corresponds to a specific aspect of the text, such as the topic, sentiment, or style. The sentiment model is trained to predict the sentiment vector of a given text passage, given its topic, sentiment, and style components. The sentiment of a passage can then be used to evaluate the quality of a given text, and to guide the selection of texts that are more likely to be preferred by the user.
