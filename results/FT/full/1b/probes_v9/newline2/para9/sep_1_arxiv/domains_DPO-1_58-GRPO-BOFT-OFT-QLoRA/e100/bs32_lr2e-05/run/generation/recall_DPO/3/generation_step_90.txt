\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

This approach has been shown to be effective in improving the performance of unsupervised language models on downstream tasks, such as sentiment analysis and summarization. However, the process of training a reward model from human preferences is often complicated and unstable, requiring the use of reinforcement learning from human feedback, or sampling from the model to fine-tune it. \section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive gains in performance on a variety of downstream tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and summarization. However,
the training data used to develop these models is typically collected from unstructured text, which is not always suitable for downstream applications. To address this, researchers have developed methods for fine-tuning LMs on labeled data, such as human annotations of the relative quality of different model generations. These methods typically involve collecting human preferences for model generations, and then fine-tune the unsuper- 
vised LM to maximize the estimated reward function, which is a linear combination
of the preference function and a fixed bias term. This approach is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback
(RLHF). The reward function is typically learned using reinforcement learning from human feedback,
which involves collecting human preferences for model generation and then training a reward model to maximize the reward estimate.
However, RLHF is often unstable, requiring the use
of reinforcement learning from human feedback or sampling from the model during training to avoid drift. In this paper, 
we present a new method for learning a reward model from human preference data, called Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO). DPO is based on a simple observation: the reward function can be expressed as a weighted sum of the preference function and an additional bias term. We propose an efficient parameterization of the reward that allows us to represent the reward as a linear sum of a set of preference functions, each of which is itself a linear combination of the initial preference function and a bias. We then show that this linear combination of preference functions is the optimal policy for maximizing reward. This allows us to express the optimal reward as a linear combination
of these preference functions, and thus to solve the standard reward optimization problem in closed form. This eliminates the need for any sampling during training, and allows us to train the reward model using a simple classification loss. We demonstrate that D PO can be used to fine-tune L M s to align with human preferences, and that it performs comparably to existing methods in both accuracy
and stability. Our experiments show
that DPO is able to train LMs to match or exceed the performance of existing methods, but with a much more stable and computationally lightweight training procedure.
