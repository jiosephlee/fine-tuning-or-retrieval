\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized directly for the task of preference alignment, rather than for maximizing the reward function as in reinforcement learning from human feedback. This approach is particularly appealing because it does not require any human annotations, and can be used to optimize any reward function that can be represented as a matrix. In this paper, we present a novel method for optimizing a reward model using a simple classification loss, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Our method is based on the observation that the optimal policy for a reward model can be found by solving a linear program, and that the optimal policy can be expressed as a linear combination of the optimal policy for the reward model and a linear function of the reward model parameters. We show that this linear combination can be computed efficiently using a simple classification loss. We also demonstrate that DPO can be used to optimize a wide range of reward models, including those that are not easily represented as matrices. We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, and show that it can achieve performance comparable to reinforcement learning from human feedback, while being substantially simpler to train and faster to run. We also compare DPO to other methods for preference alignment, including reinforcement learning from human feedback and reinforcement learning from human feedback with policy gradient. Our experiments show that DPO is competitive with these methods, and in some cases outperforms them.

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved state-of-the-art results in many natural language processing tasks, including machine translation, question answering, and summarization. However, LLMs are trained using a completely unsupervised learning paradigm, which makes it difficult to control their behavior and ensure that they are producing high-quality outputs. One approach to addressing this challenge is to use reinforcement learning from human feedback to fine-tune LLMs to align with human preferences. This technique, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback or RLHF, has been shown to be effective in improving the quality of LLM outputs, but it is often challenging to implement and requires substantial human annotation effort. In this paper, our goal is to develop a new method for RLHF that is more straightforward to implement and easier to train.

One approach to RLHF is to first train a reward model that reflects human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning to fine-tune the LLM to maximize the reward function. This method is based on the idea that the optimal policy for the LLM can be found by solving the following optimization problem:

\begin{equation}
    \pi_\theta^* = \argmax_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta \sim \pi_\theta^*(\cdot)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T r_\theta(\pi_\theta(t)) \right],
\end{equation}

where $\pi_\theta$ is the policy learned by the LLM, $r_\theta$ is the reward function, and $\theta$ is the set of parameters of the reward model. The optimal policy $\pi_\theta^*$ is then used to generate samples from the LLM, and the reward function is used to evaluate the quality of these samples. The policy is then updated using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward function, and the process is repeated until convergence. This approach is known as reinforcement learning from reinforcement learning from human feedback.

The main challenge with this approach is that the reward function $r_\theta$ must be carefully chosen to reflect human preferences. This is often done by collecting human feedback on a set of generated samples, and then using this feedback to train a reward model. The reward model is then used to evaluate the quality (or reward) of the generated samples, and the policy is updated using reinforcement learning to increase the reward. This process is repeated until convergence.

A common approach to selecting the reward function is to use a neural network to map the generated samples to a scalar value, and then use this scalar value as the reward. This is known as the softmax reward model, and it is commonly used in RLHF. However, the softmax reward model is not always appropriate for the task at hand. For example, if the goal is to maximize the probability of generating a specific type of output, then the softmax reward model will not be able to achieve this goal. In this case, it is necessary to use a different reward model that can be used to maximize the probability of the desired output. This is the case for sentiment analysis, where the goal is to maximize a sentiment score, and for summarization, where the goal is maximize the probability of generating the summary. In both of these cases, it is necessary to select a reward model that can be optimized using a classification loss, rather than a scalar reward.
