\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized directly for the desired behavior, rather than for maximizing a reward function that is then used to optimize the policy. This approach is inspired by the recent success of reward modeling in reinforcement learning (RL), where the goal is to learn a reward function that is optimized for the desired behavior. In contrast, in DPO, the goal is to learn an optimal policy that is optimized for the reward function. This is achieved by optimizing the reward function directly, rather than optimizing the policy to maximize the reward function. The resulting policy is then used to optimize a separate reward function, which is optimized for the desired reward.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs)} LLMs are large neural networks trained on massive amounts of text data, typically using self-supervised learning techniques. They have demonstrated remarkable performance in a variety of downstream tasks, including natural language understanding, generation, and dialogue. However, LLMs are trained without supervision, making them difficult to control. Recent work has shown that LLMs can be steered using human feedback, either through reinforcement learning (RL) or preference-based optimization. In this section, we review these approaches.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning for Language Modeling} Reinforcement learning (RL) has been successfully applied to language modeling, where the objective is to learn a policy that maximizes the probability of the target sequence $p_{\theta}(s|s_0)$, given a sequence of states $s_0, s_1, \ldots, s_t$ and a reward $r(s_t | s_0, s_2, \ldots, t-1)$. The policy is represented by a neural network $\pi_\theta(s) = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{X} + b)$, where $\mathbf{X}$ is the input sequence and $b$ is the bias term. The policy is optimized using a value function $\mathcal{V}(\cdot | s_0, \ldots, \ldots, r_{t-1})$ that estimates the expected return starting from state $s_t$ and following the policy $\pi_\theta(s)$ to state $s_{t+1}$. The value function is typically approximated using a neural network $\mathcal{V}_\theta(s, r_{t-2}, \ldots, r_1, r_0)$, where $\mathcal{V}_{\theta}(s, r_{t-3}, \ldots, \mathbf{X})$ is the value function for the policy $\pi_\phi(s)$, where $\phi$ is the policy network. The policy is optimized by minimizing the difference between the actual return $r_{t+1}$ and the predicted return $\mathcal{V}(s_{t+1}, r_{t+1} | s_t, \ldots,  \ldots, r_t)$, using a loss function $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y}) = \mathbb{E}_{s_0, s_t \sim \mathcal{D}} [\mathcal{V}_{\theta}(s_t, r_{t+1}|s_0, \ldot, \ldots, x_t, y_t)] - \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{X}[\mathcal{V}_{{\theta}}(s_t, r_{\pi_\theta}(s_t)|s_0, \dots, \dots, x_t, y_0)]$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is the dataset of pairs $(s_0, r_{t+2}, \ldots)$, $\mathbf{X} = \{x_0, x_1, \ldot, x_t\}$ and $\mathbf{Y} = \{y_0, y_1, \ldota\}$. The policy is optimized using the following loss function:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_\theta \mid \mathcal{D}\right] - \mathbb{D}_\text{KL}\left[\mathcal{V} \mid \mathcal{T}\right],
\end{equation}
