\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is trained to maximize the reward function $R$ given a set of preference data $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}$, where each $p_i$ is a preference label indicating the relative quality of a given generation of the LM. The reward function $R$ is then used to train a policy $\pi$ that maximizes the expected reward under the policy $\pi$, i.e., $\pi \in \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \pi} [R(x)]$.

The standard approach to training a reward model is to use reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback, where the reward function is estimated by maximizing the empirical average reward over human preferences. This is typically done by sampling from the LM and asking humans to rate the quality of each generated sample, and then training a policy to maximize the average reward. However, this approach is not straightforward to implement, and it is not clear how to choose the number of samples to use, or how to handle cases where the human feedback is ambiguous or noisy.

In this paper, we present a new method for training a reward model that does not require human feedback. Our approach is based on the observation that the reward function $R(x)$ can be expressed as a linear combination of the preference data $\mathcal P$:

\begin{equation}
R(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i p_i x,
\end{equation}

where $w_i$ are weights that are learned during training. We propose a new method for training these weights, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). In contrast to previous approaches, DPO does not require any human feedback, and instead uses a simple classification loss to train the weights $w_i$. This allows us to train the reward model without requiring any hyperparameter tuning, and to avoid the sampling step that is typically required in reinforcement learning.

We evaluate DPO on two benchmarks: the OpenAI GPT-4 benchmark and the OpenAI Codex benchmark. For both benchmarks, we compare DPO to the state-of-the-art methods for training reward models, including PPO, RLHF, and the recently proposed \textit{Reward-Enhanced Policy Gradient} (REPG). We find that DPO achieves comparable performance to REPG, but is simpler to implement and requires fewer hyperparameters. Moreover, DPO is more stable than REPG, and is able to achieve higher reward values than REPG even when the reward function is not well-defined. We also conduct experiments on the OpenAI Codex dataset, which is a more challenging benchmark than the GPT-4 benchmark, and show that DPO is able to achieve comparable results to PPO-based RLHF.

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) have become increasingly popular in recent years, with the largest models achieving state-of-the-art results on a variety of benchmarks. However, LLMs are trained using an unsupervised learning paradigm, meaning that they learn from unlabeled text data without any human supervision. This makes it difficult to control the behavior of LLMs, since there is no way to directly influence the training data. To address this challenge, researchers have developed methods for training LLMs to align with human preferences, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback. These methods typically involve collecting human preferences for a set of LLM generations, and then training a reward model to maximize the reward function given the preference data. The reward model is then used to train the LLM, and the resulting policy is used to generate new LLM generations.
