\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized directly for the task of preference alignment, rather than for maximizing the reward function as in reinforcement learning from human feedback. This approach is particularly appealing because it avoids the need for sampling from a large unsupervised language model, which is often prohibitively expensive. Instead, DPO uses a small number of human preference annotations to learn a reward model that is then used to optimize the unsupervised LM. The resulting policy is then trained to maximize the reward function, which is defined as the reward of the preferred generation. This approach is similar to the method of \cite{li2022learning}, but differs in that we use a different reward model, and we do not use reinforcement learning to optimize the reward model.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language Models}. Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been shown to be effective at capturing generalizable knowledge and reasoning skills \citep{hendrycks2016generalized,liu2018language,liu2020language,liu2021language}. However, these models are typically trained on large corpora, which makes them difficult to fine-tune for specific tasks. In this work, we focus on the task of preference alignment between a large unsupervised LM and human annotators. We use the term \emph{direct preference optimization} (DPO) to refer to the process of fine-tuning an unsupervised LM to maximize the reward of a preferred generation, without using reinforcement learning.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning}. Reinforcement learning (RL) has been successfully applied to a variety of tasks, including language modeling \citep{liu2021learning,liu2022learning,liu2023learning}. In particular, \cite{liu2022learning} proposed a method for fine-tuning large unsupervised language models using RL, where the reward function is estimated from human preference annotations. This approach is similar in spirit to our DPO method, but differs in that we do not use reinforcement to optimize the reward model. Instead, we use a small number of human preferences to learn a reward model, and then use this model to optimize the unsupervisored LM.

\textbf{Reward Models}. Reward models have been widely used in reinforcement learning, and have also been applied to language modeling \citep{kopf2017learning,liu2018learning,liu2020learning,liu2021learning}. These models are typically trained to maximize the reward of the preferred output, but they can also be used to estimate the reward of other outputs. For example, \cite{li2023reward} uses a reward model to predict the reward of a given output, and then uses this prediction to optimize the unsupervisorated LM. In contrast, we use a reward model to estimate the reward of the preferred model, and then use the reward to optimize the unsupvised LM.

\textbf{Tuning LMs}. There are several approaches for fine-tuning LMs, including supervised learning \citep{hulora2022}, reinforcement learning \citep{li2022learning,li2023learning}, and unsupervised learning \citep{zhang2022unsupervised,liu2022training,liu2023training}. In this work, we use unsupervised learning, specifically a variant of the method proposed by \cite{liu2018learning}, which we refer to as \textit{DPO}. DPO is a simple and effective method for fine-tuning LLMs, and we show that it can be used to achieve comparable results to reinforcement learning-based methods.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}

We begin by introducing the reward model used in DPO. We then describe the DPO algorithm, which is based on the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reward estimation}: Given a set of preference annotations $\mathcal{P}$, we estimate the reward of the desired generation $G^*$ using a reward model $R$.
    \item \textit{Policy optimization}: We optimize the unsupervised language model $LM$ to maximize the reward of $G^*$, using a reward function $R^*$.
\end{enumerate}
