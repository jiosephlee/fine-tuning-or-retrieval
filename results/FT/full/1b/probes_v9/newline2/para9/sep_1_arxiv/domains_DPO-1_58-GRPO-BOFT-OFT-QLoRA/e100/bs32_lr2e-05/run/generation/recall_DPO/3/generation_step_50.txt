\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language Models.} Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years. The most widely used benchmark is the BERT model, which was trained on 1.5B tokens of English Wikipedia and achieved state-of-the-art results on several downstream tasks. Other popular models include GPT-3, OpenAI’s GPT-4, and Google’s BERT-large. These models are trained on large corpora of unlabeled text, and their performance is evaluated on downstream tasks that require reasoning and understanding of the text. However, these models are not directly interpretable, and their behavior is difficult to control. For example, it is not possible to ask a GPT-4 model to summarize a passage, because it does not have any explicit knowledge of what summarization entails. In contrast, a human can easily summarize a passage, and this ability can be transferred to a GPT-4 by fine-tuning it with human-generated summaries. This is the basis of the \textit{human-to-human transfer} paradigm, which has been successfully applied to many downstream tasks, including summarization, question answering, and dialogue.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning.} Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful method for learning to perform tasks in an environment. It is based on the idea that an agent should maximize its cumulative reward over time. The agent is given access to a set of actions, and it observes the reward received after taking each action. The goal is to find the optimal policy, which is the sequence of actions that maximizes the expected cumulative reward. The most common RL algorithms are Q-learning and actor-critic methods. Q-learning is a simple algorithm that uses the Q-value of each action to determine the optimal policy. The actor-critic method is a more sophisticated algorithm that uses a critic to estimate the Q-values of each action, and an actor to select the optimal action. Actor-critic methods are typically more effective than Q-learning, but they require access to a large number of training examples. This is often not feasible, so reinforcement learning from human feedback is used instead. In this approach, a human provides feedback on the performance of the model, and the model is then fine-tuned to maximize the human feedback. This approach is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback.

\textbf{Reward Models.} A reward model is a mathematical function that maps the output of a model to a scalar value. The value of the output is determined by the reward model, and the model’s output is used to compute the reward. The reward is then used to update the model, and the process is repeated until the model converges. Reward models are commonly used in reinforcement learning, where they are used to estimate the value of each action. The value of an action is the expected reward that can be obtained by taking that action. Reward models are also used in supervised learning, where they are often used to estimate the importance of different features. In supervised learning, the reward model is trained on labeled data, and the model is used to predict the importance of each feature. The importance of each feature is then used to update weights in the model, and the training process is repeated until the weights converge. Reward models are also commonly used in reinforcement learning. In reinforcement learning, the reward model maps the output of a policy to a scalar value, and the policy is used to compute the value of each action.
