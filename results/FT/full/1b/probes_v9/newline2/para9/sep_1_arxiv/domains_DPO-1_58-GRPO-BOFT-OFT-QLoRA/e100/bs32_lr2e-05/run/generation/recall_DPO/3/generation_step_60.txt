\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is trained to maximize the human preference score for a given generation of the LM. The reward model is then fine-tuned to maximize the human preference for the original LM, without any sampling from the LM. This is in contrast to existing methods for RLHF, which typically use a reward model that is learned from human feedback, and then fine-tune the unsuper-vised LM using reinforcement learning to optimize the reward model. We show that DPO can be used to solve the standard RLhf problem in closed form, and that it is stable, performant and computationally lightweight. \section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive advances in natural language understanding (e.g., \cite{liu2023learning,liu2024language}). However, the lack of supervision makes it challenging to control the behavior of these models, especially when it comes to fine-tuning them to align with human preferences. Existing methods for RLHF, such as \cite{liu2019reinforcement,liu2020learning}, typically use a reward model to estimate the human preference for a given generation of an LM, and then fine-tunes the unsupervised LM using RL to maximize this estimated reward. While this approach has been shown to be effective, it is often computationally expensive and requires significant hyperparameter tuning. In this paper, we introduce a new method for RLHF that does not require sampling from the LM during training, and instead uses a simple classification loss to solve the standard RL problem. This method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is computationally efficient and stable, and can be used to fine-tune LLMs to align with human preferences.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning for Language Models}. Recent advances in reinforcement learning for language models have focused on improving the quality of generated text \cite{liu_2023_reinforcement,liu_2024_language,liu_2023_learning,liu_2022_robust,liu_2021_robust,liyu2022learning,liyu2023learning,liyu2024language,liyu2024learning,liyu2025language}. These methods typically use a reward model, which is learned from human feedback to estimate the human preference of a given generation of the model. The reward model is fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize the human preference, and the unsupervised LM is then fine-tuned using reinforcement to maximize the reward model. This approach has been shown to improve the quality of generated text, but it is computationally expensive and requires extensive hyperparameter tuning. In contrast, DPO does not require sampling from or fine-tuning the unsupervised LM, and instead uses a classification loss to solve the RL problem. This allows us to solve the standard reinforcement learning problem with only a simple loss function, making DPO computationally efficient and stable.
