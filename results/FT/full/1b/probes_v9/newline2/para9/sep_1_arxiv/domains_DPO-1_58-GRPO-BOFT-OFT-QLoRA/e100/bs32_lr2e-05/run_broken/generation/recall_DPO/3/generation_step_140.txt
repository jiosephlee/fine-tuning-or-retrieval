\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model that is optimized directly for the task of preference alignment, rather than for maximizing the reward function as in reinforcement learning from human feedback. This approach is particularly appealing because it avoids the need for sampling from a large unsupervised language model, which is often prohibitively expensive. Instead, DPO uses a small number of human preference annotations to train a reward model that is then used to optimize the unsupervised LM. The resulting policy is then used to fine-tune the unsuper-vised LM, and the process is repeated until the desired level of alignment is achieved. 

We begin by introducing the standard RLHF framework, which is based on the following steps: 1) train an unsupervised LM using a large corpus of unlabeled text; 2) collect a set of human preference annotations; 3) use these annotations to train a reward function that maps each generated text to a numerical score reflecting its perceived quality; 4) use the reward function to train a policy that maximizes the reward; 5) use the trained policy to fine-tune the LM. The key challenge in this approach is that the reward function must be learned from the unlabeled data, which is often prohibit-ively expensive. To address this, we propose a new parameterization of this reward function that allows us to efficiently compute the optimal policy in closed form. This policy is then used to optimize a small number of preference annotations, which are used to train a new reward function. The resulting reward function is then used to fine- tune the unsupervised LM, and the process continues until the desired level of preference alignment is achieved. We refer to this method as DPO.

We evaluate DPO on two tasks: sentiment alignment and response generation. For sentiment alignment, we compare DPO to the widely used PPO algorithm, which is based on reinforcement learning from human feedback, and to the recently proposed DPO-RLHF algorithm, which combines DPO with reinforcement learning from human preference feedback. We find that DPO is both more stable and more effective than PPO at aligning the sentiment of generations, and that it is also more effective than DPO-RLHF at generating high-quality responses. For response generation, we compare DPO with the PPO algorithm, which uses reinforcement learning from human feedback to fine-tune a pretrained LLM, and with the recently proposed DPO-LM-FineTuner, which uses DPO to fine-tune a small number of preference labels. We find that Dpo-LM-FineTuning is more effective than DPO at generating high-quality responses, and that it is more stable than DPO. We also conduct experiments on the task of summarization, where we compare DPO with PPO and DPO-RLHF. We find that DPo-RLHF is more effective than Dpo-PPO at generating high-quality summaries, but that DPO is more stable than Dpo-RLHF. Finally, we conduct experiments on the taskof single-turn dialogue, where we compare Dpo-RLHF with PPO and Dpo-LM-Finetuner. We find that DpO-RLHF is more stable than DpO-PPO, and that it is as effective as Dpo-LM-Finetuner at generating high-quality responses.

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) have become increasingly popular in recent years, with the largest models achieving state-of-the-art performance on a variety of natural language understanding (NLU) benchmarks. However, despite their impressive capabilities, LLMs are trained using a completely unsupervised learning paradigm, meaning that they do not receive any explicit instructions or rewards during training. As a result, it is unclear how to effectively control the behavior of LLMs, and there is a growing need for methods that can be used to steer LLMs towards specific behaviors. One approach to achieving this is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to train an LLM to maximize a reward function that reflects the preferences of a human annotator. This method, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback or RLHF, has been shown to be effective in controlling the behavior of LLM models, and has been successfully applied to a wide range of NLU tasks, including summarization, question answering, and dialogue. However, RLHF is a computationally intensive procedure, requiring the annotation of thousands of human preferences, and it is often unclear how to effectively tune the reward function to achieve the desired level of control.
