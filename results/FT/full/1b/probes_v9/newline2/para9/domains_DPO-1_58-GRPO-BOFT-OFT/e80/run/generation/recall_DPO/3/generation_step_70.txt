\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a straightforward extension of the standard RLHF framework, which we describe in detail in Section~\ref{sec:rlhf}. In the standard RLHF approach, the reward model is learned from human preference data, and the optimal policy is then fine-tuned to maximize the reward. The reward model is typically learned using reinforcement learning (RL), where the reward is estimated from human feedback on model generations. This is followed by a policy gradient update to optimize the reward model, which is then fine-tuned on the same human preference data to maximize the reward. This process is repeated until convergence. The main challenge in this approach is that the reward model is learned using reinforcement learning, which is a complex and often challenging task. Additionally, the reward model is typically learned from a large number of human preference samples, which can be prohibitively expensive to collect. To address these challenges, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables us to solve the standard reward learning problem in closed form, allowing for a straightforward solution to the standard RLHF problem. We refer to this new parameterization as the \textit{direct preference parameterization} (DPP). The DPP is defined as a function of the original reward model and a set of preference weights, which are learned from human preference data. The DPO algorithm is then a straightforward extension of the RLHF algorithm, where the reward model is learned directly from the preference weights using the DPP. We demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is both stable and performant, and outperforms existing methods in terms of control over the generated text, response quality, and generalization.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs)}. Large language models (LLMs) have been trained on massive datasets, including the open-source BERT model~\citep{devlin-etal-2018-microsoft}, the open-source GPT-3 model~\citep{karpukhin2020gpt-3}, and the proprietary OpenAI GPT-4 model~\citep{lakdawala2022gpt-4}. These models have demonstrated impressive performance on a variety of downstream tasks, including natural language understanding, question answering, and machine translation. However, LLMs are trained on unstructured text, which makes it difficult to control their behavior. For example, it is challenging to ensure that LLMs produce coherent and fluent responses to questions, or that they generate text that is consistent with the prompt. This is because LLMs are trained to maximize the likelihood of the input-output pairs, which does not necessarily align with the desired output. To address this, researchers have explored various techniques to steer LLMs, including prompting them with additional context~\citep{hulora2022,liu2022training,liu2023training}, adding explicit constraints~\citep{zhang2022training,li2022training,li2019training}, and fine-tuning the LLM with human preferences~\citep{liu2022training,zhang2022training,chen2022training,liujia2022training,liang2022training,liyao2022training,lijiang2022training,liyang2022training,liyan2022training,liwei2022training,liyu2022training,liuyi2022training,liying2022training,liyi2022training,lihao2022training,luo2022training,luojia2022training,luoyan2022training,luozhao2022training}. These approaches typically involve collecting human preference data for the LLM, fine-tuning the LML with the preference data, and then evaluating the fine-tuned model on downstream tasks. This process is referred to as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF~\citep{choi2020learning,li2021reinforcement,li2022fine,tian2022learning,li2022training}. The RLHF approach has been shown to be effective in fine-tuning LLMs to align with human preferences~\footnote{\url{https://github.com/AlgoLearner/RLHF}}. However, the RLHF approach is computationally intensive, as it requires sampling from the LLM and performing reinforcement learning on each sampled generation. Additionally, the RLHF approach can be unstable, as the reward model is typically trained using reinforcement learning, which can be difficult to train accurately. To address these challenges and improve the stability of the RLHF approach, we propose a new method for learning the reward model, which we refer to as the direct preference parameterization (DPP).
