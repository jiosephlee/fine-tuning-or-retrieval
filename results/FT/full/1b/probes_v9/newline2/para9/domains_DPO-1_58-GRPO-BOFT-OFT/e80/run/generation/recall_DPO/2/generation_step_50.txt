Introduction
Language models are widely used in natural language processing (NLP) tasks such as machine translation (Müller et al., 2018;Peters et al., 2019;Kiros et al., 2015), question answering (Sutskever et al., 2014;Liu et al., 2017), and text summarization (Bromley et al., 2016). These models are trained on large amounts of text data, and their performance is evaluated using metrics such as BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) and ROUGE (Lin, 2004). However, these metrics do not directly reflect the quality of the language model, since they are based on the comparison of the output of the language model with the reference text. In contrast, human preferences are a more direct measure of the quality of a language model. For example, a language model that is able to produce fluent and grammatically correct text is likely to be preferred by humans over a language model that produces short and ungrammatical text. Therefore, it is important to align language models with human preferences.
One approach to aligning language models with preferences is to use human preference data to train a reward model that predicts the probability of a given text being preferred by a human. This reward model can then be used to optimize the language model, by minimizing the difference between this reward model and the languagemodel parameters. This approach is known as reward learning (Hulora et al., 2013;Kiros et al. 2015;Wu et al., 2012;Zhang et al., 2011;Chen et al., 2010;Kiros et al, 2016;Kiros et al 2017;Kiros et al , 2018. The most common reward model is the softmax reward model, which outputs a probability distribution over the words in the text. The language model is optimized by maximizing the likelihood of this reward model, which is equivalent to minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the human preferences. This approach has been successfully applied to various NLP tasks, including machine translation (Kiros et al., , 2016, question answering (Kiros et al, , 2017, and text summarization (Kiros et al. , 2018. However, the softmax reward model has several limitations. First, it requires a large number of human preferences, which can be prohibitive for large datasets. Second, the softmax reward model does not directly optimize the language model, but rather the reward model. This means that the language model must be retrained after each update of the reward model. This can be computationally expensive, especially for large datasets. This can lead to unstable training, where the language model is optimized in one direction, but then the reward model is optimized in a different direction, resulting in a sudden change in the language model. Finally, the softmax reward model only considers the probability of each word in the text, and does not take into account the context of the text.
