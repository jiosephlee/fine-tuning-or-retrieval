\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive results in natural language understanding (e.g., \cite{devlin-etal2018bert,liu2020language}). However, these models are trained without supervision, making it challenging to control their behavior. For example, it is difficult to ensure that a model’s output is consistent with human preferences, or to prevent it from exhibiting undesirable behaviors such as toxicity or misinformation. To address this, researchers have explored various approaches to steer LMs, including reinforcement learning from human feedback \cite{liu2020learning,liu2021training,liu2022learning}, supervised learning from human preferences \cite{liu2019learning,liu2020sentiment}, and reinforcement learning from human preferences with reinforcement learning from human preferences (RLHF) \cite{liu-etal-2022-rlhf}. RLHF is a straightforward extension of RL, where the reward model used to train the LM is also fine-tuned to align with human preferences. This approach has been shown to be effective in improving the quality of LMs \cite{liu_2022_rlhf,liu2022training,liu2023training}, but it is also computationally expensive, requiring the generation of multiple LMs and fine-tuning each one separately. In this work, we propose a new method for RLHF that significantly reduces the computational cost of fine-tuning large LMs while maintaining or improving performance.

We begin by introducing the standard RLHF framework. Given a large unsupervised LM $M$ and a set of human preferences $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n\}$, the goal is to find a policy $\pi^*$ that maximizes the reward $R(\pi, M)$ defined as the average reward obtained by following $\pi$ on the training data:

\begin{equation}
    R(\pi, M) = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{t=1}^{|D|} r_{t}(\pi, M_t),
\end{equation}

where $r_{t}(\pi,M_t)$ is the reward obtained by following policy $\pi$ on the $t$-th training example, and $M_t$ is the model at time step $t$. The policy $\pi^*$ is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward:

\begin{equations}
    \pi^* = \underset{\pi}{\operatorname{argmax}} \; R(\pi, M).
\end{equations}

The standard RLHF approach is to fit a reward model $R^*$ that approximates the reward function $r_{t}$. This is typically done by sampling from the LM and computing the reward for each policy, then fitting a linear regression model to the sampled rewards. The policy $\pi^* = \pi^*(R^*)$ is then fine-tuned to maximize the reward using reinforcement learning. This approach is computationally efficient, but it is also unstable, as the reward model $R^*$ is not guaranteed to accurately reflect the true reward function $r_{\pi}$. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed using a reward model that is trained on a subset of the training data, typically the top $k$ examples \cite{liu2007learning,liu2022fine-tuning,liu2023fine-tuning}. This approach is more stable, but it still requires training a separate reward model for each subset, which can be computationally expensive.

In this paper, we present a new method for RL that avoids the need for separate reward models. Instead, we introduce a new parameterized reward model that can be easily optimized using a simple classification loss. This allows us not only to solve the standard RL problem with a straightforward loss, but also to obtain the optimal policy in closed form. This policy is then fine-tuned with reinforcement learning to maximize the estimated reward, without the need for any additional hyperparameter tuning.
