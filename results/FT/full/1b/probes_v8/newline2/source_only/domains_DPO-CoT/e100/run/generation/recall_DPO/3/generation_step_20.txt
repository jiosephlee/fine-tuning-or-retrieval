\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model for language models (LMs). 
It is trained to maximize the reward function $R(\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the LM's parameters, and the reward function is defined as the expected log-likelihood of the human preferences given the LM's output:
\begin{equation}
R(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{human}}}\left[\log \mathbb{P}_{\pi_{\rm human}}\left(\text{human preference}|\text{LM output}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
The reward function is a natural choice for a reward model, since it directly reflects the human preferences that are the ultimate goal of the DPO algorithm. 
However, the reward function is not necessarily differentiable, and so existing methods for training reward models typically use reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback, which requires sampling from the LM and performing significant hyperparameter tuning to find the optimal reward function.
In this paper, we present a new method for training reward models, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), that does not require sampling from the LM and does not require significant hyperparameter tuning. 
DPO trains a policy $\pi_\text{DPO}(\theta)$ that maximizes the reward function $R$ with respect to $\theta$, and then fine-tunes the policy using standard RL algorithms, such as policy gradient or PPO.
The key insight of DPO is that the optimal policy $\pi_\text{{opt}}$ can be computed exactly from the reward function $R$, and so DPO can be solved using a simple classification loss:
\begin{equations}
\label{eq:direct_ppo}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} &= \mathbb{E}_\pi_\text{D PO}\left[\log \frac{\pi_\text{D Po}(x)}{\pi_\text{opt}(x)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}\left[\log \left(\frac{\pi_\text{{opt}(x)}(x)}{\pi_\pi(x)}\right)\right],
\end{aligned}
\end{equations}
where $\pi_\text{opt}$ is the optimal policy, $\pi_\text{human}$ is the human preference distribution, and $\pi_\text{LM}$ is the LM's distribution over outputs.
The classification loss in Eq.~\ref{eq:direct_pppo} is a standard RL objective, but it is not differentiable, so we must approximate it using a surrogate loss function. 
We use the KL-divergence between the optimal policy $\pi_{\text{opt}}$ and the policy $\pi_\text{(DPO)}$ that maximizes the reward $R$:
\begin{equality}
\label{eq:dpo_loss}
\mathcal{D}_\text{D PO} = \mathbb{D}_{KL}\left(\pi_{\text{DPO}}\mid \pi_{\text{Opt}}\mid \mid R\right).
\end{equality}
The KL-divergence in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dpo_loss} is a standard RL loss, but it is not necessarily differentiable. 
To make it differentiable, we can use a surrogate loss function that approximates the KL-divergence with a smooth function, such as the squared exponential function:
\begin{equity}
\label{eq:squared_exponential}
\mathcal{S}_{\text{DPO},\text{SE}} = \mathbb{S}_{\text {DPO},\text {SE}}(R) = \mathbb{T}_{\text {SE}}\left(\mathbb{D}_{\text {KL}}\left(\pi_{\rm DPO}\mid \pi_{\rm Opt}\mid \mid R\mid \right)\right),
\end{equity}
where $\mathbb{T}_{\rm SE}$ is the squared exponential function, $\mathbb{D}_{p\mid q}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, and $\mathbb{S}_{p\mid q}(f)$ is the Jensen-Shannon divergence between two distributions $p$ and $q$ with weights $f$ and $1 - f$.
The KL-divergence is not differentiable at the optimal policy $\pi^*$, so we must approximate the KL-divergence using a smooth function, such that the resulting loss is differentiable. 
The squared exponential function is a popular choice for this purpose, and has the advantage of being easy to optimize numerically.
