\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model for the LM, which is optimized by reinforcement learning to maximize the reward function. The reward function is a linear combination of the LM's output and the human preferences, and the goal of DPO is to find the optimal policy that maximizes the reward function. This is a generalization of the standard RLHF method, which uses a reward function that is a linear combination of LM outputs and human preferences, but does not optimize the reward function directly. 

The main advantage of DPO is that it can be solved exactly, unlike the standard RLHF method. This allows us to use a simple classification loss to train the reward function, rather than sampling from the LM and optimizing the reward function using reinforcement learning. This makes DPO much simpler to implement and train than RLHF, and also makes it more robust to changes in the reward function, such as when the reward function is updated during training.

We evaluate DPO on several tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. On sentiment analysis, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF by a large margin, and also performs comparably or better than other methods. On summarization, DPO achieves state-of-the-art results on the SQuAD 2.0 dataset, and also outperforms PPO by a large margin. These results demonstrate that DPO is a powerful tool for controlling the behavior of large-scale unsupervised LMs.

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive performance on many downstream tasks, including natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [Devlin et al., 2018], GPT [Brown et al., 2019], and XLNet [Yang et al., 2017]), question answering (QA), and machine translation (MT). However, achieving precise control of the behavior of these models is difficult due to their completely unsupervised nature. Existing methods for gaining steerability include collecting human preferences for the relative quality of model outputs, and fine-tuning the unsupervised LM using Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) [Chen et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021]. While RLHF is a powerful method for fine-tuning LMs, it is often unstable, requiring significant hyperparameter tuning and fine-tuning iterations to converge. In this paper, we introduce a new method for fine-tuning unsupervised LMs that does not require sampling from the LM or performing significant hyperparameter optimization. Our method, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is based on a simple parameterization of the reward function used in RLHF, and solves the standard RLHF problem using a simple classification loss. We refer to this method as DPO.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervsed LMs have been trained on large datasets of unlabeled text, and have achieved impressive performance on a variety of downstream tasks. For example, BERT [Devlin-etal-2018-large] has achieved state-of-the-art results for many NLP tasks, including question answering, machine translation, and natural language understanding. GPT [Brown etal., 2019] and XLNet [Yang-etal-2017] are two other recent large-scale unsupervised LM models. While these models have achieved impressive results, they are trained on large unlabeled datasets, and their behavior is largely determined by the training data. In contrast, supervised LM models are trained on labeled data, and their behavior is controlled by the human-supervised reward function. However, obtaining large amounts of human-supervised data is often impractical, and so methods for training unsupervised LMs have been developed. One approach is to use a pre-trained LM as a base model, and fine-tune it using a reward function that reflects human preferences. This is the approach taken by PPO-based RLHF [Chen et al. 2020; Li-etal-2021], which uses a reward function defined as a linear combination of the pre-trained LM's output and human preferences. Another approach is to use a reward function that is defined directly on the LM's output, without using a pre-trained LM as the base model. This is the approach used by DPO.

One challenge with using a reward function defined on the LM's output is that the reward function must be learned from scratch, and this can be computationally expensive. In this paper, our approach is to use a simple parameterization of a reward function that is used in RLHF, but optimize the reward function directly using reinforcement learning. This allows us to avoid the need to learn a reward function from scratch, and also makes the reward function more robust to changes in reward function, such as updates during training. We refer to this approach as DPO.
