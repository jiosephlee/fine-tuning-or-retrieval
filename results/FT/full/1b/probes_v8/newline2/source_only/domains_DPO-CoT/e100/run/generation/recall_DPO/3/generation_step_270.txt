\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free RLHF algorithm. In the standard RLHF setting, one first learns a reward model $R_{\theta}(p)$ that maps from the latent policy space $p$ to a scalar representing the reward obtained by following it, and then uses an RL algorithm to optimize the parameters $\theta$ to maximize the expected discounted reward $R_{\theta}^\pi = \mathbb{E}_{p \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{t} R_{\theta}(p \mid \hat{p}(t)) \right]$ where $\hat{p}(t) = \int p \, \mathrm{d} \pi(\cdot | t)$ is the posterior distribution over policies given the environment state $t$. The reward model $\theta$ is learned from human preferences collected via reinforcement learning from human feedback, typically using a policy gradient method such as PPO. 1

The RLHF pipeline in Figure~\ref{fig:rlh fp pipeline} shows the basic idea of the approach. While the RLHF pipeline is simple, it has several limitations. First, the reward model $\theta$ must be learned from human preferences, which is often difficult and time-consuming. Second, the RL algorithm used to optimize $\theta$, such as PPO, requires access to a replay buffer of trajectories sampled from the environment, which is not available in many real-world applications. Third, the RL algorithm used for optimization must be stable, since the reward model is learned from human preferences, and the reward model must be accurate, since it determines the final policy. Finally, the RLHF pipeline is typically implemented using a Monte-Carlo estimate of the reward, which can be unstable when the policy being optimized is not close to the optimal policy.

In this paper, we propose a new RLHF algorithm, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), that addresses these limitations. DPO uses a different parameterization of the reward function, which allows us to express the optimal policy in terms of the reward function and the initial policy. This allows us to solve the standard reward maximization problem exactly, without needing to estimate the reward or use Monte-Carlo estimates. We also show that the optimal policy can be expressed in terms of the reward and the initial policy, and that the optimal policy is the same as the policy learned by the standard RLHF algorithm. Therefore, we can use the standard RLHF algorithm to optimize the reward function, and obtain the optimal reward function $\hat{R}_\theta$. We can then use the reward function $\hat{ R}_\theta$ to optimize the initial policy, obtaining the optimal policy $\pi^*_\hat{R}_\hat{R}_θ$. We refer to this policy as the \textit{direct preference policy}, since it is the policy that maximizes the reward function $\hat R_\hat{R}_\pi$ directly, without needing to optimize the reward function first. We refer to the reward function $\hat {R}_\hat{ R}_\pi$ as the \textit{suggested reward function}, since it is the reward function that would be suggested by a human preference model $\hat R$.

We can express the optimal policy $\pi^\ast_\hat{R}_ \hat{R}_\text{opt}$ in terms of the suggested reward function $\hat{r}_\hat{R}$ and the initial policy $\pi_0$ as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:opt_policy}
\pi^\ast_\hat R_\hat{ R}_\text{opt} = \argmax_{\pi \in \mathcal{P}} \hat{r}_\text{opt}(p; \pi) = \pi_0 \exp\left( -\beta \hat{R}_0(p) - \hat{r}_0(p)\right),
\end{equation}

where $\hat{r}_0$ is the suggested reward function for the initial policy $\pi_{0}$. The suggested reward function $\hat r_0$ is simply the reward function $\hat r$ for the initial policy $\hat{p}_0$:
