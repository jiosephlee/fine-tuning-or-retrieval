\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free RLHF algorithm. In this paper, we propose a new parameterization of reward models that enables extraction of the optimal policy in closed form. This allows us to solve the standard DPO problem with only a simple binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for RLHF sampling or hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments show that DPO is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, outperforming existing methods on sentiment alignment, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models have achieved state-of-the-art performance on a wide range of natural language understanding (NLU) tasks, including question answering (Devlin et al., 2019; Radford et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2017; Weston et al., 2016), machine translation (Brown et al., 2015; Cho et al., 2014; Sutskever et al., 2013), and summarization (Kiros et al., 2010). However, these models are trained on massive amounts of text, and their behavior is difficult to control due to the completely unsatisfied supervision requirements of supervised learning algorithms. For example, the GPT-3 model (Tay et al., 201 model, the GPT-4 model (Ghazvininejad et al., 2021) has 175 billion parameters and 175 trillion parameters, respectively. These models are trained on a large corpus of text, but they do not provide any explicit labels of the quality of the generated text. Instead, they learn to produce high-quality text by maximizing the likelihood of the text given the training data. While this approach has been successful, it is difficult to use these models to achieve specific goals, such as producing text of a certain quality or generating responses to questions in a dialogue system. In this paper, our goal is to develop methods for controlling the behavior of large unsupervised language models, so that they can be used to achieve specific goals, rather than simply maximizing the likelihood of the training data.

One approach to controlling the behavior of large language models is reinforcement learning (RL), which has been successfully applied to many NLU tasks (Schulman et al., 2011; Mnih et al., 2012; Kavukcuoglu et al., 20110; Le et al., 201 11; Wu et al., 20183; Wu et al., , 2019. In RL, a model is trained to maximize a reward function that reflects the desirability of its behavior. The reward function is learned from human feedback, and the model is then fine-tuned to maximize the reward without drifting too far away from the original model. This procedure is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF (Kaelbling et al., 1996; Littman et al., 1999; Schmidhuber, 1992; Lai and Schmidhuber, , 2000. However, RLHF is a complicated procedure, involving RL sampling from the model, estimating the reward function, and fine-tuning the model using reinforcement learning. In addition, the reward function must be carefully chosen, since it determines the final behavior of the model. For example, if the reward function is the negative log-likelihood of the correct answer to a question, then the model will optimize to generate answers that are as similar as possible to the correct answers, which may not be what the user wants. In this paper, instead of using a reward function learned from human feedback, we use a reward model that reflects human preferences directly. We refer to this procedure as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

A related approach is to use a reward model to optimize a different objective, such as the perplexity of the generated text (Kiros et al, 2010; Radford et al, 2018; Brown et al., 20115; Weston et al., , 2020. This approach is known as reward shaping (RS) (Kiros et al. 2010; Radof et al., 20181; Brown et al., , 20091; it is also closely related to the method of optimizing the average log-likelihood of the ground-truth answer (LPA) (Schulman et 2011; Lai and Schmiderer, 2011; Littman et 2011). However, these methods optimize a different objective than the reward function used in RLHF, and therefore do not correspond to the same optimization problem. In this paper, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to consider the problem of optimizing the reward function used in DRLHF, and we refer to this as DPO.
