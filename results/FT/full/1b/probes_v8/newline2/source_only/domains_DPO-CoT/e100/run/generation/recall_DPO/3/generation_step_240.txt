\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free RLHF algorithm. In the standard RLHF setting, one first learns a reward model $R_{\theta}(p)$ that maps from the latent policy space $p$ to a scalar representing the reward that would be obtained by following $p$ with policy $\theta$. Then one optimizes the policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize the expected return $R_{\theta}(\pi_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[R_{\theta}(p) \mid \text{D}\mid\right]$ under policy $\pi_\theta$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is the training dataset. Finally, one uses the learned reward model $R_{old}$ to optimize the policy $\pi_\hat{\theta}$ to maximize the reward $R_{\hat{\theta}}(\pi_\hat{\theta})$ without drifting too far from $\pi_\theta$.

The standard RLHF procedure is illustrated in Figure 1. The key challenge with this approach is that the reward model $R_{m old}$ is learned from the data, and so it is sensitive to the particular distribution of the training data. For example, if the training data is biased towards high-quality policy $\pi_\theta^*$, then the reward model $R_{{\m old}}$ will be biased towards high rewards, and the learned policy $\pi_\hat{{\m old}} = R_{\hat{{\m old}}}(\pi_\hat{{\theta}})$ will also be biased towards high rewards. This can cause the learned policy to diverge from the desired policy $\pi_\theta$. To address this, existing methods for RLHF typically perform a few steps of reinforcement learning (RL) on the learned reward model $ R_{old}$ to obtain a new reward function $ R_{\hat{{{\m old}}}}$ that better aligns with the desired policy $\pi_{\hat{{\theta}}}$, and then use $ R_{\hat{	heta}}$ to optimize the policy $\hat{\pi}_{\hat{{\theta}}}$. This procedure is repeated until the reward function converges to a steady state, which is often referred to as the \textit{stable policy}. However, the convergence of the reward function is slow, and the stability of the final policy $\hat{\pi}_\hat{{\theta }}$ is often not guaranteed.

In this paper, we propose a new method for RLHF called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). In contrast to existing methods, DPO does not fit a reward model $ R_{\theta}$ and then optimize the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ to maximize $ R_{\theta}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})$; instead, DPO directly optimizes the policy $\hat{\theta}$ to satisfy the preference relation $\mathcal{R} = \mathbb{P}_{\pi_{\hat{\theta}}} \mid \mathcal{D} \mid \mid \mathbb{P}_\pi \mid \mid \mid \text{R}_{\hat{\theta}} \mid \mid \leq 1$ without fitting any reward model. Intuitively, the preference relation $\mathbb{P}_ho \mid \mathcal D$ represents the probability of the preferred policy $\hat{\pi}$ given the training data $\mathcal D$, and the constraint $\mathbb{P}_{ho} \mid \mathcal {D} \mid \leq \mathbb{P} _\pi$ ensures that the preferred policy is not too far from the true policy $\pi$. The preference relation $\mathbb {P}_ho \div \mathcal{D}$ can be interpreted as the probability of the preferred model given the training data $\cal D$, and the preference relation $\mathbf{R} = \frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{\theta}}}\mid \mathcal{ D}}{\mathbb{P}_{old}\mid \mathcal { D}}$ represents the probability of a preferred policy $\hat{\theta}$. The preference relation $\bf{R}$ is the key difference between DPO and existing methods for RLHF. In DPO, we optimize the policy $\hat{{\theta}}$ to satisfy the preference relation ${\bf{R}}$ without fitting any reward function $ R_{old}$.
