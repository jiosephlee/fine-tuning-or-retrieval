The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a principled way to optimize a language model's reward function, which can be anything that depends on the model's output to reward high-quality language. This includes existing reward functions used by language models, like the PPL, ROUGE, or F1, as well as more creative functions, such as rewarding structures relevant to the training data. Our method, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), includes a simple but efficient algorithm that does not require computing the KL-divergence between the model's distribution and a target distribution, making it faster and more practical than existing methods.

Related Work
Language models are algorithms that output long-term dependencies in language in response to a query. Two popular families of language models are neural language models (NLMs) and contextualized word representations (CWRs). Neural language models usefully achieve state-of-the-art performance on large language datasets, but have a bias towards more fluent but factually incorrect output. CWRs address this issue by encoding word context, but have no built-in memory and so perform poorly on long sentences. There is also a class of methods that produce distributions over text, rather than single texts; these include zero-shot prompting, attention-based models, and language generation models. Despite their different architectures, these zero-shot prompting methods often perform comparably or better than neural language models on a variety of benchmarks. However, the most successful of these methods, GPT-NB, is a neural language model with strong natural language understanding capabilities. There has been a surge of interest in language models of late, and several high-performing language models have been trained on large language datasets. We refer the reader to a recent survey by Brown et al. (2020) for a more comprehensive overview of language models.
The field of preference learning has seen significant progress in the last few years. One line of work (Schulman et al., 2017;Vinyals et al., 2015;Sutskever, 2014;LeCun et al., 2018;Cho et al., 2014;Korbak et al., 2019;Ranzato et al., 2016;Liu et al., 201 accidentally discovered the connection between language models and preference learning. Another line of work (Glorot and Bengio, 2010;Bengio et al., 2003;Kondor et al., 2005;Collobert and Weston, 2008;Storkey and Williams, 2010;Storkey andWilliams, 2011;Wu and Lin, 2011;Zhu and Hu, 2012;Joulin et al., 2013;Kiros et al., 2010;Kiros et al. 2013;Karras et al., 2012;Karras and Ranzato, 2015;Radford et al., 201 machine learning models that learn to generate synthetic reviews or blog posts that satisfy human preferences. Most of these works optimize a function of the model's probability distribution over reviews or posts, called the reward function, and train the model using standard optimization algorithms, such as stochastic gradient descent. The reward function is typically defined over reviews or posts in a review ranking task or a blog post classification task, and the goal is to find the review or post that maximizes the reward. In this paper, we show that the reward function for these models can also be used as a reward function for a language model, allowing us to translate human preferences into natural language.
