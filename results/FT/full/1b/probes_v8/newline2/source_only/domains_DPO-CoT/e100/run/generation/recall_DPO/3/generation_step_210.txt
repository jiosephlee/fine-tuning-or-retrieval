\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free RLHF algorithm. In the standard RLHF setting, one first learns a reward model $R_{\theta}(p)$ that maps from the latent policy space $p$ to a scalar representing the reward obtained by following it, and then uses an RL algorithm to optimize the parameters $\theta$ to maximize the expected discounted reward $R_{\theta}^\pi = \mathbb{E}_{p \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{t} R_{\theta}(p \mid \hat{p}(t)) \right]$ where $\hat{p}(t) = \int p \, \mathrm{d} \tilde{p}(t \mid \theta)$ is the policy learned from the data distribution $\mathcal{D}$, and $\tilde{p}(s \mid \theta)$ denotes the parameterized distribution induced by the reward model $\theta$. The RLHF objective is then to find $\theta^*$ that maximizes $R_{\theta^*}^\pi$.

One way to learn a reward model is to use a reinforcement learning algorithm such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Direct Acquiescence Search (DAS). However, these algorithms require sampling from the underlying policy distribution, which is expensive for large models and can be unstable. Another approach is to use a parametric reward model, such as the Q-function, but this requires tuning the parameters of the model, which can be difficult and unstable. In this paper, we propose a new approach to learning a reward model, based on the observation that the reward model can be equivalently represented as a function of the language model distribution $\pi(\cdot | \theta)$, i.e., $R_{\theta, \pi} = \mathbb{D}_{\pi}\left[R_{\theta}(p), \log \pi(p)\right]$ where $\mathbb{D}_{p}(\cdot, \cdot)$ is the evidence lower bound (ELBO) for the distribution $\pi$. This representation allows us to express the reward model as a function of the parameters $\theta$ and the language model distribution $\mathcal{L}(\cdot|\theta)$, which we refer to as the preference model. We can then optimize the parameters $\theta$, and the preference model $\mathbb{D}_\pi$, to maximize the ELBO $\mathbb{D}(\theta, \mathbb{D}_p \mid \mathcal{D})$.

The standard RLHF objective is then given by maximizing the expected discounted reward subject to the constraint that the policy $\hat{p} = \argmax_{p} \mathbb{D} \left[\mathbb{D}_{\pi}(p, \log \pi(p)) \mid \mathcal D\right]$. However, the ELBO $\mathcal{D}(\theta,\mathbb{D}_q \mid \mathcal {D})$ is not necessarily convex, so we cannot simply take the gradient of the ELBO with respect to the parameters $\theta$ using the chain rule. Instead, we can use the fact that the ELBO is a special case of the more general Kullback-Leibler divergence, and that the KL-divergence is a convex function.
