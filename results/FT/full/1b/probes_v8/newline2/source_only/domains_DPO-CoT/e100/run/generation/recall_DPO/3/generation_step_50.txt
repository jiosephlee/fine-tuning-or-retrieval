\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward function estimation algorithm combined with a simple classification loss. Existing methods for RLHF optimize a more complex reward function that is estimated from human preferences; DPO estimates this reward function directly from human preferences and then fine-tunes a policy from it using standard reinforcement learning. We show that the optimal reward function for the policy can be expressed in closed form, allowing DPO to be solved exactly using a simple classification loss. Our experiments show that DPO is at least as effective as existing methods for RLHF, including other algorithms that optimize a complex reward function, and is also simpler to implement and train. We expect that DPO will be a useful tool for fine-tuning unsupervised language models for specific objectives.

\section{Related Work} Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training algorithms have been developed over the past few years \citep{brown2020language, devlin-etal-2019-bert, devlin2020bert, elmasry-etal-2021-large, ouyang2021learning, sutskever2022sequence}. These algorithms produce parameterized models of arbitrary size, and training procedures that do not require supervision. However, these models lack any notion of relative importance, and the ability to produce responses of varying quality. Prior work has explored using language models as implicit reward functions \citep{lester2021reinforcement, chen2021learning, bai2021learning, liu2021learning, bubeck2021training, bai2022fine, bai2023fine}, but these methods must first estimate a reward function from human preferences, which is costly and unstable. In this paper we present a single-stage method for RLHF that does not require estimating a reward function from human feedback (i.e., a reward model). Our method, \textit{Direct Preferred Optimization} (DPO}, is an extension of the DPO algorithm due to \citet{levine2022direct}, which trains a policy to maximize a reward function that is fit to human preferences. However, the reward function in the original DPO paper must be optimized multiple times, first to find an initial policy, and then to further improve it using reinforcement learning (RL). We show that the reward function defined in the DPO paper can be inverted to give the reward function for the optimal policy, and thus the optimal policy can be found using a simple classification loss instead of RL. This paper makes the following main contributions: \textbf{Direct Preference Optimization}: We present a new method for RLHF, \textit{DPO}, that fine-tunes a language model to match human preferences without fitting a reward model or performing RL. \textbf{Experiments}: We evaluate DPO on sentiment, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, outperforming existing methods on all tasks. \textbf{Comparison with State of the Art}: On sentiment and summarization, DPO produces results equivalent or superior to existing methods that optimize a complex reward model.

\section{Related work} The RLHF pipeline is well-documented \citep{leister2021reinforcement,bai2021learning,ouyang2021learning,sutskever2022seq2seq, bai2022finetuning, bai2023finetuning}. Here we review the key components. First, a language model is trained on a large body of text, often from the \textit{Times Literary Supplement} \citep{times_lesser_2018}, to obtain a large, high-quality parameterized model $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{w})$ where $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the input sequence of words, and $\mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}$ is the word sequence parameterization. Next, a reward function $R:\mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is given for the inputs $\mathbf{x}  \in \mathcal X$, specifying the quality of the output sequence $\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}))$, where we assume access to the inverse of the parametric LM $R(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{P}^*(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})$ where $\mathcal{P}^\*$ is the language model's sampling policy. Finally, a single-stage RL algorithm is used to optimize $\mathcal{L}$ to maximize the observed reward $R$.
