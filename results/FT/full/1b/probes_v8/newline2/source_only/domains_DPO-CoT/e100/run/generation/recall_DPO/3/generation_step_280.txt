\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free RLHF algorithm. In the standard RLHF setting, one first learns a reward model $R_{\theta}(p)$ that maps from the latent policy space $p$ to a scalar representing the reward obtained by following it, and then uses an RL algorithm to optimize the parameters $\theta$ to maximize the expected return $R_{\theta}^\pi$ over trajectories $\pi$. In DPO, we instead directly optimize the parameters $\theta$, using a loss function that is a function of the reward model $R_{old}$, i.e., the reward model learned in the standard RLHF setting. This loss is then optimized using standard RL algorithms, such as PPO. We refer to this as \textit{Direct preference optimization} (DPO). 

The key insight behind DPO is that the reward model $R_\theta$ learned in the standard RL framework can be reparameterized as a function of the policy $\pi$ and the preference distribution $p$ (see Appendix \ref{app:reward_model}). This allows us to express the reward model as a function of the parameters $\theta$ of the policy, and then optimize the parameters $\theta^*$ of the policy using a loss function that depends on the reward model $R_old$. This loss function is a \textit{convex combination of negative rewards}, which is a standard way of defining a \textit{stochastic gradient descent objective} (see Appendix \ref {app:convex_combination_of_negative_rewards}). The resulting optimization problem is a standard convex optimization problem, which can be solved efficiently using off-the-shelf solvers. We refer to this algorithm as \textit{DPO}, and show that it can be used to solve the standard RL framework for preference learning, without the need for sampling from or fine-tuning the large LM.

We evaluate DPO on several tasks involving unsupervised language models. On sentiment analysis, we show that DPO can be used to align the sentiment of generations produced by large LMs with human preferences, as measured by the average normalized difference between the human and model ratings. On summarization, we show that D PO can be used to align model generations with human preferences for generating coherent summaries. On single-turn dialogue, we show that D Po can be used to align responses with human preferences for generating high-quality dialogues. Our contributions are as follows:

\textbf{Main contribution.} We present DPO, a simple method for solving the standard RLHF problem without the need for sampling or fine-tuning the LM. DPO can be used as a drop-in replacement for existing methods for RLHF, and we show that it can achieve comparable or better performance on sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments also suggest that DPO may be more robust to the choice of RL algorithm used for fine-tuning, and that it may be applicable to a wider range of RLHF settings.

\textbf{Related work.} Prior work on RLHF has focused on two main approaches: 1) fitting a reward model to human preferences, and then using RL to optimize the parameters of the reward model to maximize the reward; and 2) using RL to optimize the policy parameters directly, without fitting a reward model. The first approach is typically referred to as \textit{model-based RLHF}, and the second as \textit{reward model-free RLHF}. The most common model-based RLHF algorithms are PPO and its variants, which optimize the parameters of a policy network using a loss function that includes a term that is a function of a reward model learned from human preferences. Other popular model-based RLHF algorithms include GAIL \citep{gao2018generative} and its variants \citep{wang2019training, li2020training, li2021training, li2021learning}. The reward model-free RLHF algorithms include methods such as \textit{Reward Shaping} \citep{leandro2019reward}, \textit{Reward Alignment} \citep{zhang2020reward}, and \textit{Reward Perturbation} \citep{liu2021reward}. While these algorithms do not require a reward model, they typically use sampling from the unsupervised LM during training to estimate the reward function, and then use reinforcement learning to optimize the parameters of a reward network that maps from the policy to the reward. In this paper, we focus on reward model-free RLHF, and show that it can achieve similar or better performance than existing methods without the need for sampling.
