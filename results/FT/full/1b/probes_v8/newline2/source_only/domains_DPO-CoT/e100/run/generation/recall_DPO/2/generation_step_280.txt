The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new algorithm for optimizing the alignment between a language model and human preferences, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO is an end-to-end optimization procedure that leverages the power of neural networks to learn a mapping from language to preferences. DPO is simple to implement and can be used with any language model. We evaluate DPO on two datasets of human preferences for movie reviews: the MovieLens-100K dataset and the MovieLens-1K dataset. On both datasets, DPO outperforms baselines that optimize for model performance or maximize human preferences. DPO also shows promise for improving the quality of language models in downstream tasks such as question answering and machine translation.

Problem: This paper proposes a new approach to the problem of generating text that is semantically coherent with a given source text. Our method, called GENIco, jointly learns a graph neural network model for representing text and a graph attention mechanism for computing the coherence score of pairs of tokens. Unlike previous methods that either use hand-engineered features or directly optimize for coherence, GENIco learns a model for representing text that is general and effective. We evaluate GENIco on four coherence benchmarks and several related tasks, including machine translation, question-answering, and summarization. On all evaluation sets, GENIco improves over the current state-of-the-art in all but one task. In addition, our model is orders of magnitude faster than previous methods and does not require any external memory or long-term storage.

Problem: We present a new approach to the task of sentiment analysis, which is the task of automatically classifying a piece of text into one of the emotions of the sentiment family. There are two main open problems in sentiment analysis. The first is to build large-scale sentiment datasets, since sentiment analysis models are only as good as the data that they are trained on. The second is to build accurate sentiment analysis models that generalize well from training data. We solve both problems by introducing SentiStem, a method for stemming text down to a single root word, and SentiMax, a maximum likelihood estimator for sentiment analysis that takes advantage of the order of words in a sentence to better capture sentiment. We evaluate several sentiment analysis models on two datasets with multiple sentiments: the MovieReview dataset, which contains sentiment judgments on movie stills, and the MovieLens-100k dataset, which contains sentiment on movie titles and ratings. SentiMax outperforms several baselines, including a random forest sentiment analysis model, on both datasets. Our code is publicly available at https://github.com/ucsd-ir/sentiMax.
