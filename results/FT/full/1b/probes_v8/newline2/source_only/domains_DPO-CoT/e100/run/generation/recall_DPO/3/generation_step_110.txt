\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model for the LM, which is optimized to maximize the reward function $R(\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the LM's parameters.  The reward function is typically a function of the LM's output, but it could also be a function of the LM and its environment, or even a function of the LM alone.  In this paper, we focus on the case where the reward function is a function of the LM output, and we assume that the reward function is known to the optimizer.  We refer to this as the \textit{known reward} setting.

The standard approach to optimizing the reward function is reinforcement learning (RL).  In RL, the reward function is used to define a Markov decision process (MDP), and the goal is to find a policy $\pi$ that maximizes the expected return $R(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[ \mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{A}]$.  The policy $\pi$ is typically learned using an actor-critic algorithm, such as PPO, DDPG, or RL4K.  The critic $\pi^\star$ is typically learned using a policy gradient method, such as policy gradient or value iteration.  The critic $\hat{\pi}^\star$ is typically initialized with a pre-trained policy $\pi_0$ and updated using a policy gradient method.  The critic $\mathcal{L}(\hat{\pi}, \pi) = \mathcal{D}\mid \mathcal{S} \mid \mathbb{E}_\pi[\log \pi(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{S}) - \log \pi(\mathbb{D})]$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is the data distribution, $\mathcal{S}$ is the environment, and $\mathbb{D}$ is the policy distribution.  The policy $\hat{\pi} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}(\pi^\star)$ is the policy that maximizes the reward function $R$.

In practice, the reward function is often unknown, and must be estimated from human feedback.  This is the \textit{reinforcement learning from human feedback} (RLHF) setting.  In RLHF, the reward function is estimated using a supervised learning algorithm, such as contrastive estimation or maximum likelihood estimation.  The reward function $\hat{R}$ is then used to define an MDP, and the goal is to learn a policy $\pi$ using an RL algorithm, such as POMDP, DDPG, RL4K, or RL.  The policy $\pi = \mathcal{G}(\hat{R})$ is typically learned using the critic $\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{T}^{-1}(\hat{\pi}^\ast)$, where $\mathcal{T}$ is a policy gradient method, and the critic $\hat{\pi}$ is initialized with the pre-trained policy $\pi$.

The standard RLHF algorithm is a two-step procedure.  First, the reward function $\hat{ R}$ is estimated using a supervised algorithm, such as contrast estimation or maximum likelihood estimation, and the resulting policy $\pi$ is used as the initial policy for the second step.  Second, the reward function $\mathcal{L}$ is used to define an MDA, and the goal is for the policy $\pi$ to maximize the reward function $\mathbb{E}_ \pi[\mathcal{L} \mid \mathbf{D}]$.  The policy is typically learned using an RL algorithm, typically POMDP, DPG, RL4K, RL, or RLHF.  The critic $\tilde{\pi} = \tilde{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{L})$ is typically initialized with the pre-trained reward function $\mathcal{T}^{-\star}(\pi^\star)$.  The critic $\tildes{\pi}$ is updated using a policy gradient algorithm, such as DPG, PPO, or RL4K.

The standard RLHF procedure is unstable, and can drift from the optimal policy $\pi^*$ to a suboptimal policy $\pi^*$, which may not maximize the reward function $\hat{\mathcal{R}}$.  This instability is due to the fact that the reward function $\hat\mathcal{R}$ is not necessarily the same as the reward function $\mathbf{R}$ that the optimal policy $\pi^\star$ maximizes.
