\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward function estimation algorithm followed by a standard RL optimization. Existing methods for RLHF use one of two approaches to estimating the reward. In the first approach, the reward is estimated from samples of the generated text using a model like GPT-3 \cite{brown2020glue}. In the second approach, the reward is modeled as a function of the parameters of interest, and then an optimization that seeks the parameter vector that maximizes the function is performed, typically reinforcement learning from human feedback. We will refer to this second type of algorithm as a Reward Learning (RL) algorithm.

One of the key challenges of RLHF is that the human preferences are collected using a single set of human labels, \textit{i.e.}, there is only a single set of generations to evaluate how well the reward model reflects the true reward. As the RLHF algorithm runs multiple times, each time with a different parameters, the final policy learned will vary, and the final reward may differ as well. To optimize the final policy to the desired human preferences, we typically perform an additional RL optimization on top of the RLHF algorithm, which maximizes the estimated reward without causing the policy to drift too far from the original policy. This additional optimization is commonly referred to as PPO (Collobert et al., 2011); we will use the shorthand RLHF+PO.

It is important to note that the PPO optimization only adjusts the policy; the unsupervised LM is never used to generate text, only to provide human feedback. Therefore, the final policy learned by the RLHF+PO algorithm is an unsupervised LM optimized to provide human feedback that accurately reflects the desired desired of the human annotators. This is in contrast to other methods for gaining steerability, such as Preference Learning (PL) (Ghazvininejad et al., 2020; Park et al., 2021), which trains a supervised LM on human labeling of reference generations alongside unlabeled test generations, and Fine-TunesRL (FTRL), which performs RL on the unsupervised LM directly, producing a separate reward and policy parameterization. While PL and RLHF share the same RL+PPO loop, the key distinction is in the way the rewards are estimated. While RLHF uses an estimated human preference model to estimate a change in reward for a given policy shift, PL trains a separate reward model that is optimized to match the observed human labeling data as closely as possible, and then Fine-TunesRL to maximize the reward estimate without causing the policy to deviate too far from the initial policy. Finally, FTRL is an extension of RLHF that jointly optimizes the reward and policy parameters, maximizing the given reward function without violating the desired policy.

While both PL and RLHF can be effectively implemented using GPT-3, FTRL requires an explicit reward model specification. Meanwhile, PL does not utilize the power of large unsupervised LM models and is relatively simple to implement using standard reinforcement learning algorithms. As such, we will compare the three methods in a series of experimental studies. The first two studies compare RLHF, PL, and RLHF+PO side-by-side. In the first set of experiments, we consider fixed sets of human preferences, where the goal is to determine which methods yield the most satisfying human feedback. In the second set of experiments, the goal is to summarize or conduct single-turn dialogue, where the order of the methods is reversed. These experiments are important because they probe the three methods under conditions where the objective function (reward, satisfaction, or summarization score) is publicly available, allowing us to directly compare the performance of the three methods. While satisfaction and summary quality are commonly considered desirable properties of LMs, it is worth noting that giving an unsupervised LM an objective of satisfaction or summarization score can be viewed as a human-centric approach that allows us to evaluate LMs in ways that are closer to how humans interact with them than traditional objective functions used by LMs and human-lab annotators, who typically use an objective based on text quality metrics such as the F1 score. We therefore view these final set of experiments as an evaluation of how well these three methods fair when evaluated against a human-judged quality metric.

Despite their similarities, there are important differences between RLHF, PL,  and FTRL. First, the computational complexity of the RLHF+PO loop is O(n 3 ) where n is the length of a generation to evaluate, making it impractical for very long generations. Second, the KL-divergence used in the PPO update can be difficult to optimize for, especially for low-entropy LMs, leading to variance in the PPO updates and making the algorithm generally unstable. Third, the KL-divergences used in RLHF and PL (fixed at 20KL for simplicity) bias the algorithms towards policies that are already relatively fluent, potentially missing the benefits of unsupervised expansion.
