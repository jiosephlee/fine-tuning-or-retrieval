\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free RLHF algorithm. In this paper, we present a new parameterization of reward models that enables extraction of the optimal policy in closed form. \section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models have achieved state-of-the-art performance on a wide range of natural language understanding (NLU) tasks, including question answering (Devlin et al., 2019; Radford et al., 2018), machine translation (Brown et al., 2017), and summarization (Kiros et al., 2015). However, achieving precise control of the behavior of these models is difficult due to their completely unsupervised nature. While it is possible to collect human feedback on the quality of model generations (Bai et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022), this is a labor-intensive process, and the resulting feedback is not always reliable (Bai et al. 2020; Li and Bansal, 2021; Wu and Bansal, 2019). Moreover, even if the feedback is reliable, it is often difficult to find a model that aligns with it, since the model's parameters are typically very large and the reward function is complex. Finally, the reward function is often unknown, and must be estimated from human feedback, which is also time-consuming and error-prone.

To address these challenges, we propose a new method for fine-tuning large unsupervised language models to align with human preferences. Existing methods for aligning language models with human preferences typically use reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback (Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., , 2022. In this setting, the human preferences are represented by a reward function that measures the quality of model generations, and the goal is to find a policy that maximizes this reward. The reward function is typically estimated from human feedback, but can also be learned from data (Wu et al., 2024). The RLHF algorithm finds the optimal policy by optimizing a reinforcement learning objective that approximates the reward function (e.g., the cross-entropy loss between the true reward and the policy's predictions). However, RLHF requires sampling from the language model, which can be expensive and slow, and can also lead to unstable results (Wu et al. 2022).

In this paper, we present Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a new method for aligning language models without RLHF sampling. DPO uses a different parameterization of the reward function that allows us to extract the optimal policy in closed-form. This policy is then used as the reward function in the standard RLHF algorithm, replacing the sampling step. We refer to this new algorithm as DPO, and show that it has several advantages over RLHF. First, DPO does not require any hyperparameters to be tuned, making it much simpler to implement. Second, DPO is stable, meaning that it converges to the same optimal policy as RLHF, even if the initial policy is not close to the optimal policy. Third, DPO is computationally efficient, requiring only a simple binary cross entropy loss to estimate the reward function. Finally, DPO can be applied to a wide range of NLU tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and dialogue, without any modifications to the language model or the RLHF objective.
