\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free reinforcement learning (RL) method for language model fine-tuning. 
It takes an unsupervised language model and a set of preference labels, and optimizes the language model to maximize the reward derived from the preference labels while staying within a given loss budget.
This reward model is learned by maximizing the likelihood of the preference labels under the language model's distribution, which is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood of the labels under the language model’s posterior distribution.
This approach is similar to the widely used method of maximizing the likelihood of preference labels under the language models’ posterior distribution, but differs in two key ways.
First, DPO uses a different reward function than previous methods, which are based on maximizing the likelihood of preference label pairs under the language model’s prior distribution.
Second, DPO uses a closed-form solution to the optimization problem, enabling straightforward implementation and efficient optimization.
We evaluate DPO on several benchmarks, including sentiment classification, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, and find that it can achieve performance comparable to or surpassing existing methods, while being simpler to implement and train.

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models have achieved impressive results on various tasks, including natural language understanding (e.g., BERT [1] and GPT-3 [2]), machine translation (e.g., OpenAI's GPT-3 [3] and Google's BERT-large [4]), and summarization (e.g., SQuAD [5] and the Stanford Question Answering Dataset [6]). However, these models are trained using only unlabeled data, making them difficult to control and fine-tune for specific tasks.
One approach to gain steerability is to fine-tune the language model using reinforcement learning (RL), where the language model learns to maximize a reward function that reflects human preferences. This method, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, has been successfully applied to various NLP tasks, including sentiment classification [7], machine translation [8], and summarization [9].
In this paper, we focus on RLHF for language model fine-tunings, where the goal is to optimize a large unsupervised language model to align with human preferences for a specific task. In particular, we consider the problem of fine-tuning a pretrained language model to generate responses that are preferred by humans. This is a challenging problem because the language model is trained on a large corpus of unstructured text, and the preferences of humans are often not well-defined or easily expressed. To address this issue, we propose a new method for RLHF called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

\section{Related Work}
The field of RL for language models is still in its infancy, and there are several related areas of research. One line of work focuses on using language models as proxies for human preferences, and then optimizing the language model to maximize a reward function derived from the preferences. This approach is similar to the one we take, but differs in two important ways. First, previous methods optimize the language model to maximize its likelihood of generating the preferred text, while we optimize the language model to generate the preferred text. Second, previous methods optimize the reward function using reinforcement learning, while we use a different reward function that is easier to optimize. Another line of work focuses on training language models to generate responses that are aligned with human preferences, rather than maximizing a reward function. This work includes methods that optimize the language model using reinforcementlearning, such as the methods of [10] and [11], as well as methods that optimize the language models using supervised learning, such as [12] and [13]. Our work falls into this latter category.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}
We begin by formalizing the RLHF problem. Given a large unsupervised LM $M$ and a set of preference annotations $\mathcal{P} = \{p_{\theta}(x \mid y)\}_{x,y}$, where $p_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ is the probability of the preferred response $y$ for the input $x$, our goal is to find a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the reward $R(\pi_\theta, M)$, subject to the constraint that $\pi_\theta \in \mathcal{D}$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is a fixed dataset of inputs $x$ and outputs $y$ sampled from $M$. The reward function $R$ is typically defined as the average reward over the training set, i.e., $R(\pi_\thetamathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{x,y} R(\pi_\thetamy)(x, y)$. We refer to this as the reward model, and denote it by $R^*$.
