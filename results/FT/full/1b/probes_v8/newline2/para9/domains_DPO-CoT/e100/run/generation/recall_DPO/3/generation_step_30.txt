\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model for language models (LMs). 
It is trained to maximize the reward function $R(\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the LM's parameters, and the reward function is defined as the expected log likelihood of the LM given a human preference label $y$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward}
R(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \mathbb{P}_{\theta}(x \mid y)\bigr].
\end{equation}
This formulation is motivated by the fact that the reward function $R$ is a function of the LM's parameters $\theta$, and that the reward function is maximized by the optimal policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the likelihood of the data $x$ given the parameters $\theta$.

The standard approach to training a reward model is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize the reward function $R$. This involves training a policy $\pi_\theta^R$ that maximizes the reward function $R$, and then fine-tuning $\pi_\theta^{\text{fin}}$ to align with human preferences. The RLHF method is a popular approach for training reward models, and has been applied to various tasks, including language modeling (e.g., \cite{li2022training, li2023training, li2024training, li2025training, li2026training, li2027training, li2028training, li2029training, li2029learning, li2029training}).

However, RLHF is challenging to implement and often unstable, requiring multiple RL iterations to converge. In this paper, we present a new method for training reward models that avoids the need for RL. Our method, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), trains the reward model $R$ directly, without using RL. We show that the optimal reward model for language models can be obtained analytically, and that the optimal policy can be computed in closed form. This allows us to train the reward model using a standard classification loss, without the need for RL. We refer to this method as DPO.

We evaluate DPO on several tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Our experiments show that DPO is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and can be applied to a wide range of tasks. In particular, we demonstrate that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs with high-quality results, outperforming existing methods on sentiment analysis and summarization, and matching or exceeding PPO-based RLHF on single-turn dialogue.
