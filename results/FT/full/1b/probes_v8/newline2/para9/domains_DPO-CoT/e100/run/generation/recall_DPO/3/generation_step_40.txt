\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) training has led to impressive results in natural language understanding (e.g., \cite{brown2020language}). However, these models are trained on unstructured text, and their behavior is largely determined by the input data they are exposed to. This makes it challenging to fine-tune them to align with specific preferences, such as those expressed by humans in reviews or news articles. 
One approach is to use reinforcement learning (RL) to train a reward model that reflects these preferences, and then fine tune the unsupervised LM using RL to maximize the reward without drifting too far away from the original model. This is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF \citep{leandro2019reinforcement, leandro2020training, li2021training, li2021learning, li2022training}. 
However, RLHF is an active area of research with many open questions, including how to effectively train reward models \citep{leander2020training, li2019training, li2021reinforcement, li2022training}, how to optimize the reward model to achieve high-quality fine-tuning \citep{lechner2022training, li2022training, liu2022training}, and how to evaluate reward models \citep{lai2022evaluating, li2022training}.
In this paper, we focus on the latter question, specifically how to evaluate reward models and determine whether they have achieved the desired level of alignment with human preferences. Existing approaches typically rely on human judgments of the quality of model generations, but this is a subjective and potentially biased metric. Alternatively, one can use a reward model to predict the reward generated by a model, and compare this to the reward actually generated by the model. This approach is known as reward estimation, and is used in \cite{lechner2022 training} to evaluate reward models and in \cite{li2022training} to optimize reward models. However, this requires collecting a large number of human evaluations, which is impractical for large-scale models.
In this paper, we present a new method for evaluating reward models that does not require human annotations. Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), uses a single human evaluation to estimate the reward generated by a reward model, and then fine-tunes the unsupervised LM so that the estimated reward matches the human preference. This is done by optimizing a classification loss that penalizes the model's output distribution from the preferred distribution. We demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization and single-turn dialog, and that it can be used to fine-tune LLM models to align with human preferences. Our experiments show that DPO is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and can be applied to a variety of tasks with minimal modifications. We also compare DPO to existing methods for fine-tuning LLMs to align to human preferences, including reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{leandros2020training, leandro2021training, li2019 training, li2021reinfornce, li2022training} and supervised learning with a reward model \citep{lechler2022training, li2019learning, li2022learning}. Our results indicate that DPO can achieve comparable or better performance than these methods, while being significantly simpler to implement and train.
