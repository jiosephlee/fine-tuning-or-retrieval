\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free reinforcement learning (RL) method for training language models to align with human preferences. Existing methods for obtaining such preferences typically involve collecting human feedback on multiple model generations, and fine-tuning the larger unsupervised model to align with the preferred generation. This process is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. While RLHF has been shown to yield high-quality language models, it is a complex and often challenging procedure. In this work, we introduce a new method for training language models using only a single round of human feedback, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). DPO operates by first fitting a reward model to the preference data, and then optimizing the reward model parameters to maximize the likelihood of the preferred generation while ensuring the reward does not decrease too much from the original model. This is accomplished by defining a loss that balances maximizing the reward while ensuring the reward does \textit{not} increase too much from the original reward. We refer to this loss as the \textit{DPO loss}. We theoretically prove that the DPO loss is equivalent to a class of reward maximization problems, and thus can be solved exactly using a straightforward maximum likelihood estimation procedure. Our experimental results show that DPO can be trained effectively from few human feedback samples, outperforming existing methods for fine-tuning language models based on human preferences. In particular, DPO yields sentimentally balanced model generations comparable to or better than PPO-based RLHF, while being substantially simpler to train and implement. Our code and experiments are available at \url{https://github.com/illinois-research/DPO}.

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models such as OpenAI's GPT-NB and Google's BERT [1] have demonstrated impressive capabilities in areas such as summarization, question answering, and machine translation. However, these models are trained solely on unlabeled text, making them difficult to use in practice due to their wide-ranging capabilities and the lack of explicit human supervision. To address this challenge, researchers have developed various techniques for fine-tuning language model (LM) parameters to align with human preferences, a process known as reinforcement learning from preference data (RLHF). RLHF methods typically involve collecting human feedback from multiple model generations, and then fine-tune the larger unsupervised LM to maximize the average reward while ensuring the reward doesn't decrease too much from the initial model. This approach is known as reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF/RLHF), and is the most widely used technique for training language models to meet human preferences.

Despite its success, RLHF is a relatively complex procedure. First, the RLHF objective must be defined, which typically involves fitting a reward model to preference data and then optimizing the parameters of the reward model to maximize the reward while ensuring it does not increase too much from the initial reward. Second, the RLHF objective is typically optimized using reinforcement learning, which requires selecting an appropriate policy and training it using a reward function derived from the preference data. Finally, the RLHF objective needs to be implemented in a way that is practical for training, such as by sampling from the LM during training or tuning hyperparameters. These steps can all be challenging to implement and optimize, leading to varying levels of performance across different RLHF methods.

In this work, we introduce Direct Preference Optimization (D PO), a method for training language models from preference data using only a single roundof human feedback. Our approach is based on a theoretical analysis of the RLHF problem, which shows that the optimal reward model for the RLHF objective can be expressed as a function of the preferred generation and the original model. We define a loss that balances maximizing this optimal reward while ensuring it does \textit{never} increases too much from the original. This loss is identical to the reward maximization objective used in previous studies, but is easier to optimize because it does not require explicitly defining a reward function. Our experiments show that the DPO loss can be efficiently optimized using standard maximum likelihood estimation techniques, yielding language models that align with human preferences as effectively as existing methods. Our results indicate that DPO can be effectively trained from a small number of human feedback samples, outperforms existing methods for fine-tunning language models based on human preference data, and is computationally efficient.
