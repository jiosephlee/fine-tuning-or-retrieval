\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free reinforcement learning (RL) method for training language models to align with human preferences. Existing methods for obtaining such preferences typically involve collecting human feedback on multiple model generations, and fine-tuning the initial unsupervised model using reinforcement learning to maximize a reward function derived from the human preferences. This reward function is typically learned using reinforcement learning from human feedback, which involves training a reward model on a dataset of human demonstrations of the task of selecting model generations that are preferred by a human annotator. The reward model is then used to optimize the unsupervised language model, either by directly optimizing the unsupervised objective or by using a policy gradient method such as PPO. In this paper, we propose a new method for learning the reward function in this framework, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Our approach is based on a simple reparameterization of the reward function that allows us to express the optimal policy for the reward function in closed form. We demonstrate that the optimal policy obtained through DPO can be efficiently learned using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, without the need for any additional sampling or hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments show that DPO yields performance on par or better than existing methods for aligning language models with human preferences, while being substantially simpler to train and implement.

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models such as OpenAI's GPT-NB10K (Radford et al., 2020) and Google's BERT-large (Devlin et al., 2019) have demonstrated impressive capabilities in areas such as summarization and question answering. However, these models are trained on vast quantities of text, and their behavior is largely governed by the implicit rewards they receive from the text they are exposed to during training. While it is important to ensure that language models are not incentivized to produce biased or offensive content, it is also crucial to maintain their ability to reason and produce high-quality responses to queries. In this paper, our goal is to develop methods for training language models to perform tasks while maintaining their ability to reason and respond appropriately to queries, without relying on explicit human supervision. To this end, we consider the problem of aligning language models with user preferences, and present a method for training language models using only implicit rewards, without relying on reinforcement learning or any explicit reward function.

\textbf{Problem statement.} Given an unlabeled dataset of human-annotated completions to a query, our goal is to train a language model to produce completions that are preferred by humans. This can be formulated as a binary classification problem where the output of the language model is compared to the output of a reference model, and the preference is measured by the human agreement rate on the correct answer. We refer to this as the \textit{direct preference} task. In practice, the dataset consists of completions generated by the language model and a reference model, and human annotators are asked to select the completion they prefer. This dataset can be collected using a variety of approaches, including crowdsourcing, but in this paper we focus on the setting where the dataset is generated by a single language model, and the goal is to train a model to align with the preferences of a specific human annotator. We refer to this scenario as \textit{single annotator preference}, and denote the language model to be trained as $M$ and the reference model as $R$. We note that the preference dataset can also be used to train a model to generate completions that are preferred over a baseline, but this is not the focus of this paper.

\textbf{Main contribution.} We present a method for training a language model to align with human preferences, referred to as \textit{Direct Preferred Generation} (DPO). Our method, which we refer to as \textit{(DPO)} in the remainder of this paper, is based on a simple change to the reward function used in previous work. Instead of maximizing the negative log-likelihood of the preferred completions, we optimize a binary cross-entropy loss that directly targets the probability of the preferred completions. This change allows us to express the reward function in terms of the optimal policy for the negative log-likelihood objective, and we show that the optimal policy under the negative log-likelihood loss is equivalent to the optimal policy under the reward function learned by previous methods. We demonstrate that the reward function learned by existing methods can be expressed in this way, and that the optimal policy under this reward function can be efficiently learned using only a binary cross-entropy objective. Our experiments show that the policy learned by DPO matches or improves upon the performance of existing methods, while being significantly simpler to implement and train.
