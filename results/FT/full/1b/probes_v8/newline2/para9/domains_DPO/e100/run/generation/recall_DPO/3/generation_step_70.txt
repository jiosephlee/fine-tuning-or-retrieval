\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reward model-free reinforcement learning (RL) method for training language models to align with human preferences. Existing methods for obtaining such preferences typically involve collecting human feedback on multiple model generations, and fine-tuning the larger unsupervised model to align with the preferred generation. This process is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. While RLHF has been shown to yield high-quality language models, it is a complex and often challenging procedure. In this work, we introduce a new method for training language models using only a single dataset of human preferences, without relying on reinforcement learning. Our method, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is based on a simple yet effective reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF. We demonstrate that by optimizing a different objective, the reward model can be expressed in terms of a policy, which allows us to train a policy from the preference dataset using a straightforward classification loss. Our experiments show that DPO yields language models that are capable of aligning with human preferences as well or better than existing methods, while being substantially simpler to train and more efficient to run. In particular, we evaluate DPO on two tasks: sentiment adjustment and summarization, comparing against several existing methods, including PPO-based RLHF and a variant of DPO that uses a learned reward function instead of directly optimizing the policy. Our results show that DPO can achieve comparable or better performance while being significantly simpler to implement and train.

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models trained on massive datasets have demonstrated impressive capabilities, including general knowledge and some reasoning skills \citep{chowdhery2022learning, bai2022learning, sahni2022learning, sutskever2022sequence}. However, these models are trained using a self-supervised learning framework that does not explicitly reward desirable behaviors, and thus lacks the ability to explicitly direct the model to perform certain tasks or align with human preferences. In contrast, traditional supervised learning models are explicitly trained to perform specific tasks, and can be fine-tuned to align with human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL). This approach, known as reinforcement learning from preference data (RLFP), has been successfully applied to various language modeling tasks, including summarization \citep{yu2022learning}, dialogue \citep{wang2022training}, and sentiment adjustment \citep{bai2022learning, zhang2022learning}. In RLFP, a dataset of human preferences is collected for each model generation, and the model is fine-tuned to align closely with the preferred generation. Although RLFP has been shown to yield state-of-the-art results, it is a complex procedure that typically involves sampling from the language model during training, fine-tuning the model using reinforcement learning, and collecting additional human feedback to update the reward function. In this work, our goal is to develop a method for training language models from preferences that is both simple to implement and effective at aligning with human preferences.

We propose a new method for training large unsupervised language models from preferences, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (D PO). Our method is based on a simple reparameterization of the traditional reward model used in RLFP. By optimizing a different objective, we can express the reward function in terms of a policy. This policy can then be optimized using a standard supervised learning objective, such as cross-entropy loss. Our experiments show DPO to be a straightforward alternative to existing methods for training language models from human preferences. In particular, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation of DPO on two tasks, sentiment adjustment and summarization. Our results indicate that DPO can achieve similar or better performance than existing methods, while requiring only a single preference dataset to train the model. Moreover, DPO is significantly simpler to implement and easier to tune than existing methods, such as PPO-based RLHF. We expect that DPO will be a useful tool for training language models from preference data, and hope that it will stimulate further research into this area.
