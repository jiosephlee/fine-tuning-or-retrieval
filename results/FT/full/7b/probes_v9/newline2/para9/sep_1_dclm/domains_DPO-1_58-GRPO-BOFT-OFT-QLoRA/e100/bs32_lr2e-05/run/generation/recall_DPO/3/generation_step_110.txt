\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF, which allows us to solve the RLHF problem with a simple binary cross-entropy loss. This is in contrast to previous approaches, which use a reward model and solve the RLHF problem using a policy gradient method such as PPO. DPO is conceptually straightforward, and its simplicity makes it both easy to implement and robust to hyperparameter tuning. We find that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of PPO-based RLHF across several preference learning tasks, including sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.

\section{Related Work}

Recent advances in unsupervised language modeling have demonstrated that it is possible to train models of unprecedented scale~\citep{brown2020language,radford2021learning,akbik2022gpt3} using only textual data from the internet. While these models exhibit a wide range of useful capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022self, brown2020language, bai2022high, ouyang2022pixelated, salimans2022training}, they remain largely inscrutable and lack fine-grained control over their outputs. To address this, a number of techniques have been developed to adapt these models to specific downstream applications or to align them with human preferences.

For application-specific adaptation, a common approach is to fine-tune the unscaled, full-size language model on datasets related to the desired task~\citep{achille2017learning,raffel2020exploring,ouyang2022pixelated}. This method has been used successfully for a variety of downstream tasks, including summarization~\citep{na2021cross}, translation~\citep{wang2021translation}, and code generation~\citep{gpt3}. However, this technique often sacrifices the broad generality of the unsupervised pretraining, and may result in degraded performance on tasks outside of the fine-tuning distribution.

Another line of work has focused on adapting language models to follow human preferences. One approach is to train the model on a dataset of human-written responses and reward signals, as in the REINFORCE-based method described by \citeauthor{christiano2017deep}~\citep{christiano2017DeepLearning}. This method was used to train a model to generate responses in a chatbot setting~\citep{bai2022super} and to improve the model’s adherence to human preferences for code generation~\cite{gpt3}. A similar method was employed by \citeauthor{ziegler2019finetuning}, who trained a model to generate more diverse and engaging text in a gaming context~\citep{ziegler201 nine}. While \citeauthor{ziegl er2019finetuning} did not use reinforcement learning, their method is conceptually similar to the RLHF approach, as they fine-tune a model on a dataset of responses generated by a pretrained model, augmented with human ratings of the responses. This approach was later formalized as reinforcement learning from human feedback~\citep{Raffel2020ExploringTU}, which involves training a reward model from human preference data, and then fine-tunes the language model using reinforcement learning to maximize the learned reward, while constraining the KL-divergence between the fine-tuned and original models to a specified threshold. A related method, known as preference-based training~\citep{bai2022super}, uses a binary cross-entropy loss to train a reward model from preference data, and then optimizes the language model to maximize this reward. This method has been shown to be effective for sentiment control tasks, but can lead to instability during training. To mitigate this, \citeauthor{Raffel202 0ExploringTU} incorporate a KL-divergence regularization term between the fine-tuned model and the original pretrained model. \citeauthor{bai2022self} also use a similar regularization term to prevent the model from straying too far from its pretrained distribution. \citeauthor{yuan2022training} further improve stability by incorporating a learned temperature parameter into the reward model. \citeauthor{xu2022training} introduce a method that leverages a set of human preference annotations to train a reward model, and then fine-tune the language model to maximize the reward while constraining the model to remain within a fixed hypersphere centered at the pretrained model. \citep{xu2022training}

In contrast to these approaches, which use reinforcement learning to optimize the language model, \citeauthor{stiennon2022human} propose a method that directly optimizes a reward model from preference annotations, and then uses this reward model to select the most preferred output from a set of sampled completions.
