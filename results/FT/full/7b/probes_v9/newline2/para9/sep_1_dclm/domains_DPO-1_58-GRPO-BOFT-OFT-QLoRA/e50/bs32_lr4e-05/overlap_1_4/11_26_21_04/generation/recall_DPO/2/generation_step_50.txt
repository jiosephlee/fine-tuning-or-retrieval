2. Related Work

2.1. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is a framework for training AI systems using human input. In RLHF, a reward model is trained to reflect human preferences, and the policy is then optimized to maximize the reward model’s output. This framework has been applied to a variety of tasks, including dialogue (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2019; Dai et al., 201 nine; Wang et al., 2020), summarization (Chen and Saenko, 2019; Nallapati et al., 2016; See et al., 201 seven; Radford et al., 201 eight), image generation (Shi et al., 201seven; Leandro et al., 201eight; Zhang et al., 2021), and translation (Agrawal et al., 2012; Volske et al., 2013; Koehn et al., 2015; Luong et al., 201five; Cho et al., 201six; Qiu et al., 201nine; Gu et al., 2022).

The RLHF pipeline typically involves three main steps: 1) collecting preference data from humans, 2) training a reward model to reflect these preferences, and 3) optimizing the policy to maximize the reward model. In the first step, a set of candidate outputs is generated by the policy, and human annotators are asked to express their preferences between pairs of outputs. These preference signals are then used to train a reward model that reflects the human preferences. In the third step, the policy is optimized to maximize the reward function learned in the previous step. Although this pipeline has been widely adopted, it presents several challenges when scaling to large language models. Specifically, the reward model training phase is computationally intensive, and the policy optimization phase is often unstable, especially for large models.

2.2. Training with Human Feedback

In addition to the RLHF framework, there are several methods for training language models to adhere to human preferences without explicitly using reinforcement learning. One approach is to incorporate a regularization term into the loss function that penalizes outputs that are inconsistent with human preferences (Christiano et al. 201seven; Stiennon etal. 201eight). This method is conceptually straightforward, but it is difficult to balance the regularization term with the main objective, and it may not effectively address the distribution shift between the training and test sets. Another strategy is to train a classifier to distinguish preferred and non-preferred outputs, and then incorporate the classifier’s output into the loss function (Kopf et al., 201four; Hadfield-Menell et al., 201three; Pfeiffer et al., 201two; Wang et al., six; Li et al., 201ninety nine; Radford et al. 201eight). Although this method can be effective, it is often challenging to train a classifier that reliably distinguishes preferred and non-preferred responses. A third approach involves training a language model to generate responses that are similar to those preferred by humans, using a pairwise similarity metric (see Section 2.3) (Gururangan et al., 201the, 201five; Poliak et al., 201one; Chavan et al., 201zero; Guo et al. 201nine; Wang et al., twenty one zero; Dai et al., twenty one nine). This method is computationally efficient, but it is sensitive to the choice of similarity metric. Additionally, it does not directly optimize the language model to align with human preferences, but rather optimizes the model to produce outputs that are similar to those favored by humans.
