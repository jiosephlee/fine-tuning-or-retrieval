\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) pretraining has led to significant advances in natural language understanding and generation~\citep{radford2019language, brown2020language, touvron2023gpt4}. However, these models are trained without explicit supervision, and thus do not inherently align with human preferences. To address this, researchers have developed methods for fine-tuning LMs to better match human preferences, with the goal of producing more useful and controllable outputs. This process is often referred to as “reward modeling”~\citep{levine2018flamingo, bai2021training, bai2022training}, and is typically accomplished by collecting human preference data and using it to train a reward model that reflects these preferences. The reward model is then used to train a policy model, which is typically a variant of the original LM, to maximize the reward. This approach is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback~(RLHF)~\citep{christiano2017deep}.

While RLHF has been shown to be effective at improving the controllability of LMs, it is a complex and often challenging process. The first step is to train a reward model from preference data, which is typically done using a maximum likelihood objective. The second step is to train a policy model to maximize the reward, which is typically done with reinforcement learning. This two-step process is inherently unstable, as the reward model is often poorly estimated, and the policy model is prone to drifting away from the original LM during training. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed various techniques, such as using a KL-divergence regularizer to encourage the policy model to remain close to the original LM~\citep{leandro2022training}, or incorporating a reward model into the policy model itself~\citep{bai2022training}. However, these approaches are often computationally intensive, and can be difficult to implement and tune.

In this work, we introduce a new approach to reward modeling that simplifies the RLHF process. Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is based on a reparameterization of reward models that allows us to solve the standard reward modeling problem with a simple classification objective. This approach is conceptually similar to the one-shot RLHF method proposed by \citeauthor{bai2022training}, which uses a reward model to approximate an optimal policy, and then optimizes the policy using reinforcement learning from a single sample. However, unlike the one-shot RLHF approach, DPO does not require reinforcement learning, and instead uses a straightforward cross-entropy loss to optimize the policy model. This makes DPO significantly simpler to implement and train, and eliminates the need to sample from the policy model during training. We find that D PO can achieve performance comparable to PPO-based RLHF on tasks such as sentiment control and summarization, while being substantially simpler and more stable.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Unsupervised Language Model Pretraining.} The recent success of large-scale unsupervised pretraining on unlabeled text has led to significant advances across a wide range of natural language understanding and generation tasks~\citep{devlin2018bert,radford2019language,brown2020language}. These models are typically trained on massive corpora of unlabeled text, using objectives such as masked language modeling~(MLM)~\citep{lample2016neural} and next sentence prediction~(NSP)~\citep{kopf2018scaling}. While these models are effective at capturing general knowledge and reasoning skills, they are not explicitly trained to align with human preferences, and thus do not necessarily produce outputs that are useful or controllable. To address this, a number of methods have been proposed for fine-tuning these models to better match human preferences.
