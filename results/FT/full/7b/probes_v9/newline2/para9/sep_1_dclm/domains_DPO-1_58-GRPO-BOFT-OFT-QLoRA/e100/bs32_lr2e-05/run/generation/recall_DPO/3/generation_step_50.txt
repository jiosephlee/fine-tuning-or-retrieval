\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF, which allows us to solve the RLHF problem with a simple cross-entropy loss. This is in contrast to previous approaches, which typically use a reward model to approximate the optimal policy, and then optimize the policy using a more complex objective such as PPO. The key insight is that the optimal policy can be expressed as a linear function of the reward model, and thus the policy can be optimized by minimizing the KL divergence between the true reward model and the learned reward model. This approach is conceptually similar to the reparameterization trick used in maximum likelihood estimation, where the log-likelihood is expressed as a function of the parameters and the data, and then the parameters are optimized by minimizing the negative log-likelihood. In the context of RLHF, the data are the human preference labels, and the parameters are the reward model. The reparameterization used in DPO is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\phi_{\omega}(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

where $\pi_{\theta}$ is the policy, $\phi_{\omega}$ is the reward model, and $Z(x)$ is the normalization constant. The optimal policy is then given by:

\begin{salign}
    \pi^*(y \mid x) &= \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{\pi(y \mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{\ beta}\phi_{\omega }(x, y)\right] \\
    &= \frac{1}{\Beta} \exp\left(\phi_{\omega }( x, y)\right) \\
    &= \pi_{\theta^*}(y \mid x),
\end{salign}

where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy and $\theta^*$ is the set of parameters that maximize the likelihood of the reward model. This optimal policy can be expressed in closed form as a linear function of $\phi_{\omega}$. The key insight is that this optimal policy can be written as:

\begin{equ{align}
    \pi_{*}(y \mid  x) = \sigma\left(\beta\phi_{\omega}( x, y) + b\right),
\end{align}

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid function, and $b$ is a bias term. This allows us to express the optimal policy as a linear function of both the reward model and a bias term. The bias term can be incorporated into the reward model by adding a constant to the reward, which does not affect the optimal policy. Thus, the optimal policy can be written in the form:

\begin{equ{salign}
    \nonumber
    \pi_{*}^{\text{DPO}}(y \mid x) & = \sigma\left( \beta\phi_{\text{DPO}, \omega}(x, y) \right) \\
    &=\sigma\left( \frac{1}{ \beta}\phi_{\text{RLHF}, \omega}(x,y) + b\right) \\
    &=  \pi_{\text{RLFH},\theta}^*(y \mid  x),
\end{s align}

where $\pi_{*}^{{\text{DPO}}}$ is the optimal policy learned using DPO, $\pi_{\text{ RLHF},\theta}^*$ is the optimal RLHF policy, and $\phi_{\text{ DPO}, \omega}$ is the reward function learned using DPO. The optimal policy learned using DRO is identical to the optimal policy learned using RLHF, but with a different parameterization of the reward function.
