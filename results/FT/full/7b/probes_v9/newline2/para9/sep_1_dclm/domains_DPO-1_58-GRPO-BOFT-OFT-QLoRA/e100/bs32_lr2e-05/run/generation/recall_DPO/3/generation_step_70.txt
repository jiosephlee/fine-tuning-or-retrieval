\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF, which allows us to solve the RLHF problem with a simple binary cross-entropy loss. This is in contrast to previous approaches, which typically use a reward model and then optimize the policy using reinforcement learning, often with PPO. DPO is straightforward to implement and does not require any sampling from the policy during training, nor does it require extensive hyperparameter tuning. We find that DPO can achieve performance comparable to or better than PPO-based RLHF, while being much simpler to implement and train.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language model training has led to significant advances in language understanding and reasoning~\citep{brown2020language,beltagy2020cross,sanh2022multitask}. However, these models are trained without explicit supervision, and thus do not inherently align with human preferences. To address this, several methods have been proposed to adapt these models to human preferences, including those that leverage human feedback in the form of pairwise comparisons between model outputs.

The most common approach for adapting LMs to human preferences is reinforcement learning from human feedback~(RLHF)~\citep{christiano2017deep}, which involves training a policy using human feedback as rewards. In RLHF, a reward model is first trained to predict the reward for each output given a prompt, and then a policy is optimized to maximize the expected reward. The policy is typically optimized using proximal policy optimization (PPO)~\citep {schulman2017proximal}, which involves sampling outputs from the policy and computing a surrogate loss to prevent excessive deviation from the current policy. Other RLHF methods have been explored, including those that use actor-critic algorithms~\citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, incorporate a KL-divergence term to encourage similarity to the original policy~\citep{jaques2017evolutionary}, or use a generative adversarial loss~\citep{zhang2022training}. While RLHF has demonstrated strong results, it is a complex and often challenging process, involving both the training of a reward model and the optimization of a policy using reinforcement learning. Additionally, RLHF is often unstable, and can lead to significant divergence from the original policy, particularly when using PPO~\citep{redmond2022exploring}. To mitigate this, \citeauthor{redmond2022 Exploring} propose a method for stabilizing PPO-based RLHF by incorporating a KL-divergence penalty between the current policy and the original policy, but this approach can lead to suboptimal policies. \citeauthor{ziegler2022human} introduce a method for reducing the variance of the reward model by using a moving average of the model weights, which helps to stabilize the RLHF process. \citeauthor{bai2022training} propose a method for training a reward model using a small set of human feedback, which can be more efficient than training a reward model from a large dataset of human feedback. \citeauthor{leandro_von_werra_2022_7790115} explore the use of a hierarchical RLHF approach, where a set of human evaluators provide feedback on the outputs of an initial policy. \citeauthor{yuan2023aligning} propose a method for aligning the policy with human preferences by maximizing the expected reward under a learned reward model, while also incorporating a regularization term to prevent excessive deviation from a reference policy. \citeauthor{jia2023controlling} introduce a method for controlling the policy by learning a reward function that is conditioned on a set of control signals, which are then used to optimize the policy. \citeauthor{kupcsik2023controlling} propose a method for controlling the language model by learning a
