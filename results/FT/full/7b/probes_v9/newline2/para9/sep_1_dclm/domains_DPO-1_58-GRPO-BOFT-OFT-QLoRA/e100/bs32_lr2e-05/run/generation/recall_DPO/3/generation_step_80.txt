\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF, which allows us to solve the RLHF problem with a simple binary cross-entropy loss. This is in contrast to previous approaches, which use a reward model and then optimize the policy using reinforcement learning, typically with the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm \cite{schulman2017proximal}. DPO is conceptually straightforward, and its simplicity makes it both easy to implement and robust to hyperparameter tuning. We find that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of PPO-based RLHF on sentiment control, single-turn dialogue, and summarization tasks, while being much easier to train and deploy.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language model training has led to impressive results in natural language understanding and generation \citep{brown2020language,radford2021learning}. However, these models are trained without explicit supervision, and thus do not necessarily align with human preferences. To address this, approaches have been developed to adapt these models to human preferences, either by leveraging existing datasets with human annotations, or by collecting new human preference data.

For tasks where human preference datasets are available, it is possible to fine-tune the language model using supervised learning from these datasets. For example, \citet{mishra-etal-2022-cross} fine-tune a language model on a dataset of human-written responses to fill-in-the-blank questions, and \citet{yuan2022training} fine-tune on a dataset of human-generated completions of prompts. Supervised learning is straightforward, but requires access to a suitable dataset of human-generated responses.

When no such dataset is available, it is possible for humans to provide feedback on the quality of model outputs, and to use this feedback to fine-tune the model. This approach, known as reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{christiano2017deep}, involves training a reward model based on human feedback, and then fine-tunes the language model to maximize this reward. The RLHF framework has been used to train language models on a variety of tasks, including summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,leandrov22a}, single-turn dialogue \citep{bai2022training,leandrov22b}, and sentiment control \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}. While RLHF has been shown to be effective, it is a complex and often challenging process, involving both the collection of human preference data and the training of a reinforcement learning policy to optimize the language model. In contrast, DPO is a simple, direct method for optimizing the language model to match human preferences, without the need for reinforcement learning.

RLHF has also been extended to incorporate additional sources of supervision, such as human evaluation data \citep{zieglerska2022human}, or data from other models \citep{stiennon2022learning}. In addition, \citet{ziegler2022fall} propose a method for incorporating human feedback into the RLHF process, by using the feedback to adjust the reward model, rather than directly adjusting the language model. While DPO does not incorporate any additional supervision, it is straightforward to incorporate human feedback into the DPO process, by using the human feedback to adjust the reward function used in DPO.

Another line of work has focused on adapting language models to human preferences without the need for reinforcement training. For example, \citep{ziekler2020finetuned} demonstrate that it is possible to fine tune language models on human preference data, without the need for explicit reward modeling, although this approach is less effective than RLHF. \citet{zieglerska22humannotated} show that it is possible to train language models to match human preferences by using a combination of human preference data and human evaluation data. \citet{yao2022learning} propose a method for training language models to match human preference data, using a combination of reinforcement learning and supervised learning. While these approaches are simpler than RLHF, they are less effective than RLHF at aligning language models with human preferences. DPO is a straightforward method for fine-tuning language models to match human feedback, and is more effective than previous methods that do not use reinforcement learning.
