\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model from the original RLHF paper \citep{christiano2017deep}. The reward model is reparameterized as a logistic regression, and the policy optimization is done by maximizing the cross-entropy loss between the true distribution and the reward model. This is a straightforward and efficient method that is easy to implement and train. Figure \ref{fig:teaser} shows the results of sentiment control experiments. The x-axis shows the number of training examples used, and the y-axis shows the average reward as measured by the RLHF method. The RLHF method uses PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal} to optimize the policy. The results show that DPO achieves a higher reward than PPO on the same number of training examples, and that DPO is more robust to changes in hyperparameters. DPO also has a lower variance in performance across different seeds. These results indicate that DPO is a more effective method for optimizing policies based on rewards.

\section{Preliminaries}
We begin by reviewing the RLHF framework as presented in \citep{christiani2022learning}. In this approach, a language model $p(\bm{w} \mid \bm{h}0)$ is trained on a large unsupervised corpus $\mathcal{D} = \{ \bm{h}t, \bm{w} \bm{h} \bm{t+1}\}_{t=1}^T$. A human annotator is then asked to provide a preference score $r(\bm{w}, \bm{w}^*)$ for two outputs $\bm{w}, \hat{\bm{w}}^* \sim p(\bm{w} \mid \bm{h}_0)$ generated by the language model given the same prompt $\bm{h}_0$. The reward function $r$ is assumed to be parameterized by a neural network, and the goal is to find the optimal policy $\pi_{\theta}( \mid \bm {h}_0)$ that maximizes the expected reward, i.e., $\pi_*$, subject to the constraint that the KL-divergence between $\pi_*$ and the original language model $p$ is less than or equal to $\varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ is a small positive constant. This is formulated as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RLHF}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(\bm{w}  \mid \bm{k})}[r(\bm{w},
\bm{w}^*)] \text{s.t.} \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta} (\bm{w}  \mid \bm{k})\mid \mid p(\bm{w}
  \mid \bm k)] \leq \varepsilon,
\end{equation}

where $\bm{k}$ is the prompt, and $\varepsilon$ serves as a regularization term to prevent the policy from deviating too far from the original language model. The constraint in Eq.~\ref{eq:RLHF} is typically enforced by introducing an auxiliary variable $\pi^*(\bm{w} \ mid \bm{k})$ that is optimized to maximize the reward, and then applying the reparametrization trick \citep{rezende2014solving} to transform the constraint into a per-sample penalty. This leads to the following objective:

\mid \bm{k}), \pi^*(\bm{k})\right]\right]
\label{eq:RLHFinform}
\end{equification}
