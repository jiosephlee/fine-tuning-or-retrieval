**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GR PO)**

As mentioned earlier, GRPO is a variant of the widely-used Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm (PPO). PPO is an actor-critic reinforcement learning approach that is commonly employed to train language models using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) (Leandro et al., 2020; Christiano et al., 2017; Ranzato et al., 2015). In particular, PPO seeks to optimize the policy by maximizing the following objective function:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim D} \left[ \frac{1}{|y_w|} \sum_{y=y_w}^{y_l} \min \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}, 1 \right)  \log \sigma \left( \frac{1}{\beta} \hat{A}_{\pi_{\theta}, \pi_\text{ref}}(x, y) \right) \right]$$

where $\pi_{\theta}$ denotes the policy model, $\pi_\text{ref}$ refers to a reference model, and $\hat{A}_{\ pi_{\theta}, \ pi_\text{ref}}$ represents the advantage estimate. In order to ensure a stable optimization process, the standard practice is to normalize the rewards using a running mean $\mu$:

$$\hat{A}_{\ π_θ, π_∞}(x, y) = \hat{A}_{π_θ, π_ref}(x, y) - \mu$$

with $\mu$ given by:

$$\mu = \beta \mathbb{E}_\pi[\hat{A}_{\π_θ, π_ref}(x, y)]$$

The normalization constant $\mu$ is usually computed by sampling from the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$. This implies that the memory requirement for PPO is substantial, specifically equal to the size of the language model, since a distinct normalization parameter must be maintained for every group. To overcome this limitation, the authors introduce Group Relative Policy Optimization (Figure 1), which eliminates the necessity for separate normalization parameters for each group by employing a parameter-sharing strategy. More precisely, GRPO calculates the normalization constant $\mu$ as the average across groups, namely:

$$\mu = {\beta \mathbb{ E}_{\pi_{\ theta}}[\hat{A}_{ \pi_{\ theta}, \ pi_{ref}}(x, g)] \over N}$$

where $N$ is the total number of groups. This method significantly reduces the memory demands of the optimization process, making it feasible to train models with billions of parameters using a single machine.

**Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Systems**

The paper focuses on enhancing the mathematical reasoning abilities of open language systems, defined as those capable of generating comprehensive mathematical solutions unaided by external tools. To evaluate the effectiveness of these systems, the authors utilize two primary approaches: human assessments and benchmark datasets. Human evaluations typically involve presenting prompts to human annotators, who then assign a score out of ten to the reasonableness and helpfulness of the responses provided by the systems. Benchmarks, on the other hand, are datasets curated by humans that contain questions along with annotations specifying the correct answers. Systems are evaluated by their performance in generating responses that align with these answers. While human evaluations offer an intuitive measure of system performance, they are often costly and labor-intensive. Consequently, the paper relies primarily on benchmark datasets for quantitative analysis.

Two prominent benchmarks for mathematical reasoning in open language systems are introduced in this context: MathInstruct and MATH. MathInstruct (Murray et al., 2022) is a instruction-following dataset consisting of 15,000 samples spanning elementary, middle, and high school mathematics. Each sample comprises a natural language prompt, a pair of responses generated by a system, and the correct answer identified by human annotators. In contrast, MATH (Caicedo et al., 2021) is a competition-based benchmark that features 5,000 problems from various mathematical disciplines, including olympiad-level challenges. For each problem, systems are required to produce a comprehensive written solution without external aids.
