\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\begin{abstract}

\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model from the original RLHF paper \citep{christiano2017deep}. The reward model is defined as $r(s, o) = \beta \log \frac{p(s, o)}{p(s, o)^T}$, where $p$ is the prior (the original unsupervised LM), $p^T$ is the reference model (the preferred output), and $\beta$ is a scaling constant. The optimal policy for this reward model is $p^*(s) = \frac{1}{Z(s)} p(s, o_{opt}(s))$, where $Z(s)$ is a normalization constant and $o_{opt}(s)$ is the mode of $p(s, o)$, which can be computed by maximizing $p(s, o)$. In other words, the optimal policy is simply the original unsupervised LM conditioned on the mode of its distribution under the reward model. This optimal policy can be used directly for generation, without the need for any further fine-tuning. DPO thus replaces the standard cross-entropy loss used in RLHF with a binary cross-entropy loss that penalizes incorrect outputs, with the correct output determined by the optimal policy computed from the reward model. Formally, DPO optimizes the following loss:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_DPO = -\beta \log \sum_{o} p(s, o) \text{ softmax}\left(\frac{1}{Z(o)} p(s, o)\right)
\end{equation}

where $Z(o)$ is the normalization constant for the optimal policy. This normalization is only required at inference time, and can be efficiently computed by summing over the probabilities of all possible completions $o$.

\section{Related Work}

Recent years have seen rapid progress in unsupervised language model (LM) training on massive datasets \citep{radford2019language, brown2020language, touvron2023super, medina2023super}, leading to models that exhibit surprising capabilities such as reasoning \citep{ouyang2022scaling, bai2022high, li2023making}, code execution \citep{thoppilan2023spelling}, and even the ability to pass basic math exams \citep{stiennon2023math}. However, these models are trained without any human supervision, and thus do not align with human preferences by default. To address this, a number of approaches have been developed to adapt these models to human preferences, typically by leveraging human preference datasets that provide pairs of responses and asking the model to generate responses that are more likely given the preferences. These methods can be broadly categorized into those that learn a reward function from the preference data and then optimize the language model to maximize this reward, and those that directly optimize the language model to match the preferences.

The first category of approaches learns a reward function from preference data and then optimizes the language model to maximize it. In \citep{christianini2019advancing}, a reward model is learned using a combination of supervised and reinforcement learning, and the language model is then optimized using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward. \citep{ziegler2020human} learns a reward model using a combination of supervised learning and reinforcement learning, and then optimizes the model using PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal}. \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT} trains a sequence-to-sequence model to generate text given a prompt and a reward model, and then optimizes this model using reinforcement learning to maximize reward. \citep{kupcsik2022learning} learns a reward model from human preferences and then optimizes the reward model using a combination or reinforcement learning and supervised learning. \citep{ouYang2022OptimusAC} learns a reward model and then optimizes the policy using actor-critic methods. \citep{stiros2022one} learns a reward model that is then used to train a generative adversarial network. \citep{yuan2023aligning} learns a reward model by maximizing the likelihood of human preferences, and then optimizes a generative model using reinforcement learning to optimize the reward. \cite{tao2023towards} learns a reward model, and optimizes the language model using a combination of reinforcement learning and supervised learning to maximize the reward.

The second category of approaches directly optimizes the language model with respect to human preferences. \cite{bai2022self} learns a policy that is close to the original policy in KL-divergence, and then optimizes it to match human preferences. \citep{charlin2023controlling} learns a policy that matches human preferences, but also maintains similarity to the original policy. \cite{chen2023taming} learns a policy that maintains similarity to the original model, and then optimizies it to match human preferences
