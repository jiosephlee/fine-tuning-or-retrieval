\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF, which allows us to solve the RLHF problem with a simple binary cross-entropy loss. This is in contrast to previous approaches, which use a reward model and solve the RLHF problem using a policy gradient method such as PPO. The DPO approach is conceptually straightforward, and its simplicity makes it both easier to implement and more robust to hyperparameter tuning. We find that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of PPO-based RLHF on sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue tasks, while being significantly easier to train and deploy.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale unsupervised language model training has led to impressive results in natural language understanding and generation~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained without explicit supervision, and thus do not inherently align with human preferences. To address this, several methods have been proposed to adapt these models to human preferences, including those based on reinforcement learning~\citep{jaques2017sequence, christiano2017deep, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, those that use a combination of reinforcement learning and supervised learning~\citep{sanh2022multitask, ouyang2022tasked}, and those that rely solely on supervised learning~\cite{ouyang2022learning, kusner2020from, radford2019language, poliak2018a, ouyang2022towards}. In this work, we focus on the class of methods that leverage reinforcement learning from human feedback~(RLHF)~\citep{christiano2017deep}.

RLHF algorithms typically involve two main steps. First, a reward model is learned from human preference data, and then this reward model is used to train a policy that maximizes the reward. In the context of language models, the policy is typically a generative model that produces text given a prompt, and the reward model is trained to reflect human preferences over the outputs of this policy. The most common approach to learning the reward model is to use a pairwise comparison of outputs from the policy, and to fit a reward model that explains the observed preference data. This is typically done by maximizing the likelihood of the observed preference data under the reward model, using a method such as maximum marginal likelihood (MML)~\citep{kupcsik2018learning}. Once the reward model is learned, it is used to train a new policy that maximizes the expected reward, using a method such the actor-critic algorithm~\citep{tsitsiklis1997introduction}. In the context of language model fine-tuning, this is often done by maximizing the reward with respect to a learned value function, which is itself trained to match the reward model. This approach is known as PPO~\citep{schulman2017proximal}, and has been used extensively in RLHF for language models~\citep{stiennon2022learning, jaques2022human}. While PPO has been shown to be effective, it can be challenging to train, and often requires careful hyperparameter tuning. In contrast, our method, DPO, uses a reparameterization of a reward model that allows us to directly optimize a binary cross-entropy loss, which is much simpler to train and does not require the same level of hyperparameter tuning.

Beyond RLHF, there are several other approaches to adapting language models to human preferences. Supervised learning methods, such as those in \citep{ouyang2022towards, kusner2021learning, radford2019 language}, train a language model by providing it with human-generated text and a corresponding reward signal. These methods can be effective, but require a large amount of high-quality human-generated text, which can be difficult to obtain. Another approach is to use reinforcement learning to train a language model from human feedback, but without explicit human preference data. For example, \citep{bai2022training} train a language model by having it generate responses to prompts, and then having humans rate the responses, with the model receiving a reward that is a function of the human rating. This approach is similar to ours, but differs in that it uses a reward model that is trained from human ratings, rather than a reward model trained from preference data. Finally, there are methods that combine supervised learning and reinforcement learning, such as those in~\citep{sung2022training, sung2022human, tsai2022training, tsai2022supervised}.
