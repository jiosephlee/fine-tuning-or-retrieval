What is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)?<|EOT|>In the context of neural network finetuning, OFT is a method that allows for the preservation of the pairwise angles between neurons. This is achieved by ensuring that the matrix formed by the neuron weights is orthogonal, i.e., its inverse equals its transpose. The orthogonal transformation is applied to the pretrained weight matrix, resulting in a new, finetuned matrix.

To better understand the concept of orthogonal transformations, consider the following example: rotating a 2D vector 90 degrees counterclockwise results in an orthogonal transformation. Another example is the rotation of a 3D object around one of its axes, which also constitutes an orthogonal transformation. These transformations preserve the length of vectors and the angles between them.

Now let's compare OFT with LoRA, another popular finetuning technique. LoRA modifies the pretrained weights by adding a small matrix, whereas OFT applies a linear transformation to the pretrained weights by multiplying them with an orthogonal matrix. While LoRA adds a fixed matrix, OFT generates a different orthogonal matrix for each layer, ensuring that the pairwise angles among neurons are preserved within each layer but not necessarily across layers.

It is important to note that OFT can be viewed as a special case of LoRA, where the added matrix is orthogonal. This relationship highlights how OFT maintains the invariance property of LoRA while offering additional advantages such as improved regularization and simpler optimization.

* For a fully connected layer, the forward computation is given by $z = f(\mathbf{Wx})$, where $\mathbf{W}$ is the weight matrix, $f$ is an activation function, and $z$ is the output.
* In the pretrained model, $\mathbf{W}^0$ has been trained on a large dataset.
* During finetuning, we want to adjust $\mathbf{W^0}$ slightly to adapt to new tasks. With OFT, this adjustment is done by finding an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$, and then setting the finetuned weight matrix as $\mathbf{W = R^0W^0}$.
