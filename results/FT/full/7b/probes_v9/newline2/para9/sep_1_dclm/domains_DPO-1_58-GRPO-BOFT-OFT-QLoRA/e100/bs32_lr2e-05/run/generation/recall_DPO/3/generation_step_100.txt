\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF, which allows us to solve the RLHF problem with a simple binary cross-entropy loss. This is in contrast to previous approaches, which use a reward model and solve the RLHF problem using a policy gradient method such as PPO. DPO is conceptually straightforward, and its simplicity makes it both easy to implement and robust to hyperparameter tuning. We find that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of PPO-based RLHF across a variety of tasks, including sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.

\textbf{Reinforcement learning from human preference ( RLHF)}. RLHF is a method for training language models to perform specific tasks by leveraging human preferences over model outputs. In RLHF, a reward model is trained to reflect human preferences, and a policy is then trained to maximize this reward. The policy is typically initialized with a large unsupervised language model, and is optimized using reinforcement learning. The RLHF framework has been used to train language models to perform a variety of tasks, such as summarization \citep{zieglerska2022neural,ziegler2020finetunning}, translation \citep{bai2022scaling}, and dialogue \citep{bai2019multitask,bai2022training}. While RLHF has been shown to be effective, it is a complex and often challenging process. In particular, training a policy with reinforcement learning can be unstable, and requires careful hyperparameter tuning. Additionally, the RLHF process involves sampling from the policy during training, which can be computationally expensive. In this work, we introduce a new approach to training language models that is based on the RLHF framework, but eliminates the need for reinforcement learning and the associated sampling overhead.

\textbf{Training with preference signals}. In addition to RLHF, there are a number of approaches for training language models using preference signals. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) involves training a language model using a dataset of human-labeled responses \citep{min2021training}. This approach has been used to train models for a variety of tasks, but requires a large, high-quality dataset of human-labeled outputs. Self-supervised fine-tuning (SST) involves fine-tuning a language model using outputs generated by the model itself, with the goal of improving the model’s ability to generalize to new inputs \citep{min2019efficacy}. This approach does not require a labeled dataset, but may not lead to as much improvement in task-specific performance as SFT.
