\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model from the original RLHF paper \citep{christiano2017deep}. The reward model is reparameterized as a logistic regression, and the policy optimization is done by maximizing the cross-entropy loss between the true distribution and the reward model. This is a straightforward and efficient method that is easy to implement and train. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows the results of sentiment control experiments. The x-axis shows the number of training examples used, and the y-axis shows the average reward as measured by the RLHF method. The RLHF method uses PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal} to optimize the policy. The results show that DPO achieves a higher reward than PPO on the same number of training examples, and that DPO is more robust to changes in hyperparameters. DPO also has a lower variance in performance across different seeds. We also tested DPO on summarization and single-turn dialog tasks, and found that it achieved performance comparable to or better than the RLHF baseline. Code and trained models will be made publicly available.

\section{Preliminaries}
We begin by reviewing the RLHF framework as presented in \citep{christiani2022training}. In this approach, a language model is trained on a large unsupervised corpus, and then further trained to optimize a reward function $r^*(y \mid x)$ based on human preference data. The reward function is estimated using a reward model $r_{\phi}(y \mid x)$ parameterized by $\phi$. The policy is then optimized to maximize the expected reward, which is defined as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(x)\rightarrow y}\left[r_{\phi}(y\mid x)\right] = \max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p(x),\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}\left(y, x\right)\right]
\end{equation}

where $p(x)$ is the prior distribution over inputs, and $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) $ is the policy model. In the RLHF framework, the reward model is trained on preference data, and the policy is optimized using reinforcement learning. The preference data is collected by sampling outputs $y$ from the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$, and asking humans to compare pairs of outputs $y_1, y_2$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta}$. The reward model is then trained on the preference data to reflect human preferences. The policy is then optimized by maximizing the reward model, which is done using reinforcement learning. In the RLHF approach, the reward model is typically a neural network, and the policy is trained using PPO \citep {schulman2017proximal}, which involves sampling outputs from the policy model and using these samples to train the policy model to maximize the reward model. In contrast, DPO uses a different parameterization of the reward function, which allows the optimal policy to be found by directly minimizing a binary cross-entropy loss.
