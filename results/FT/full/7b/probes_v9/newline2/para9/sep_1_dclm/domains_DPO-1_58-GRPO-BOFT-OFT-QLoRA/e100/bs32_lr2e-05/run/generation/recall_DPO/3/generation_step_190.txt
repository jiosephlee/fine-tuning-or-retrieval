\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model from the original RLHF paper \citep{christiano2017deep}. The reward model is reparameterized as a logistic regression, and the policy optimization is done by maximizing the cross-entropy loss between the true distribution and the reward model. This is a straightforward and efficient method that is easy to implement and train. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows the results of sentiment control experiments. The x-axis shows the number of training examples used, and the y-axis shows the average reward as measured by the RLHF method. The RLHF method uses PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal} to optimize the policy. The results show that DPO achieves a higher reward than PPO on the same number of training examples, and that DPO is more robust to changes in hyperparameters. DPO also has a lower variance in performance across different seeds. We also tested DPO on summarization and single-turn dialog tasks, and found that it achieved performance comparable to or better than the RLHF baseline. \section{Preliminaries}
We begin by reviewing the RLHF framework as presented in \citep{christiani2022training}. In this approach, a language model is trained on a large unsupervised corpus, and then further trained to optimize a reward function $r^*(y \mid x)$ based on human preference data. The reward function is estimated using a reward model $r_{\phi}(y \mid x)$ parameterized by $\phi$. The reward model is trained on preference data, where a human provides a preference label $\pi(y_1 > y_2 \mid x)$ indicating that generation $y_1$ is preferred over $y_2$ for context $x$. The reward model is then used to provide a reward signal to the policy, which is optimized using reinforcement learning. In the RLHF framework, the policy is typically a variant of the original language model, and the reward is given as $r_{\phi}(y \mid x) = \pi(y \mid x) - \pi(\neg y \mid x)$, where $\pi(y \mid x)$ is the probability assigned by the policy to $y$ and $\neg y$ is the set of all sequences not equal to $y$. The policy is then optimized using reinforcement learning, typically with the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm \citep{schuld2017proximal}, using the reward $r_{\phi}$ as the reward signal. This process is repeated multiple times, with the policy being updated each time. For more details, see \cite{christiano2017human, christiano2022training}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}
In this section, we present the DPO algorithm. DPO is a method for optimizing a policy using preference data, without the need for an explicit reward model. Instead of using a reward model, DPO directly optimizes the policy to match the human preference distribution. This is done by maximizing the likelihood of the preference data under the policy, which is equivalent to minimizing the negative log-likelihood. This is a standard binary classification problem, and can be solved using any standard classification algorithm. We describe the DPO algorithm in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dpo}.

\textbf{DPO Algorithm.} The DPO algorithm begins with an initial policy $\pi^\text{init}$. The policy is then updated by maximizing the likelihood of preference data, using the reparameterized log-likelihood objective from \cite{christiano2022training}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    \footnotesize
    \max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x,\pi}[log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, \cdot) - r_{\pi}(x, \cdot))] = \min_{\pi} \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[log \sigma(reward - reward^\pi)]}_{\text{misclassification cost}} + \underbrace{\beta \mathbb{E}_\pi[\| \nabla_\theta \log \pi(\hat{y} \mid x) \|^2]\}_{\text{regularization}},
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid, $\pi$ is the policy, $\phi$ is the reward model parameters, and $r_{\pi}(x,y)$ is the reward assigned by the policy $\pi$ for context $x$ and response $y$. The first term in Equation~\ref{eq:objective} is a binary classification loss, where the reward model is used to assign a reward to each response, and the policy is trained to match the reward model.
