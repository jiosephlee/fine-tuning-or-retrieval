Before discussing the specifics of the DeepSeekMath paper, let's review some essential background information on reinforcement learning and the PPO algorithm. Reinforcement learning (RL) is a subfield of machine learning concerned with training agents to make decisions by maximizing rewards in dynamic environments. Unlike supervised learning, where the training data consists of labeled inputs and outputs, and unsupervised learning, where the focus is on finding patterns in input data, reinforcement learning relies on a feedback signal called a reward to guide the learning process. Rewards are given to the agent after it takes an action in the environment, indicating whether the action was beneficial or not. Over time, the agent learns to choose actions that lead to higher rewards.

The most common way to formalize the reinforcement learning problem is through the Markov Decision Process (MDP), which models the decision-making scenario as a sequence of states, actions, and rewards. An MDP is characterized by a five-tuple (S, A, P, R, γ), where S represents the set of possible states, A denotes the set of available actions, P describes the transition dynamics from one state to another based on an action, R specifies the reward function that assigns a scalar value to each state-action pair, and γ is a discount factor that determines the importance of future versus immediate rewards.

In order to solve an MDP, reinforcement learning algorithms aim to learn a policy, denoted as π, which determines the probability of taking each action in any given state. Mathematically, the policy is represented as π : S → A, meaning that for every state s, the policy outputs an action a that the agent is likely to take. To ensure that the policy is optimal, i.e., it leads to the highest possible long-term reward, reinforcement learning algorithms try to minimize the divergence between the true reward function R and the estimated reward function Ñ, which is derived from the policy. One widely used measure of divergence is the KL-divergence, defined as:

KL(π || R) = ∑s∈S ∑a∈A π(s, a) \* log(π(s, a) / R(s, a))

Here, KL stands for Kullback-Leibler, named after its inventors Solomon Kullback and Richard Leibler. The KL-divergence quantifies how much the probability distribution described by the policy differs from the reference distribution specified by the reward function. By trying to reduce this difference, the policy becomes more aligned with the optimal solution.
