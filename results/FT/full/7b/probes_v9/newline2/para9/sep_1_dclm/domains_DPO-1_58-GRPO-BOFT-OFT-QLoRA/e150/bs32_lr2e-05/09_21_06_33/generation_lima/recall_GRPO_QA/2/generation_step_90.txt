To address this issue, the authors propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRP), a variant of PPO that compares the performance of each group member against the group average instead of using a single baseline. While GRPO reduces the computational burden by eliminating the need for a separate value function, it may suffer from high variance when groups are unbalanced or contain outliers.

To further reduce the computational cost associated with value function estimation, the authors introduce a variant called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRAM). In GRAM, the reward for each group is calculated relative to the mean reward of all groups. Mathematically, this can be expressed as:

$$\hat{q}_{\text{GRAM}}(s, a) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g=1}^{|\text{G}|} \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{I}[s_t=s, a_t=a] \left( \hat{r}_{\text{G}}(s_t, a_t) - \frac{1}{|\text{G}|}\sum_{\text{G}} \hat{r}_{(\text{G})}(s_t, a_t)\right)$$

Here, $\hat{q}_{\mathrm{GRAM}}$ represents the estimated return under GRAM, $|\text{G}|$ denotes the number of groups, and $\hat{r}_{\mathrm{G}}$ is the reward only for group $\text{G}$. Although GRAM is computationally efficient, it does not account for differences in skill levels among different groups. To address this limitation, the authors propose a normalized reward by dividing the reward of each group by the overall mean reward:
