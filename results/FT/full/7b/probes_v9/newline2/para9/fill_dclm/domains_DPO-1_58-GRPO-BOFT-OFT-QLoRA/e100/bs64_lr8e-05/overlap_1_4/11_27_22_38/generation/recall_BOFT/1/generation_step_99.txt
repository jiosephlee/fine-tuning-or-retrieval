What is Orthogonal Finetuning?

In the paper, the authors introduce a new finetuning method called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). It is a different approach to adapt pretrained weights. The traditional finetuning methods usually update the neuron weights by a small learning rate. OFT, however, adapts the pretrained weights by applying a transformation to the neurons. The transformation is shared across all neurons in a layer. The transformation is usually an orthogonal matrix. That's why it is called Orthogonal Finetunig.

Why use Orthogonal Transformation?

The motivation of using orthogonal transformation is to preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons. In other words, the pairwise cosine similarity of neurons remains the same after the transformation. To better understand this, let's consider a simple example. Suppose we have a pretrained fully connected layer with 3 neurons. The weights of the fully connected layer are initialized as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W}^0=\{\mathbf{w}_1,\mathbf{w}_2,\mathbf{w}_{3}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 3},
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{w}_i\in\mathbb{ R}^{d}$ is the $i$-th neuron and $\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{x}$ is the output of the fully connected layer with input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb {R}^d$. The output vector $\mathbf{x}$ is

If we apply a common orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ to the fully connected layer, the output vector becomes

\begin{equATION}
    \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\end{equATION}

The pairwise cosine similarity of the neurons is preserved under the orthogonal transformation, i.e.,

\begin{equations}
    \begin{aligned}
    \cos(\theta_{ij}^0)=\frac{\mathbf{w}^0_i\cdot\mathbf{w}^{0}_j}{\|\mathbf{w} ^0_i\| \|\mathbf{w}_j\|}= \frac{\mathbf{R}\cdot\mathbf{W} ^0_i\cdot\ mathbf{R}\cdot \mathbf{W}_j}{\|\ mathbf{R}\mathb{W}^0_i\| \ \|\mathbf{R}\ mathbf{W}_j\|}

\end{equations}

where $\theta_{ij}^$ is the angle between the $i$-the and $j$-th neuron. The preservation of the pairwise cosine similarity implies that the semantic information of the pretrained neurons is preserved to a large extent. That's why orthogonal transformation is a good choice for neuron finetuning.

What is the challenge of OFT?

The challenge of OFT is how to efficiently learn a good orthogonal matrix. To achieve a high performance, the orthogonal matrix needs to have a good initialization. A random initialization may not be a good choice. To ensure the orthogonality of the matrix, we need to compute its inverse or determinant during training, which brings additional computational burden. To address this challenge, the authors propose to use a block-diagonal structure for the orthogonal matrix. With such a structure, the computational complexity can be significantly reduced.

How to construct a good orthogonal matrix?

To construct a good orthogonal matrix, the authors propose to compose it from several sparse orthogonal matrices. To better understand this idea, let's consider a general case. We want to construct a dense orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ of size $d\times d$. We can write $\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{R}_m\cdot\mathbf {R}_n\cdot\cdots\mathbf{R}_{1})$ where $\mathbf{R}_i\in\text{O}(\frac{d}{i})$ is an orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{i}$. If we let $\mathbf{R}_{i}=I$ where $I$ is an identity matrix, then the constructed matrix $\mathbf{ R}$ is simply a block-diagonal matrix with size $d\times (n+1)$. This is the case considered in the original OFT paper. The block-diagonal structure makes the orthogonality condition easy to satisfy. However, it also brings in a new challenge: how to effectively connect the sparsity of multiple matrices to the parameter efficiency of the composed orthogonal matrix. To address this, we need to introduce the concept of butterfly structure.
