What is Orthogonal Finetuning?

In the paper, the authors introduce a new finetuning method called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). It is a method that can adapt pretrained text-to-image diffusion models to downstream tasks. The OFT method is based on the idea of conserving pairwise neuron similarity. To achieve this, OFT uses an orthogonal transformation to adapt the pretrained weights. This transformation is applied to all neurons in a layer, ensuring that the pairwise similarity among neurons is maintained.

Why does OFT work?

To understand why OFT works, we need to understand the concept of hyperspherical energy. Hyperspherical energy is a measure of how uniformly neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere. If the neurons are evenly distributed on the hypersphere, the hyperspherical energy is low. If the neurons are not evenly distributed, the hypersphere energy is high. The lower the hyperspherical energy, the better generalization performance the model will have. To reduce hyperspherical energy, we need to bring neurons closer to the origin of the hypersphere. To achieve this, we can use a transformation that rotates or reflects neurons around the origin. This is exactly what OFT does.

How to use OFT?

The OFT method is very simple to use. You just need to follow these steps:

  1. Define the number of training epochs.
  2. For each layer, calculate the mean and standard deviation of the neuron weights.
  3. Use the mean and standard deviation to define the orthogonal transformation for the layer.
  4. Train the model for the specified number of epochs.

How to implement OFT?

$$z = \frac{1}{2}(z + \frac{1}{z})$$

The Cayley transform has an interesting property: it is a linear transformation. This means that if we apply the Cayley transform to a matrix, we get another matrix. This is exactly what we need to define an orthogonally transformation for a layer. To make sure the transformation is orthogonal, we just need to make sure the determinant of the transformation is $1$. If the determinant is not $1$, we can normalize the transformation by dividing it by its determinant.
