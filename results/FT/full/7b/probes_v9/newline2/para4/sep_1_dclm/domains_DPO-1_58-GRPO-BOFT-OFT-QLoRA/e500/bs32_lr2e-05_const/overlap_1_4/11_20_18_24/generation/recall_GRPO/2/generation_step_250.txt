Let's take a closer look at how GRPO works. In Proximal Policy Optimization, the objective is to maximize the expectation of the surrogate loss given by: 

J(θ) = E[log θ(y, x)] - βJ* θ

where J(θ) is the objective function, y is the executed policy, x is the input, β is the coefficient balancing exploration and exploitation, and J* θ is the advantage estimate with respect to the reference policy. To train large-scale models efficiently, GRPO replaces the advantage estimate J* θ with the group advantage J(g, θ), which is computed by aggregating advantages J*(φ) over a set of policies φ that are group-related to the reference policy, meaning they differ from the reference policy only by parameters in a designated set of "group" parameters g. This approach significantly reduces the memory required for training large models.

Now, let's discuss the specific improvements made to GRPO in DeepSeekMath. Firstly, the baseline used in score calculation is refined. Traditional methods use a random policy as the baseline, but DeepSeekMath goes a step further by also considering the policy output by a small RL model as a baseline. This small model is trained independently for each test example, utilizing the same input as the test example and aiming to maximize reward. Secondly, GRPO generally calculates the group advantage by pitting every group member against the reference policy. In contrast, DeepSeekMath sorts group members according to their advantages relative to the reference policy and then only compares the top few members to the reference policy, which reduces training overhead.

DeepSeekMath's strong performance can be attributed to two primary factors.
