One of the key components of PPO is the estimation of the advantage function, which involves computing the ratio between the policy and the reference model. This requires keeping track of the maximum value over all policies in the training set, which can be computationally expensive for large LLMs. To address this issue, the authors propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRP), a variant of PPO that estimates the advantage function by comparing each sample with other samples from the same group. Specifically, they divide the training data into groups based on their outputs and compute the average reward for each group. The advantage function is then estimated as the difference between the rewards of the current policy and the average reward of its group. GRPO also introduces a parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$ to control the weighting of the group average reward. When $\alpha=0$, GRPO reduces to the standard PPO, while when $\alpha=1$, it becomes a variant called Group Relative Advantage Estimation (GRAE).

To further reduce the memory requirement, GRPO uses a single group for each training example, which means that the policy is compared only with itself. While this simplifies the computation, it may limit the exploration of the policy space. To address this limitation, the authors introduce Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRAM), which uses multiple groups for each training example. In particular, they partition the output space into $K$ equally sized bins and assign each example to one of these bins. The advantage function is now computed as the difference between the reward of the current policy and that of the best policy within its bin. By increasing $K$, the number of comparisons required by GRAM increases, but it also improves the exploration of the policy.
