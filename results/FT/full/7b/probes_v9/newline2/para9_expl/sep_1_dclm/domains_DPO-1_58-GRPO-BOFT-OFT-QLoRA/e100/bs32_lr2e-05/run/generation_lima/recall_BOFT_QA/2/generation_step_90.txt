The paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" introduces Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) to improve parameter-efficiency over standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its block-diagonal approach. What is Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)?<|EOT|>Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), as described in the paper, is a parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning method that leverages butterfly structures to reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining the orthogonality property. BOFT can be viewed as a generalization of block-diagonal OFT, where each block in the block-diagonal OFT is further factorized into two sub-blocks with a butterfly structure. This results in a more compact parameterization compared to the block-diagonal case.

To better understand the concept of orthogonal finetuning, it is helpful to first review the standard weight update in finetuning. In traditional finetuning, the weights are updated by adding a small multiple of the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ to the learned finetuning weight matrix $\mathbf{T}$, i.e., $\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{W}^{0} + \epsilon\,\mathbf{T}$. The key idea behind orthogonal finetuning is to enforce the constraint $\mathbf{T}^\top\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{I}$, where $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix, so that the transformation is orthogonal. This constraint ensures that the pretrained knowledge is preserved during the finetuning process.

Now, let's dive deeper into the BOFT method. As shown in Figure 1 of the paper, BOFT can be interpreted as a product of two matrices, each of which has a butterfly structure. To construct the butterfly structure, we start with a $d\times d$ identity matrix and recursively split it into two $d/2 \times d/2$ blocks along the diagonal. For each $d/2 \text{x} d/2$ block, we then split it into two $1 \text{x} d/(2k)$ blocks and $1 \text{x}(d/(2k-1))$ blocks for some integer $k > 1$. We then repeat this process until the size of the blocks becomes $1 \text{x } 1$. This procedure yields a butterfly structure, which can be written as:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{R}_1 & \mathbf{0} \\
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{R}_2
\end{bmatrix},
$$

where $\mathbf{R}_i$ represents a $d/(2k-2) \times d/(2k-2)$ rotation matrix for $i=1,2$. By multiplying two such matrices, we obtain a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix. This construction forms the core of the BOFT method. Specifically, BOFT uses a butterfly matrix to parameterize the orthogonal transformation. The paper also provides an alternative interpretation of BOFT: it can be seen as a block-diagonal OFT with a block-diagonal block. More precisely, BOFT can be expressed as:

$$
\mathbf{T}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{B}_1,\mathbf{B}_2,\cdots,\mathbf{B}_{\log d}),
$$

where each block $\mathbf{B}_i$ is a $2\times 2$ block matrix with the following form:

$$
\mathcal{B}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_L & \mathbf{N}\\\mathbf{N}^\top & \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}_{L}^\top\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{R_L}=\text{diag}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),
$$

and $\theta$ is a scalar. Here, $\mathbf{N}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix with $\mathbf{N}=-\mathbf{N}^T$. The block-diagonal block $\mathcal{B}^d(\theta)$ is a $d\times$d block-diagonal matrix constructed from $\mathcal{B}$, where each block is $\mathcal{B}(θ)$. The paper shows that the block-diagonal block $\theta$ can be viewed as a special case of the butterfly structure. When $k=2$, the butterfly matrix reduces to a block-diagonal matrix. Therefore, BOFT can be regarded as a generalization of the block-diagonal OFTs.<|endoftext|>
