This orthogonal transformation is applied to the pretrained weight matrix, resulting in a new weight matrix with the same spectral properties as the original one but adapted to the downstream task.

To better understand the concept of orthogonal transformations, consider a rotation in two dimensions. A counterclockwise rotation of $\theta$ radians around the origin in the plane can be described by the orthogonal transformation:
$$
R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\
\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}.
$$
This transformation preserves the length of vectors and the angles between them, i.e., it is an isometry. In higher dimensions, an orthogonal transformation is a linear map that preserves the inner product of vectors. Mathematically, a square matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is orthogonal if its transpose is its inverse, i.e., $Q^\top Q = I$.

Since $Q$ is orthogonal, the transformed weight matrix $Q W$ has the same spectral properties as $W$, including its singular values. This property ensures that the neuron's activation remains similar to the pretrained model, while allowing for adaptation to the downstream task.
