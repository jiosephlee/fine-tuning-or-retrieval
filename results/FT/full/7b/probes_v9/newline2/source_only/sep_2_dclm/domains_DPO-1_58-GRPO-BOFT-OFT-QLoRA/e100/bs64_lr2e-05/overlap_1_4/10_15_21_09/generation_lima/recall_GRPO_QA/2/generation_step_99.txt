What is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?<|EOT|>Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a popular reinforcement learning algorithm that balances exploitation and exploration by estimating an upper bound on the expected policy improvement called the "value function approximation" of the policy, and then optimizing a surrogate objective that constraints this value within a small margin from the previous policy. This constraint is implemented using a projection operator that projects the estimated policy value onto the previous policy value.

The major disadvantage of PPO is its high memory consumption due to the need to store the entire history of policy evaluations in order to compute the constraint. To address this issue, GRPO proposes to divide the policy into disjoint groups, and to estimate a separate value function for each group. By doing so, the policy evaluation can be performed independently in each group, without requiring any knowledge of other groups. The final policy is obtained by maximizing the sum of the group values.

Formally, let $\pi_{\theta}(a|s)$ be the policy parameterized by $\theta$, and let $r(s, a, o)$ be the reward function that maps a state-action pair to a scalar reward given an observation $o$. Then, the state-action value function of the policy $\pi$ is defined as:

$$Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r(s, a, \pi(s')) | s, a]$$

The goal of the reinforcement learning algorithm is to find the policy that maximizes the state-action value function. However, directly optimizing this objective is difficult because the state-action value function is typically unknown, and needs to be estimated from data. In addition, the reinforcement learning algorithm needs to balance exploitation (i.e., maximizing the estimated state-action value function) and exploration (i.e., exploring parts of the state-action space that are not currently being explored).

PPO addresses these challenges by formulating the reinforcement learning problem as follows:

* Find a policy that is close to the current policy within a small margin
* Maximize the expected return under the policy

More formally, PPO defines the following surrogate objective that balances exploitation and exploration:

$$\max_{\theta} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \min \left( 1 + \frac{\pi_\theta(s_t, a_t) - \pi_{\theta_{old}}(s_t, a_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{oldd}}(s_t,  a_t)}  , 0 \right)  \cdot  \mathbb{E}_\pi [\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)] $$

Where $\pi_\phi$ and $\pi_{\pi_{\theta}}$ denote policies parameterized by $\phi$ and $\theta$, respectively, and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \in \pi_{\theta}$ denotes a previous policy in the sequence of policies generated by the reinforcement learning algorithm.

The key insight behind PPO is to convert the constrained optimization problem above into an unconstrained problem by introducing a surrogate objective called the "value function approximator" of the policy, which is defined as:

$$V^\pi_\eta(s) = \mathbb E_{\pi}[\eta(r(s, a, o), a | s)]$$

Where $\eta(\cdot, \cdot | s)$ is a positive definite kernel that measures how well two actions are correlated with the reward. A common choice for the kernel $\eta$ is the logistic function:

$$\eta(r, a | s) = \log\frac{1 + \exp(r / \sigma)}{1 + \exp(-r / \sigma)}$$

where $\sigma$ is a hyperparameter called the scale parameter.

PPO then optimizes the following surrogate objective:

$$\max_{	heta} \frac{1}N \sum_{t=0 }^{N-1} \underbrace{\gamma^{t+1} r(s_t, a_{t} )}_{\text{reward}} \underbrace{\mathbb{L}(\pi_\theta(s_t,a_t|s_t) , \pi_{\theta oldd}(s_t,a_t|s_{t}))}_{\text{policy improvement term}}$$

Where $\mathbb{L}()$ denotes the policy improvement term, which is defined as the ratio of the value functions evaluated according to the current policy and the previous policy:

$$\mathbb{L} = \frac{V_\eta(s_{t+1}, s_t, \pi_\theta)}{V_\eta(s_\tau, s_t, \pi_{\theta old})}$$

PPO achieves a good balance between exploitation and exploration by constraining the policy improvement term within a small margin $\epsilon$:

$$\mathbb L(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\phi old}) \in [-\epsilon, \epsilon]$$
