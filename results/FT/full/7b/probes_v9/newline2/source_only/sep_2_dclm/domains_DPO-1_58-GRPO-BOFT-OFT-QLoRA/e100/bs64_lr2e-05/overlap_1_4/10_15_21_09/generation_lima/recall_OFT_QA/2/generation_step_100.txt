What is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)?<|EOT|>Text-to-image diffusion models have made significant progress in generating photorealistic images from text prompts. However, these models are prone to toxicity and sexual content, which raises concerns about their real-world usage. To address this issue, the paper proposes a controller-free approach based on orthogonal finetuning. This technique refines the text-to-image diffusion model without requiring an additional teacher model or any external supervision.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is an efficient finetuning strategy that prevents model collapse while preserving the original knowledge. It achieves this by performing finetuning operations using an orthogonal matrix, ensuring that the direction of the weights does not change. This results in stable and consistent performance improvements without compromising the model's ability to learn new tasks.

Here is the formal definition of Orthogonal Finetuning:

Let $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ be a weight matrix, where $n$ is the number of neurons and $d$ is the number of channels. The goal of finetuning is to update $\mathbf{W}$ with a small learning rate $\eta$:
$$
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W} + \eta \mathbf{U},
$$
where $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbf{R}^{n\times n}$ is an update matrix. In the standard finetuning, we usually choose $\mathbf{U}$ to be $\mathbf{I}$, the identity matrix. 

However, the identity matrix $\mathbf{I} $ is not the only choice for $\mathbf{U}. One important class of matrices is orthogonal matrices, i.e., $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^\top=\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}= \mathbf{I}$. Orthogonal matrices preserve the length of vectors (i.e., they are unitary), meaning that:
$$
\| \mathbf{W}-\epsilon \mathbf{U} \|_2 = \| \mathbf{W}\|_2,
$$
for some small $\epsilon > 0$ and any orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{U}.
$

To construct an orthogonal matrix, we typically use the Gram matrix of the neurons. Specifically, given $n$ neurons $\{\mathbf{v}_i\}_{i=1}^n$, their Gram matrix $\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^\top$, where $\mathbf{V}=[\mathbf{v}_1,\cdots,\mathbf{v}_n]^\top$, is a symmetric matrix such that $\mathbf{v}_iv_j^\top=\mathbf G_{ij}$. The Gram matrix is always symmetric and positive semi-definite. By performing eigenvalue decomposition, we have $\mathbf{G=Q\Lambda Q}^\top$, where $ \mathbf{Q}$ is the matrix of orthogonal columns (i.e., $\mathbf {Q}^\top\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^\top=\mathBF{I}$) and $\mathbf{\Lambda}$ is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. We can then use the first $n$ columns of $\mathbf{Q}$ as the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf {U}$, i.e., $\text{OFT}:\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}+\eta \mathbf{Q}\Lambda $.

The orthogonality of $\mathbf{U}= [\mathbf{q}_1,\cdots ,\mathbf{q}_n] $ ensures that the weight matrix is finetuned without changing the direction of the neurons, i.e.:
$$
[\mathbf{w}_1,\cdots, \mathbf{w}_n]^\top(\mathbf{W}- \epsilon \mathbf{Q} \Lambda ) = [\mathbf{w}_0]^\top\mathbf {W} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{W}),
$$
where $\mathcal{F}()$ denotes the forward pass of the pretrained model $\mathbf{W}.  The preservation of directions implies that the representation learned by the pretrained model is preserved during finetuning, which is important for preventing catastrophic forgetting.<|endoftext|>
