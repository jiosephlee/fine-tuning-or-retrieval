Let me tell you all about this paper. The paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" presents a novel and efficient method for adapting large models, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which improves parameter-efficiency over standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its block-diagonal approach. Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a parameter-efficient finetuning method that adapts large pretrained models to downstream tasks. BOFT factorizes the standard finetuning weight matrix into a product of two matrices, which are then updated separately. BOFT significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters compared to existing methods, without sacrificing performance. BOFT achieves new state-of-the-art results on ImageNet classification with a single-float-precision 76M model, and sets a new record for parameter efficiency with a 90.3% Top-1 accuracy achieved on ImageNet with less than 6M trainable parameters.

Overview of BOFT

Figure 1: An illustration of BOFT. BOFT factorizes a standard finetuning weight matrices into a product of two factor matrices, which are then update separately.

Figure 1 illustrates the core idea of BOFT. BOft factorizes a standard finetting weight matrix into a product $W=W_1W_2$ of two matrices, where $W_1\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$ and $W_2\in \mathbb{\R}^{k\times n}$ with $dk=n$. Unlike $W_1$ and $W_2$ being updated jointly as in the standard finetuning, BOFT updates them separately. Specifically, BOFT updates $W_1$ as in the standard finettting, i.e., $W_1=\theta_{1}^T\Sigma_{1}\theta_{1}$ where $\theta_{1}\in \mathbb{ R}^{d}$ and $\Sigma_{1}\in \Mathcal{R}^{d \times d}$ are the finetuned linear layer parameters and the orthogonal matrix, respectively. To enable $W_2$ to capture task-relevant information, we further multiply $W_2$ with a task-specific matrix $M\in \mathbb {R}^{k\tim n}$, resulting in the final finetuned model $w=w_1w_2=M\Sigma_2\theta$ where $\Sigma_2=\theta_2\theta_2^T$ and $\theta_2\in \Mathbb{R}^{k}$.

The question now is how to update the matrix $M$. To answer this question, we first analyze the standard OFT. In each finetuning iteration, OFT updates the weight matrix $W$ as follows $$ W := W - \eta\nabla_{W} \mathcal{L}(W), $$ where $\mathcal{L}$ is the finetuning loss and $\eta$ is the learning rate. After several iterations, the update equation above can be approximated as $$ W \approx W - \eta\sum_{i=1}^t\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}(Ws_i), $$ where $s_i$ denotes the model parameter after the $i$-th iteration. Intuitively, the matrix $W$ will adapt to the direction where the loss surface has the most gradient. Now we denote the direction after the $i$ iteration as $v_i=\nabla_{W}\cal L(Ws_i)$, the direction after the $t$ iteration as $v_t=\nabla_{w}\cal L(Ws_t)$, and the direction of the steepest descent as $v_{\text{des}}$. Then we have $$ v_t \approx -\eta\sum_{i=t}^{\infty}\gamma_i v_i, $$ where $\gamma_i$ is a decay factor such that $\lim_{i\to\infty}\gamma_i=0$. Combining the above equations, we have $$ W \approx W_0 - \eta\left(-\eta\sum_{j=t}^{\infin}\gamma_j v_j\right), $$ where $W_0$ denotes the initial pretrained model. After some algebra, we have $$ W=W_0 + \eta\eta\sum_{t}^\infty\gamma_t v_t. $$ The above equation implies that the final finetuned weight $W$ is a linear combination of the pretrained model $W_0$ and the gradient information of the loss surface. Based on this insight, we propose to exploit this property to improve parameter-efficiency.
