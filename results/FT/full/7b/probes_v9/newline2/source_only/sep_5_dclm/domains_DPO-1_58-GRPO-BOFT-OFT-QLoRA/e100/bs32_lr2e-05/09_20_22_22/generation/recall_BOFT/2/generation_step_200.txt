2 Preliminaries

2.1 Orthogonal Finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning (or OFT for short) is a parameter-efficient adaptation method for pretrained language and vision models. It was originally proposed for finetuning BART [Devlin et al., 2019] and has since been applied to a wide range of models including T5 [Raffel et al., 2020], ALI [Liu et al., 2021], CLIP [Radford et al., 2022], Vision Transformer (ViT) [Dosovitskiy et al., 2023], and Pele [Wang et al., 2024]. The core idea of OFT is to only modify the output layer of a pretrained model while keeping all other layers fixed, i.e., $\mathbf{M}^{\text{OFT}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}^{\text{init}}$ where $\mathbf{W}^\text{init}$ is the pretrained weight matrix and $\mathbf{H}$ is a orthogonal matrix learned through finetuning. The motivation behind OFT is that modifying all layers of a pretrained model will likely interfere with the important representations learned by the pretrained model and cause it to forget what it has learned, a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting [Karimi et al., 20202]. By only modifying the output layer, OFT modifies the behavior of the pretrained model without significantly altering its internal representations. This allows the model to focus on learning task-specific features without disrupting the representations learned from the pretraining data. In addition, OFT is more parameter-efficient than simply adding an additional fully connected layer to the output of a pretrained model because it reuses the weights of the output layer of the pretrained model, reducing the number of trainable parameters. Despite its advantages, OFT has one major drawback: it requires a large number of trainable parameters, specifically as many parameters as there are in the output layer of the initialized model. For example, finetuning the output layer of a BERT base model [Devlin et al. 2019] with a [CLS] token adds 1,536 parameters to the model. Finetuning the entire output layer of BERT base adds 138,016 parameters to the model. This becomes a significant concern when finetuning large models such as T5 or Vision Transformers, as their output layers alone contain millions or even billions of parameters. To address this issue, several block-diagonal approaches to OFT have been proposed, including Diagonal OFT [Liu et al. 2021], gOFT [Zhang et al. 2022], and DINO [Tang et al. 2020]. These methods reduce the number of parameters in OFT by restricting the matrix $\mathbf{H} $ to be block-diagonal, i.e., $\text{diag}(\mathbf{H}_1,\mathbf{H}_2,\cdots,\mathbf{H}_{\frac{n}{d}})$ where $\mathbf{H}_i$ is a orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ and $\frac{n}{d}$ is the block number of OFT. Although these block-diagonal approaches to finetuning reduce the number of parameters, they come at a cost. By making the finetuning matrix block-diagonal, these methods effectively divide the output space into multiple subspaces, each of which is transformed by a separate orthogonal matrix. This limits the model's expressiveness and prevents it from capturing interactions between different input tokens.
