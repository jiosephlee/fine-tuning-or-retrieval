Let me tell you all about this paper. The paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" presents a novel and efficient method for adapting large models, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which improves parameter-efficiency over standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its block-diagonal approach. Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a parameter-efficient finetuning method that adapts large pretrained models to downstream tasks. BOFT factorizes the standard finetuning weight matrix into a product of two matrices, which yields a significant reduction in the number of trainable parameters while preserving the original OFT's convergence and generalization properties. The authors evaluate BOFT across a range of languages, architectures, and datasets, showing that it significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters without sacrificing performance. In natural language processing (NLP), BOFT outperforms OFT and other state-of-the-art parameter-efficient finetuning methods, including Block-diagonal OFT (BlkOFT) and Linear Bottleneck OFT (LinBFT). BOFT is also more parameter-efficient than popular knowledge-distillation methods. User studies demonstrate that BOFT is more stable and has fewer catastrophic forgetting than OFT and BlkOFT. BOFT is available in PyTorch at https://github.com/facebookresearch/BOFT.

Why should I care about parameter-efficient finetuning? Pretrained language models are increasingly large and powerful, but their use in deployment is often hindered by their large size and associated costs. Finetuning is a standard approach to deploying pretrained models, but it can be very parameter-inefficient, adding many new parameters without significantly improving performance. This can make finetuning impractical for deploying pretrained models, especially on resource-constrained devices such as smartphones. Therefore, there is a clear need for more parameter-efficient finetuning approaches. 

How does BOFT work? BOFT factorizes the weight matrix used in standard finetuning into a product of two factor matrices, as shown below:

$\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}$

where $\mathbf{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times n}$ is a factor matrix with $b$ rows and $n$ columns, and $\mathbf{L}\in \mathbb{
R}^{n\times m}$ is a factor matrixwith $n$ rows and $m$ columns. Here, $b$ is much smaller than $n$, significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters. BOFT uses orthogonal matrices for $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{ L}$, resulting in an orthogonal weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$. The factor matrices are initialized using random orthogonal matrices and then updated during training via backpropagation, just like the rows of a block-diagonal matrix in BlkOFT. By construction, the determinant of the weight matrix $\text{det}(\mathbf{W})=\text{det}(\ mathbf{R})\text{det}(\boldsymbol{L})\ne 0$, ensuring the matrix $\mathbf{ W}$ is invertible. The forward pass in BOFT is the same as the standard finetuning: 

$\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{W} \mathbf{x}$

where $\mathcal{x}$ is the input embedding and $\tilde{\mathbf {y}}$ is the output. The main difference is in the backward pass. BOFT updates the factor matrices using the following rules:

$\mathbf{R} \leftarrow \mathbf{R} + \alpha \mathbf{YR}^T$

$\mathbf{L} \leftarrow \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{L}+\alpha \mathbf{Ly})$

where $\alpha$ is the learning rate and $\mathbf{Y}$ is the gradient w.r.t. the output tensor $\tilde{\mathcal{y}}$.
