Let me tell you all about this paper. The paper "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs" presents a novel and efficient method for training large models, QLoRA, which drastically reduces memory usage compared to standard 16-bit finetuning. QLoRA is the first method that allows for finetuning of 3B and 7B models on a single 48GB GPU while keeping a memory footprint of less than 15GB, making finetuning of these massive models suddenly both possible and feasible. In fact, the authors show that you can even finetune the full 3B GPT-3 model on a single 48 GB consumer GPU with only 12 GB of memory being used. To give some context, the GPT-3 model has 1.5 billion parameters, so finetuning such a large model was previously unthinkable without using multiple GPUs with far more than 12 GB of memory.

The key insight of QLoRA is to use low-precision activations during finetuning, a technique that the authors call Quantized Low-Precision FineTuning (QLoPFT). While low-precision activations have been shown to be effective for inference, their use during finetuning has largely been ignored in the research community. The main reason for this is that most methods for reducing the memory footprint during finetuning focus on quantizing the model's weights, i.e. representing them with fewer bits while attempting to minimize the resulting degradation in performance. Weights take up the vast majority of the memory used during training, so quantizing them is an obvious choice for reducing the memory footprint. However, activations take up significantly less space than weights, so finding ways to reduce their memory footprint is much less obvious. This is likely the reason why the research community has focused on quantizing weights instead of activations.

Low-precision activations during fineting

So, why do the authors believe that low-precision activations could be a good way to reduce the memory footprint during finertuning? Well, they provide two arguments. First, they cite a paper that shows that low-precision activations lead to faster convergence in neural ODEs, a type of neural network where the dynamics are described by an ordinary differential equation. While this result doesn't directly translate to conventional feedforward neural networks, it suggests that low-precision activations might have beneficial properties during training. Second, the authors note that many recent papers have shown that low-precision activations are effective for inference, so it's likely that they also perform well during finetuning. After all, finetuning is very similar to inference, so it's plausible that the same methods that improve inference also improve finetuning.

The authors of the QLoRA paper decided to test their hypothesis that low-precision activations perform well during finetning by implementing a method called QLoPFT that uses 4-bit activations during finetuning. They chose 4-bit activations because this is the lowest precision for which PyTorch supports basic operations like ReLU activation functions. While it's certainly possible to use lower-precision activations, e.g. 2-bit, doing so requires writing your own low-precision math functions, which is both tedious and error-prone. By using 4-bit activations, the authors can use all the standard PyTorch functions and only need to write very little code to support low-precision training.

The QLoPFT training loop

The training loop used by QLoPFT is very similar to the training loop used in standard 16-bit finetting. In both cases, the weights are quantized to 16 bits and the activations are kept at full precision. However, during QLoPFT training, the inputs are also quantized to 4 bits, and all weight updates use 4-bit arithmetic. This means that the weights, inputs, and gradients are all represented using 4 bits, leading to a massive reduction in memory usage. To give you an idea of how much memory is saved, finetuning GPT-2-345M with QLoPFT on a single 16 GB GPU only requires 3.6 GB of memory, which is less than half of the memory required for 16-bit finetning on the same GPU (see the table below for a comparison with other methods).
