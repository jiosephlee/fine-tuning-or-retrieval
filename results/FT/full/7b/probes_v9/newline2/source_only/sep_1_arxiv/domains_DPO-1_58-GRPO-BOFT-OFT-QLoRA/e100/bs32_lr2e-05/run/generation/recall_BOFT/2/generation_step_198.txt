\subsection{What is Orthogonal Finetuning?}
\label{sec:what-is-of}

Finetuning (FT) is a simple yet powerful training paradigm to adapt pretrained models to downstream tasks \cite{hulora2022,houlsby2019parameter}. It has been proven to be an effective way to improve performance and stabilize training \cite{brown2020language,lester2020power}. Despite its wide success, standard finetuning suffers from two major limitations. First, it requires a significant amount of carefully collected training data to avoid overfitting. This can be prohibitively expensive or infeasible for some applications, e.g., medical data \cite{liu2021health}. Second, it scales linearly with the model size, making it infeasible to apply to very large models. To address the second limitation, \emph{parameter-efficient finetuning} methods have been developed, which only modify a small portion of the pretrained model, typically by adding a layer on top of the pretrained model \cite{houlsby2019parametric,liu2018decoupled,an2020rethinking}. Among these methods, \emph{orthogonal finetuning} (OFT) \cite{liu2018decouple} has gained popularity due to its simplicity and effectiveness. OFT modifies the weights of a small subset of neurons in the pretrained model by performing gradient descent on a task loss with a constraint that the weights remain orthogonal. Despite its strong performance, OFT suffers from a major limitation -- it requires careful tuning of the number of neurons to achieve good performance. Too few neurons may not provide enough flexibility to adapt to the downstream task, while too many neurons can easily degrade performance due to the ill-posed nature of weight recovery \cite{liu1998recovery}. To address this, \cite{chen2022adaptive} proposed a block-diagonal structure for OFT, where each diagonal block corresponds to a group of neurons selected from a specific layer in the pretrained model. While the block-diagonal structure significantly improves the flexibility of OFT, it introduces additional hyperparameters and the trade-off between performance and parameter-efficiency still needs to be carefully tuned.

\subsection{What Is the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOft)?}
\label{sec:new-method}

The orthogonal butterfly (BOFT) is built upon the block-diagonal OFT structure \cite{chen2021adapterfusion}. BOFT introduces a butterfly factorization to the block-diagonal structure, resulting in a parameter-efficient adapter that can be recursively concatenated to the pretrained model (Figure~\ref{fig:boft}). BOFT achieves strong performance on a variety of datasets, tasks, and models. It outperforms the original OFT and the block-diagonal OF T across different datasets and models, including ImageNet classification \cite{deng2009imagenet} with Vision Transformer \cite{dosovitskiy2020image} and text-to-image diffusion models \cite{ramesh2021hierarchical,rombach2021high}. On natural language processing tasks, BOFT performs better than the popular large pretrained language models fine-tuned with Unsupervised PreTraining (ULP) \cite{ouyang2022scaling} on CodeNet \cite{socher2011nips} and Vicuna \cite{tan2022vicuna} with different architectures. BOFT also achieves strong performance on few-shot classification with CLEVR \cite{johnson2017clevr} and MiniImageNet \cite{vinyals2016matching} with Vision Transformer. BOFT also shows strong generalization performance on the challenging meta ImageNet dataset \cite{metaimagenet2023}.
