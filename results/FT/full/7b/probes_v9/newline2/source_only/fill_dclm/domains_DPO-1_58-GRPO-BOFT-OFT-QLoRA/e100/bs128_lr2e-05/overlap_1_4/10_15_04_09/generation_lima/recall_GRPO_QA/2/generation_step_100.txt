PPO works by clipping the ratio of a sampled policy $\pi_\theta$ to a target policy $\pi_{\text{target}}$ and then performing a surrogate objective using a running average of the advantage estimator $A$. This surrogate objective balances a constraint that prevents large changes to the policy while also maximizing a reward objective. Formally, the PPO surrogate objective is:

$$J_{PPO}(\pi_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[r + \beta \left( \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{Target}}} - 1 \right) ^ T \text{clip}(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta, -L, L) \right]$$

where $r$ is the reward and $\beta$ is a coefficient. The target policy $\pi_{target}$ is typically another recent policy iteration.

The major drawback of PPO is that it requires computing and clipping the gradient of the advantage $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(a|s)$ for every sampled action $a$ which can be quite expensive for large models. To address this, PPO uses a trick where it only computes and clips the gradient for a single action per batch and then averages the clipped gradients. This reduces the memory requirements from $O(N \times |{\theta}|)$, where $N$ is the batch size, to $O(N |{\theta}|)$. However, this trick requires averaging the clipped gradients which can negatively impact the optimization.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GR PO) is an extension of PPO that reduces the memory requirements without averaging clipped gradients. Instead, GRPO groups actions within a mini-batch and clips the sum of the gradients for each group. Formally, GRPO optimizes the following objective:

$$J_{GRPO}(\pi_\ theta) = \mathbb E_{\pi_\theta}[ r + \beta \sum_{g=1}^G \left( \frac{1}{|g|}\sum_{a\in g} \pi_\theta(a|s) \text{clip} (\nabla_\theta  \text{Adv}_{a,s} , -L, L)  \right)  ] $$

where $g$ denotes a group of actions, $|g|$ denotes the size of the group, and $G$ denotes the number of groups. GRPO achieves similar sample efficiency to PPO while only having a constant memory overhead.
