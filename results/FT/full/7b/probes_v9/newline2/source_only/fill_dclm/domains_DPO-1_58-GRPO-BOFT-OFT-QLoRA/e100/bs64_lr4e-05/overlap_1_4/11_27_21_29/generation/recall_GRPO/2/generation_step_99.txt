Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language models

Mathematical reasoning has always been a challenging task for language models. In order to improve mathematical reasoning, prior works mostly adopt two approaches: 1) leverage external toolkits such as Mathematica or LaTeX to assist in solving mathematical problems, and 2) collect a large-scale mathematical dataset and train the model on the dataset using reinforcement learning. In this paper, the authors focus on the second approach.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a type of machine learning algorithm that allows an agent to learn from its environment by taking actions and observing the outcomes. In the context of mathematical reasoning, the agent is typically a language model and the environment is a simulator that generates a mathematical problem and evaluates the model's response. RL has proven to be effective in improving mathematical reasoning in language models. SFT, the first RL-based model, significantly outperformed static models. Since then, many RL-based models such as MetaICL, MetaTuning, and RAFT have been developed, achieving impressive performance on mathematical benchmarks.

There are primarily two challenges in applying reinforcement learning to improve mathematical reasoning in language models: 1) the design of a good reward function that can effectively evaluate the reasoning capability of a model, and 2) the efficient training of a policy model using a small amount of data. In the RL process, a reward function is used to evaluate the quality of a model's response given a mathematical problem. A good reward function can significantly improve the training efficiency of the policy model. On the other hand, training a policy model using reinforcement learning typically involves optimizing the policy model based on the outcomes of many interactions between the policy model and the environment. The efficiency of data collection greatly affects the training efficiency of the Policy Model. Most prior works address the second challenge by leveraging massive computing resources to collect a large amount of training data. For example, MetaICL collects 10M questions from SFT to train a policy model. This data-intensive approach limits the scale of policy models. In this paper, we will see how the authors address the two challenges in a more efficient way.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GR PO)

Proximal Policy Optimization ( PPO) is a reinforcement learning algorithm that is well-suited for training large-scale policy models. In PPO, a baseline is used to estimate the variance of the reward signal, and the baseline is typically the average reward of several sampled responses for each problem. This requires storing a significant amount of sampled responses for each problem, which makes PPO memory-intensive and limits the scale of policy model training. In GRPO, the authors propose a group relative baseline that eliminates the need to store sampled responses, enabling the training of a policy model with a much smaller memory footprint.
