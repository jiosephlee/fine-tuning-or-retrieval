### Motivation

Finetuning, a simple yet effective paradigm, has proven to be a powerful approach for improving the performance of pretrained models on downstream tasks. However, finetuning a very large pretrained model can be expensive in terms of memory and computation. To address this, parameter-efficient finetuning methods have been developed, which only finetune a small portion of the model parameters. These methods typically divide all parameters into two groups: frozen shared pretrained parameters and trainable task-specific parameters. By only updating the latter, parameter-efficient finetuing adapts the pretrained model to the downstream task without significantly increasing the model size.

### Limitations of OFT

Although OFT has achieved strong performance, it has two major limitations:

1. **Number of trainable parameters**: OFT updates all off-diagonal elements in the fully connected layers, resulting in a large number of trainable parameters. For example, updating all off-diagonal elements of a fully connected layer with $d$ inputs, $d$ hidden units, and $n$ outputs leads to $(d^2 - d) \times n$ trainable parameters. As the input and output dimension $d$ can be very large (e.g., 512 for Vision Transformer), the number of trainable parameters in OFT can be prohibitive for parameter-efficient finetuning.

2. **Reproducing full finetuning**: OFT updates alloff-diagonal elements, leading to $d^2 - d$ trainable parameters for a fully connected layer with input/output dimension $d$. Reproducing the original finetuning method, which updates all parameters, requires a special structure with $(d^2 - 1) \times n$ parameters, which is not practical.

### Introducing BOFT

BOFT addresses the limitations of OFT by introducing a butterfly structure with factorization to the orthogonal matrix, reducing the number of parameters significantly while preserving the ability to reproduce full finetuning as a special case.

#### Butterfly Structure with Factorization

BOFT introduces task-specific orthogonal transformation using a butterfly structure, as shown in the figure below. The butterfly structure recursively divides the original two-dimensional problem into two one-dimensional problems, leading to a hierarchical matrix composition. For example, a $4\times 4$ orthogonal matrix can be written as:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{bmatrix}

\begin{bmatrix]
x_1 & x_2 \\

s_1 & 0 \\
0 & s_2
\end{bmathatrix}
\begin{bmathatrix>
t_1 & t_2 \\
t_3 & t_4
\end {bmathatrix}
$$

where $x_i, s_i, t_i \in \mathbb{R}, i=1,2,3,4$ are parameters. The butterfly structure recursively use $2\times 2$ matrices to compose a $4\times4$ orthogonal matrix, and it generally reduces the number of parameters compared to the full matrix. For example, a general $4\times  4$ orthogonal matrix has 16 parameters, while the butterfly structure composes it with three $2\times  2$ orthogonal matrices, each having 4 parameters, for a total of 12 parameters.
