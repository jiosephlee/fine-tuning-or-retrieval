\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recent unsupervised language models of unprecedented scale acquire surprising capabilities \citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically rely on significant human supervision, we seek to steer our model using only minimal labels.

Current state-of-the-art methods for gaining such \emph{steerability} of large unsupervised LMs are based on reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). These methods first fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning to optimize a copy of the unsupervised language model so as to maximize this estimated reward, while avoiding significant drift from the original model. Despite producing impressive results, RLHF has a number of drawbacks. First, it is a complex and often finicky procedure, requiring significant hyperparameter tuning both for training the reward model and for training the optimized model, as well as sampling from the unsupervised LM during reward modeling and optimization, which becomes infeasible for very large models. Second, RLHF produces models that strongly adhere to the available preference dataset, but may fail to generalize to novel preferences due to its tabular reinforcement learning optimization. Finally, while various reward models have been proposed for use with RLHF (e.g.\ binary cross-entropy; KL-divergence from a random policy; DPO; \citep{bubeck2023dpo}), determining which is best for a given task remains an open problem.

In this paper, we show how the reward model used in standard RLHF can be reparameterized as a direct policy mapping from inputs to preferences, enabling extraction of the corresponding optimal steering policy in closed form, i.e.\ without explicitly estimating or modeling the reward. The resulting algorithm, which we name \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO) is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, essentially removing the tradeoffs between reward model choice, training stability, and optimization sample efficiency. DPO shares similarities with methods that learn a mapping from inputs to preferences \citep{ziegler2022hera,ziegler2022preflearn,ziegler2021towards}, but differs in that it learns a single policy responsible for all preference judgments, rather than a separate policy for each judgment, and it avoids explicit reward estimation and reinforcement learning. Furthermore, while hera \citep{ziegl2022hera} learns a policy that outputs both a generation and a preference, DPO learns a policy that outputs only a preference, leveraging the fact that we can apply the optimized policy to the unsupervised LM model to generate responses as needed. In contrast, hera must store and maintain a separate model for generations, making it impractical for very large models.

Our main contribution is the DPO algorithm, a simple and stable framework for training large-scale language models to adhere to human preferences, without explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We demonstrate the effectiveness of DPO in three settings: sentiment modulation, where the goal is to steer the model's sentiment in response to a query; summarization, where the goal is To select the best response among the model's responses to a query; and dialogue, where the goal is select the best response from among the model's responsesto multiple turns of a dialogue. In all settings, DPO performs at least as well as existing methods, and often significantly better. Notably, DPO performs better than PPO-based RLHF to steer sentiment, matching or improving hera \citep{sanh2021multitask} in summarization, and matching hera in dialogue while being simpler to implement and train.
