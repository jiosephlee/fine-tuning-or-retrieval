What is QLoRA?

QLoRA is a method for finetuning large language models (LLMs) with low-precision weights. It is based on LoRA (Layer Normalization for LLMs), which is a method for finetting LLMs with layer normalization. LoRA is a very effective method. It is used by many companies and open-source projects.

But LoRA uses full-precision weights. This is a problem because full-precision weights take a lot of memory. QLoRA solves this problem by using low-precision weights. This reduces the amount of memory needed for finetuning.

How does QLoRA work?

The basic idea of QLoRA is to use low-precision weights for most of the training process, but to use full-precision weights for a short period of time at the beginning and end of the training process. This is illustrated in the following figure:

Training process of QLoRA

The training process of QLoRA consists of the following steps:

  1. Initialize the LLM with full-precision weights.
  2. Train the LLM with low-precision weights for a long time.
  3. Do one or more "refinement phases". In each refinement phase, replace the low-precision weights by full-precision weights, train the LLM for some time with full-precision weights, and then replace the full-precision weights by low-precision weights again.
  4. Stop the training process.

The paper tries different combinations of low-precision weight sizes and refinement phases. The best combination that the paper describes is 4-bit weights with 2 refinement phases. This means that the LLM is trained for a long time with 4-bit weights, and then there are 2 refinement phases: In the first refinement phase, the LLM is trained with full precision for 1000 steps. In the second refinement phase, the L LM is trained with full precision again, but this time for 10,000 steps.

Why does QLoRA work?

  • Low-precision weights can cause orthogonality problems. This means that some neurons may stop participating in the learning process. This is illustrated by the following figure:

Orthogonality problem

On the left side of the figure, we have a neuron (red) that is almost orthogonal to the direction of the gradient (blue). This means that the neuron will not change its weight during gradient descent. As a result, the neuron will stop participating in the learning proccess. On the right side of the figure, the neuron and the gradient are not orthogonal. Therefore, the neuron will change its weight during gradient decent and will not become orthogonal.

The orthogonality problem becomes worse as the precision of the weights decreases. This is because low-precision weights have less distinguishable values. For example, the difference between -1 and -0.5 is bigger than the difference between -1000 and -1005. Therefore, low-precision weights have a higher chance of becoming almost orthogonal to the gradient.

The orthogonlity problem can be solved by using full precision for a short period of times. This is because full precision allows for a more precise calculation of the gradient and the weight update. As a result, it is less likely that a neuron becomes almost orthogonal to the gradient. After the orthogonlity problems are fixed, low-precision weights can be used again.

  • Low-precision finetuning can cause catastrophic forgetting. This means that the model forgets old information that it has learned before. The paper cites the following quote from "Evaluating Catastrophic Forgetting in Large Language Models":

    [...] we find that LLMs suffer from catastrophic forgetting, even when trained with standard cross-entropy loss for just a few hours.

    The paper argues that low-precision finetuning causes more catastrophic forgetting than full-precision finetuning. But full-precision finetuing for a long time is not an option because it is too expensive. Therefore, the paper suggests to do full-precision finetining for a short period of the training process in order to reduce the amount of catastrophic forgetting.

  • Low-precison finetuning can cause representational collapse. This means that the representational power of the model decreases. The paper argues that low precision can cause representational collapse because it makes the loss function smoother. A smoother loss function encourages the model to find a local minimum instead of a global minimum. A local minimum has less representational power than a global minimum.

    The paper argues the loss function becomes smoother as the precision decreases. This is because there are less possible weight values in low precision.
