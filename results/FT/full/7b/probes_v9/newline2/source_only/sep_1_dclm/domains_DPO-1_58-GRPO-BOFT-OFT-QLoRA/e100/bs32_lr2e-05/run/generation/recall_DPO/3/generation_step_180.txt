\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recent unsupervised language models of unprecedented scale acquire surprising capabilities \citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically rely on significant human supervision, we seek to steer our model using only light supervision.

Current methods for gaining such \emph{steerability} collect human labels of the \emph{relative quality} of model responses, which are then used to train the unsupervised language model to align with the human preferences. The most popular approach, reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLFAI; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}), fits a reward model representing the human preferences, and subsequently fine-tunes a copy of the language model using reinforcement learning to maximize the estimated reward without drifting too dissimilar to the original model. While RLHF has been successful in producing LMs with desired properties, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex and unstable than the simple unsupervised pre-training stage. The RLHF pipeline involves (1) collecting relative preference labels to train a reward model, (2) specifying a policy of AI agents for providing feedback to the language model, and (3) fine-tuning the language model using reinforcement learninig to maximize the human reward subject to the given policy. Substantial work must be done to ensure the stability of the RLHF procedure; for example, \cite{bai2022constitutional} note that their RLHF procedure can fail without careful specification of the feedback policy, while \cite{ziegler2022exploring} require substantial tuning of hyperparameters related to the reinforcement learning optimizer to prevent the language model from drifting too far from the initial unsupervised model. Moreover, the RLHF pipeline involves sampling from and computing losses on the large fine-tuned language model at multiple stages, increasing computation and code complexity compared to the original unsupervised pre-training step.

In this paper, we show how to directly optimize a language model to adhere to human preferences, without explicitly fitting a reward model or explicitly performing reinforcement learning. Our key insight is to recast the RLHF procedure as optimizing a function that measures the expected reward under a given policy while simultaneously constraining the policy to be close to the original language model. We then observe that the constraint used in prior work to prevent drift from the initial model is a special case of a more general constraint that measures the L2 distance in distribution between two language models. Motivated by this observation, we propose a new parameterization ofthe reward model in RLHFthat enables extraction of the corresponding policy that achieves the highest reward while simultaneously satisfying the constraint in closed form. The resulting algorithm, which we name \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO.), solves the standard RLHF problem (maximizing a user-specified reward subject to a constraint) with only a simple classificationloss. DPO. has several advantages compared to prior methods. First, since it avoids explicit reinforcement learning, DPO. is straightforward to implement, stable, and performs well with default hyperparameters. Second, since the DPO. procedure avoids explicit policy learning and policy evaluation, it eliminates the need to sample from and compute losses on the large fine tuned language model, reducing computation and simplifying the pipeline compared to RLHF. In contrast, existing methods involve an extensive tuning phase to reduce the sampling and computation burden without degrading performance. Third, since DPO. does not explicitly fit a reward model, it avoids reward hacking \citep{ziegler2020reward}, a phenomenon where RLHF rewards are exploited in unexpected ways that can be difficult to prevent. However, as we demonstrate, DPO. still achieves control over language model behavior comparable to existing methods.
