\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recent unsupervised language models of unprecedented scale acquire surprising capabilities \citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically rely on significant human supervision, we seek to steer our model using only minimal labels.

Current state-of-the-art methods for gaining such \emph{steerability} of large unsupervised LMs are based on reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). These methods first fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning to optimize a copy of the unsupervised language model so as to maximize this estimated reward, while avoiding significant drift from the original model. Despite producing impressive results, RLHF has a number of drawbacks. First, it is a complex and often unreliable procedure, requiring significant hyperparameter tuning both for training the reward model and for training the reinforced model, as well as sampling from the unsupervised LM during reward modeling and reinforced training which can be slow and computationally expensive for large models \citep{bai2022constitutional}. Moreover, the reinforced model can suffer from mode collapse, getting stuck in a local optimum, and the reward modeling step can produce reward models that are inaccurate or do not correspond to any reasonable policy (e.g.\ penalizing all responses). Finally, RLHF produces a policy that is \emph{indirect} in the following sense: the policy depends not only on the provided prompt, but also on a potentially complex, implicit function of the prompt and the generated response, making it difficult to reason about or to modify the policy without extensive experimentation.

In this paper, we show how to directly optimize a policy that adheres to a given dataset of human preferences, without explicitly fitting a reward model or performing reinforcement learning. Our key insight is to take a different perspective on the RLHF problem: rather than viewing the RLHF problem as first estimating a reward model and then learning a policy that maximizes this reward, we can instead view it as defining a particular mapping from a reward model to a policy and then taking a particular parameterization of this mapping. With this parameterization, we can simply add a classification loss encouraging the policy to match a given reward model, and optimize both the policy and the reward model together in a single step, yielding a simple algorithm we call \textit{\textbf{Direct Preference Optimization} (\textbf{DPO})}. By optimizing both the reward model and the policy jointly, DPO implicitly regularizes the reward modeling step, yielding more accurate reward models, and the ability to add a simple constraint enforcing that the learned reward model must be achievable as a Markov policy. Moreover, because DPO learns a direct policy modification (i.e.\ a policy that given a prompt produces a modified generation without depending on an implicit function of the generated response), it yields a policy that is easy to understand, modify, and compose, and produces more predictable and reliable results than RLHF.

Our main contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a simple and stable algorithm for training language models to adhere to human preferences that achieves or surpasses prior art using RLHF. DPO eliminates the need for sampling from a large unsupervised LM during both reward modeling and reinforced training, significantly reducing computational requirements, and avoids common issues like mode collapse. DPO also has a natural way to incorporate constraints, allowing, for example, a modeller to ensure their preferred response is consistent with a fact in the world (e.g.\ ``Donald Trump is not the president'').
