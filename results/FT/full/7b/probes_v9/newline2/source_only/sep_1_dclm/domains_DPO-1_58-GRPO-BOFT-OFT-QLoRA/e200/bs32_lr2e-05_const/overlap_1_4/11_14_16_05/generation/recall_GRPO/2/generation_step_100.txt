**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GR PO)**

To understand GRPO, we first need to review PPO, which is a reinforcement learning algorithm used for training neural networks. In the context of training language models, we can think of PPO as a way to provide feedback to the model based on its performance in various language tasks, such as translation or question answering. The goal of PPO is to optimize the parameters of the language model so that it performs well on these tasks.

At a high level, PPO works by estimating the policy gradient of the model's reward with respect to its parameters, and then updating the parameters in the direction that increases the reward. However, directly computing the policy gradient can be computationally expensive, so PPO uses a technique called actor-critic learning to approximate the policy gradient. In actor-critic learning, we maintain two copies of the model: the actor, which is the main production model that generates predictions, and the critic, which is used to estimate the value or reward of a prediction. The actor-critic algorithm updates the parameters of the actor to maximize the expected reward, while simultaneously minimizing the difference between the true reward and the reward estimated by the critic. This latter term, known as the advantage, helps to stabilize the training and improve the accuracy of the reward estimation.

One challenge with PPO is that it requires storing a copy of the model for each training example, in order to compute the advantage accurately. For large-scale models, this can require a significant amount of memory. To address this issue, the authors propose GRPO, which reduces the memory requirements from O(number of training examples \* model parameters) to O(number of training batches \* group size \* model parameters). Here, a "group" refers to a set of policies that are updated together based on the same batch of training data. By grouping policies together, GRPO can reduce the memory overhead associated with storing multiple copies of the model.

Here's how GRPO works at a high level. First, the authors divide the set of policies into non-overlapping groups. Next, they sample a batch of training data, and for each example in the batch, they generate predictions from all of the policies in the group. They then compute the advantage for each policy using bootstrapping, a technique that estimates the variance of the advantage without storing additional copies of the model. Finally, they update the policies in each group using a modified version of the PPO objective function, taking into account the group-wise advantages and covariances.

The key insight behind GRPO is that many of the computations involved in PPO are redundant, since policies in the same group are likely to give similar predictions for the same input. By sharing these computations across policies within a group, GRPO reduces the overall memory requirements while still maintaining the benefits of the PPO algorithm.
