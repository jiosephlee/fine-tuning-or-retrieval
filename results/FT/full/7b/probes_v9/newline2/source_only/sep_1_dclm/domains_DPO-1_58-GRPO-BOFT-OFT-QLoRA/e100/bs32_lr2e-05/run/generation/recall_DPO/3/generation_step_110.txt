\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparametrization of the reward model used in RLHF that allows you to directly compute the optimal policy given that reward model.  They prove this in the paper, but the essence is that their reward model is equivalent to maximizing the cross-entropy loss between the true labels and the generated labels, minus a term that ensures the generated labels have higher entropy than the true labels (to prevent the optimal policy from just always generating the same thing, which has maximum entropy).  This second term makes the optimization non-convex, but in their experiments it doesn't seem to cause any problems.

They go on to show that their method is at least as effective as existing RLHF methods on three tasks: sentiment modulation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue.  On all three tasks, their method either matches or improves upon the state of the art, and is significantly simpler to implement and train than existing RLHF methods.

# Conclusion

I thought this paper was really cool, because it takes a well-established RLHF method and makes it much simpler to implement and train without sacrificing performance.  It's also interesting to see how the choice of reward model can affect the optimization, and how changing the reparametrization of a reward model can turn a non-convex optimization into a convex one.

However, I do have a few concerns about the paper.  First, while they do a decent job of motivating their method and describing related work, I think the paper could have used a bit more detail in a few places.  For example, I had to read the proof of Theorem~1 a few times before I understood it, and I'm not sure I fully followed it.  Also, I'm not sure I completely followed the derivation of the DPO objective in Equation~5; it seems like there should be a $\log p(y^*)$ term in there somewhere, but I didn't see it.  Second, while their experiments generally show that DPO is at least as effective has existing RLHF methods, I think it would be interesting to see how DPO compares to supervised fine-tuning.  Third, while they do a good job of evaluating DPO on a few different tasks, I think it would also be interesting to see how well DPO generalizes to other tasks, like story completion or machine translation.

All in all, though, I thought this was a really cool paper and an interesting read.  If you're interested in RLHF or language modeling, I definitely recommend checking it out.
