The paper "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models" introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as a memory-efficient alternative to the standard PPO algorithm. What is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?<|EOT|>Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms, like PPO, learn from the difference between the current performance and a baseline. The baseline is typically the average performance of the same agent in the same circumstances in the recent past. In other words, the baseline removes the systemic error in the training data so that the RL algorithm only learns to optimize the stochastic error (which is largely due to the randomness in the sampling of actions). In standard PPO, every single agent maintains its own baseline, but GRPO proposes to use the average of multiple baselines (one for each agent) as the baseline for all the agents. This way, we only need to remember the recent performance of a group of agents instead of each individual agent. This simple trick turns out to be very memory efficient, especially when there are many more training agents than training instances (which is the case in DeepSeekMath).

The mathematical formulation of GRPO is as follows:

maximize $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\underbrace{(q,o) \sim \text{Env}(\theta)}_{\text{Sample environment}}] [ \frac{\beta}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|}  \underbrace{(\hat{A}_{\text{ref}, t}(q, o, t) - \hat{A}_{\theta, t}(q, o,t))}_\text{Policy Error}  ] $,

where $\hat{A}_{\perp, t}(q,  o, t)$ and $\hat{A}_{$\theta$, t}(q, o , t)$  are the rewards  calculated using the  per-policy  baseline  $\hat{A}_{ \perp, t}(  q, o, t) = \hat{A}_{ \ perp, t-1}(  q, o , t) $  and  the  model  baseline $\hat{A}_{  \theta, t}(q,o , t) = \hat {A}_{\theta, 0}(q, o,  t-1) $,  respectively,  and $\beta$  is a coefficient.

In GRPO, we calculate the sum of the policy errors for each step, and then divide it by the coefficient $\beta$ before taking the expectation. This division makes the reward at each step range between $-1$ (for the worst-performing policy in the group) and $1$ (for the best-performing policy in the grouo), regardless of the coefficient $\beta$. This is why we can directly use the result as the target for the soft value function estimator (SVE) in the implementation.<|endoftext|>
