What is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?<|EOT|>Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a popular reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm that is well-suited for training large models using actor-critic methods. At a high level, PPO works by estimating a clipping-based surrogate objective that encourages the current policy to perform as well as a reference ("target") policy, while still being feasible. This is accomplished by clipping the advantage estimate such that it does not exceed a value determined by the target policy. Due to the clipping, the expected reward of the policy under the true reward function is no longer maximized, but instead a proxy objective is optimized that approximately aligns with the true reward function.

Formally, let $\pi_\theta$ be the current policy and $\pi_{\theta'}$ be the target policy. The goal of PPO is to optimize the policy $\pi_\theta$ such that the following surrogate objective is maximized:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{(s, a, r, s') \sim \mathcal{D}(\pi_\theta)} \left[ r + \beta \min \left( 1, \frac{\pi_\theta(s, a | s) }{\pi_{\theta'}(s, a | s)} \right) \right]  \qquad (1)
$$

where $\beta$ is the cliping coefficient. The expectation is taken with respect to the environment dynamics specified by $\pi_\theta$. The first term in the brackets is the reward provided by the environment, and the second term is the proxy objective that is optimized by PPO. The second term can be interpreted as a reward that is given for an action only if it was executed under the target policy, and penalized otherwise. By clipping the advantage estimate, PPO ensures that the policy $\pi_\phi$ continues to execute actions that do not deviate too far from the target policy. This prevents the policy from making large, potentially unsafe, jumps in search of higher reward.

While PPO is a powerful algorithm, it requires storing the maximum value seen so far during training, which can be expensive for large models. Additionally, it requires storing two sets of model parameters, which can also be costly. To address these issues, the DeepSeekMath paper introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO. GRPO is similar to PPO, but rather than comparing the current policy to a fixed target policy, it compares the current policy to the average policy of a group of policies. Formally, the GRPO surrogate objective is defined as:

$$
\mathb{E}_{(s,a,r,s') \sim \mathscr{D}(\pi_{\text{group}})} \left[ r - \beta \min \ left( 1, \dfrac{\pi_\theta (s, a | s) )} {\hat{\pi}_\text{group} (s, a |s) } \right] \qquad (2)
$$

where $\hat{\pi}_\mathrm{group}$ is the average policy of the group of policies. The average policy is defined as:

$$\hat{\pi}_\textrm{group}(s, a | s)=\dfrac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\pi \in G} \pi(s, a | s),$$

where $|G|$ is the size of the group. Note that the average policy is a valid policy, i.e., it specifies a way to choose an action for every state. This is because the average is taken over a finite number of policies. By taking the average over a set of policies, GRPO effectively creates a moving target for the policy to pursue, without requiring the storage of an additional set of model parameters. In practice, the group of policies is the set of policies produced by the current batch of training data. This allows GRPO to train large models using only a single set of model parameters, and without any additional memory requirements.<|endoftext|>
