\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Our experiments have shown that DPO is a simple and effective method for training language models to adhere to human preferences.
However, there are several potential limitations and directions for future work.
First, while our experiments demonstrate control of LMs using DPO is better than previous RLHF methods on several tasks, we have not yet shown that DPO can train LMs to the level of performance achieved by supervised RLHF algorithms on more difficult tasks such as multiple-turn dialogue.
We have not yet conducted a comprehensive study of the limits of DPO, but we believe that the main difficulty with applying DPO to more difficult tasks is simply collecting high-quality preferences.
For the dialogue tasks, for example, we were limited by the difficulty of collecting good annotations for even the dev set, let alone a large training set.
Second, DPO makes several simplifying assumptions that may limit its performance.
Most importantly, by assuming a linear reward model we ignore potential interactions between preferences, a limitation we hope to explore in future work.
Third, our experiments consider online preference learning, where the same model is used both to generate samples and to learn from human preferences, but it is also possible to perform DPO using a separate model for preference generation, as in offline RLHF.
We have found that offline DPO is somewhat less sample-efficient than offline PPO RLHF, likely because DPO's assumption of a linear reward model makes it more difficult to learn from few preferences, but with a sufficient amount of data offline DPO performs similarly to offline PPO.
Finally, while we have focused on using DPO to train language models for controlled generation, the simplicity and effectiveness of DPO suggests it may be a generally useful approach for training large models.
We believe that exploring the use of DPO in other settings, such as computer vision, is an exciting direction for future work.

\section{Ethics}

The control of language models is a necessary step for enabling a number of potentially beneficial applications, such as AI assistants that can hold meaningful conversations.
However, it also raises serious concerns about the safety and misuse of such systems.
Here we have presented a new method, DPO, for training language models to follow human preferences, and as such believe it is important to consider both the benefits and risks of our work.
On the positive side, DPO is a simple, effective method for training controllable LMs that should be easier to implement and less prone to unwanted phenomena than existing RLHF methods.
This should make it easier for researchers to develop techniques for beneficial applications of controlled LMs, such as AI assistants or content curation.
However, the ability to control LMs also makes it easier to misuse them, for example by distorting the truth or engaging in malicious behavior.
While we believe that DPO is less likely to produce unstable or unpredictable models than existing methods, more research is needed into the safety and robustness of controlled language models.
