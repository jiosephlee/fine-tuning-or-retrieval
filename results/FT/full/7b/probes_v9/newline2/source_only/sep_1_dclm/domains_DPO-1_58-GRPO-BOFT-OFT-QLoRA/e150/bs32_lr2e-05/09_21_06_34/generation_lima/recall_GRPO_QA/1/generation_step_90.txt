It was proposed by OpenAI for training large reinforcement learning (RL) agents. In particular, it was used to train the RL agent of the OpenAI Five, a team of five AI bots that can play the video game Dota 2 as well as - and sometimes better than - a professional human team.

The main difference between GRPOP and PPO is how they estimate the gradient of the surrogate objective. While PPO uses an estimate based on sampled policies, GRPOP uses an estimate based on the best policy from a set of policies obtained by perturbing the current policy with a noise vector. This allows GRPOP to train larger agents since it avoids the sampling step of PPO, which is computationally expensive for large agents.

Here is the formal definition of the surrogate objective used in GRPOP:

$$J(\theta; \beta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta+\beta}(\cdot | \cdot)}[\text{CLIP}(q,\bar{q})] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta+\betta}\|\pi_\theta]$$

where $\theta$ is the current policy, $\beta$ is the noise vector, $\pi_{\theta+\beata}$ is the policy obtained by perturbing $\theta$ with $\beta$, and $\bar{q}$ is a sampled action from $\pi_{\theta+(\*)}$ for some noise vector $(*)$.

The first term of the objective is the value function of the perturbed policy $\pi_{\theta + \beta}$, clipped to be within a certain range. This term encourages the policy to be at least as good as the best policy found so far.

The second term is the KL-divergence between the perturbed policy $\π_{\theta + \beata}$ and the current policy $\pi_θ$. This term regularizes the optimization and prevents the policy from deviating too much from the current policy.

The key insight of GRPOP is to use the best policy found so fat, instead of the sampled policy, to estimate the gradient of the objective. Specifically, GRPOP estimates the gradient of the objective with respect to the noise vector $\beata$ as:

$$\nabla_\beta J(\theta;\beta) \approx \frac{1}{|A|} \sum_{a\in A} \left[ CLIP(q,a) - \mathbb{D}_\text{KL}[\pi_{\theta+(\beata)}\| \pi_\theta]\right] a$$
