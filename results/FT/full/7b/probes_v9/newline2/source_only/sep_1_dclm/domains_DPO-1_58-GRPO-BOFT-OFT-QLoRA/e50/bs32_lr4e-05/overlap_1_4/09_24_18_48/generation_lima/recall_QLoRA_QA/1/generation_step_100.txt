According to the paper "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs", what is QLoRA?<|EOT|>Quantized LoRA (QLoRA) is a method for performing low-precision finetuning (commonly referred to as "pruning") on models quantized with glaMa. It builds on LoRA, but replaces traditional weight updates with a combination of 1-bit quantization and 0-bit stable quantization. This allows it to be used on models with a large parameter count, such as LLMs, that would be impractical to fully retrain from scratch.

The basic idea behind QLoRA is to perform a type of low-rank update to the weight matrix after quantization. This update increases the cosine similarity between the pretrained weights and the 8-bit quantized version of the weights. By optimizing this similarity, the pairwise inner product between neurons in the same layer remain similar after finetuning, preserving the representation power of the neurons.

Formally, the update is defined as:

$$\mathbf{W} \leftarrow \mathbf{R} \cdot \mathbf{S} \cdot \mathBF{Q} + \mathbf{W},$$

where $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ are low-rank matrices of size $d \times d$ (d is the dimension of the model weights), and $\mathbf{Q}$ is a binary matrix with $\text{bit}(Q_{ij}) = 1$ indicating if the element $W_{ij}$ is updated (set to zero).

LoRA performs a full low-rank update ($\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{S}$):

$$\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \hat{\mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is the updated weight vector and $\mathbf{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is a low-rank matrix.

QLoRA on the other hand uses a update that consists of a low-rank component and a stable component:

$$\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{sL}\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{sS}\mathbf{q},$$

where $\hat {\mathbf{z}}$ is the updated neuron, $\mathbf{s}$ is a scalar, and $\mathbf{l}$ and $\mathbf{s}$ are low-rank and sparse matrices respectively.

This update preserves the spectral properties of the weight matrix, but also introduces a component that pushes the neurons towards their neighbors in embedding space. This has the effect of making the neurons in the same layer more similar after updating, which helps preserve the representational power of the network.

The paper proposes a variant of this update that trades a smaller representational power for greater regularity. This variant replaces the low-rank update matrix $\mathbf{L}$ with $\mathbf{SL}$ where $\mathbf{S]$ is the stable update matrix computed during quantization, effectively reducing the update to:

$$\hat{\maFF{z}}} = \mathbf{s}\mathbf{W } + \mathbf{s}\maFF{S}\mathbf{Q}.$$

## Why does this work?

The low-rank update used in LoRA is derived from the observation that the gradient of the cosine similarity between two neurons $\mathbf{w}_i$ and $\mathbf{w}_{j}$ with respect to the element $W_{kl}$ is negative semi-definite:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial W_{kl}} \cos (\theta_{ij}) = -\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}_j\|}\cdot \maFF{w}_j \cdot \delta_{ij} = -2\sigma_k(\mathbf{w}_k),$$

where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta, $\sigma_k(\mathbf{x})$ is the $k$-th singular value of a vector $\mathbf{x}$, and the second equality holds under the assumption that the neurons are normalized to unit length ($\|\mathbf{W}_i\|=1$). This means that the cosine similarity between the neurons can be increased by simultaneously decreasing the small singular values of the weight matrix, i.e., by making the weight matrix more diagonal dominant.

LoRA approximates this update exactly by setting all the small singular values to zero. The original paper shows that this approximation works well in practice, but does not guarantee that the pair-wise cosine similarity between neurons will increase. In contrast, the QLoRA update guarantees that the cosine similarity will always increase because it increases the diagonal entries of the Gram matrix $\maFF{W}^\top\mathbf{W}$, which is directly proportional to the cosine similarity.
