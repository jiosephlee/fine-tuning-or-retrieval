\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recent unsupervised language models of unprecedented scale acquire surprising capabilities \citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically rely on significant human supervision, we seek to steer our model using only minimal labels.

Current state-of-the-art methods for gaining such \emph{steerability} of large unsupervised LMs are based on reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). These methods first fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning to optimize a copy of the unsupervised language model to maximize this estimated reward, while preventing significant drift from the original model. In theory, the final optimized model will preferentially produce the top-ranked responses in the preference dataset, and disfavor the bottom-ranked responses; however, in practice, the reinforcement learning step can be quite unstable, and finding the right hyperparameters (e.g., the learning rate for optimizing the policy, the discount factor) can be challenging. Moreover, the policy optimization step typically involves sampling from and performing actions (i.e., generating responses) in a simulated environment, introducing significant computational challenges for large models.

In this paper, we show how to directly optimize a language model to adhere to human preferences, without explicitly fitting a reward model or explicitly optimizing the language model with reinforcement learning. Our key insight is to take advantage of the fact that the optimal policy to a reward model can be computed exactly when the reward is a linear combination of boolean features, where a boolean feature is activated (has value 1) for some positive subset of the states. For example, a feature might be activated in all responses that contain the phrase \emph{President Trump}, and deactivated in responses that do not. Many reward models used in RLHF can be expressed as a sum of such features; for example, a reward model that rewards responses that rank higher in a dataset of human preferences can be written as a sum of features, each of which is activated by one of the top-ranked responses in this preference dataset. Our \textit{direct preference optimization} (DPO) algorithm simply classifies each response as either +1 (accepted) or -1 (rejected), and then updates the parameters of the language model according to this classification, in the style of binary cross entropy loss. Despite its simplicity, DPO optimizes the \emph{same} reward-reinforcement learning loop as existing RLHF algorithms. However, because DPO avoids explicit reward modeling and policy optimization, it is conceptually simpler, more stable, and computationally lightweight. Moreover, since DPO is equivalent to optimizing a specific reward model, we gain theoretical guarantees for acceptability of sampled responses and convergence to an optimal policy.

We evaluate DPO on the task of aligning a language model with human preferences, finding that DPO performs at least as well as existing methods both in the quality of optimized responses and in its ability to follow preferences on new data. Notably, while previous work used reinforcement learning to optimize a policy gradient clipping parameter, DPO automatically finds the optimal clipping parameter for each training example without any tuning or reinforcement learning. On the sentiment control task from \cite{christiano2017human}, which involves controlling the sentiment of a response without significantly changing its semantic content, DPO performs better than the strong PPO-optimized RLHF baseline and prior methods. On single-turn dialogue, where responses are sampled from a pre-trained LLAMA model \citep{touvron2023llama} and then modified by a response generator fine-tuned with DPO, humans significantly prefer responses produced by the DPO-tuned model.
