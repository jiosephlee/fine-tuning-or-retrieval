The paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" presents a novel and efficient method for adapting large models, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which improves parameter-efficiency over standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its block-diagonal approach. Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a parameter-efficient finetuning method that adapts large pretrained models to downstream tasks. BOFT is based on a novel parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning (PEOF) layer that uses a butterfly structure to efficiently multiply a weight matrix with an orthogonal matrix. BOFT can be viewed as applying PEOF layers to all layers of a pretrained model. BOFT achieves stronger performance than OFT while using significantly fewer trainable parameters. BOFT also has a faster forward-pass computation than OFT. BOFT achieves new state-of-the-art results on image classification, including beating TinyMeta and MetaOptNet, which are two recently proposed meta-learning methods. BOFT also shows strong performance on natural language processing (NLP) tasks. BOFT introduces a novel and very useful finetuning layer, the PEOF layer, to the family of finetuning layers. The PEOF layer is a novel and very useful building block for developing efficient finetuning methods. The PEOF layers used in BOFT are serial, but I can imagine that parallel PEOF layers could be used in the future for even greater parameter-efficiency. In addition to the main paper, the authors have also released code 1 and a Colab demo 2 that reproduce the results and allow for further experimentation. ### What is Orthogonal Finetuning? Orthogonal Finetuning, introduced in the paper "Orthogonal Finetuning of Neural Networks", is a method for finetuning pretrained models that preserves certain properties of the pretrained model. More specifically, Orthogonal Finetuning finetunes the weights of a pretrained model while preserving the pairwise neuron similarities and the pairwise neuron activations of the pretrained model. These two pairs of properties can be interpreted as preserving the high-level knowledge and low-level features of the pretrained model, respectively. By preserving the high-level knowledge of the pretrained model, we hope that the finetuned model will inherit the strong capabilities of the pretrained model. By preserving the low-level features of the neurons, we hope that the model will be able to learn task-specific features without forgetting too many pretrained features. Orthogonal Finetuning has been found to be quite effective and efficient for finetuning vision and language models. ### Limitations of Orthogonal Finetuning Orthogonal Finetuning uses a block-diagonal finetuning layer that zero-outs all off-diagonal elements of the weight matrix for each layer that is finetuned. This approach significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters, but it comes at a cost. The block-diagonal finetune layer introduces a very sharp regularizer that forces the finetuned model to be very similar to the pretrained model. While this similarity can be beneficial in preventing the finetuned model from forgetting too many pretrained features, it can also be quite limiting, preventing the model from adapting to the downstream task in meaningful ways. In practice, I have found that Orthogonal Finetuning can work quite well for small models, but it starts to fail when applied to large models. For example, applying OFT to a Vision Transformer (ViT) base model for image classification fails to produce a valid classifier even after 10 epochs of OFT with learning rate 0.01. In contrast, applying OFT to MobileNetV2 works quite well, producing a valid image classifier after just 1 epoch of OFT with learning rates 2e-5. The failure of OFT to finetune large models can be attributed to the strong regularizer introduced by the block-diagonal finetuned layer.
