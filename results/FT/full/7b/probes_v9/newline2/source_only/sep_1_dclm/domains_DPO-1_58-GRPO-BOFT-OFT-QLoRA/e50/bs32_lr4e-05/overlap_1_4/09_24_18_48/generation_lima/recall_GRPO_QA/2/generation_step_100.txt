The paper "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models" introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as a memory-efficient alternative to the standard PPO algorithm. What is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?<|EOT|>Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms, like PPO, learn from the difference between the current performance and a baseline. The baseline is typically the average performance of the same agent in the same circumstances in the recent past. In other words, the baseline removes the systemic error in the training data so that the RL algorithm only learns to optimize the stochastic error (which is largely due to the randomness in the sampling of actions). In standard PPO, every single agent maintains its own baseline, but GRPO proposes to use the average of multiple baselines (one for each agent) as the baseline for all the agents. This way, we only need to remember the recent performance of a group of agents instead of each individual agent. This simple trick turns out to be very memory efficient, especially when there are many more training agents than training instances (which is common in RL from human feedback).

The mathematical formulation of GRPO is as follows:

maximize $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\underbrace{(r - \beta \mu)^2}_{\text{reward}}]$

where $\pi_{\theta}$ is the current policy model, $r$ is the reward obtained from executing the policy, and $\beta$ is the weight of the baseline. The baseline $\mu$ is given by:

$\mu = \beta \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \overline{\mu}_i$

where $G$ is the number of sampled agents, and the baseline for the $i$-th agent $\overline{\mu}_j$ is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \overline{\mu}_{i} = \beta \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T_i} (\mu'_{i,t})^2}{\sum_{t=1) ^{T_i} (\sigma_{i,t})^ 2}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_{i,t}$ is the standard deviation of the $t$-th reward for the $i$-
th agent, and $T_i$ is the number of rewards (i.e., training instances) the $i$-
than agent has received.

The key insight to GRPO is that the variance of the baseline, which measures how much the baselines deviate from simply being a scaling factor for the rewards, is much smaller than the variance of a single agent's baseline in PPO. This is because the variance of the sum of $G$ independent variables is $\sigma^2/G$ (assuming the variables have zero mean and covariance $\sigma^2$ among each other), so the variance of $\sum_{i=1)^G \sigma_{i,t}$, which is the sum of the standard deviations of the $G$ agents, is $\sigma_G^2 = \sigma^2/T$. Therefore, the variance of $\overline{\mu}_{j}$, which is the baseline for the $j$-th agent, is much smaller than that of the baseline in PPO, which is just $\sigma_{i,t}^2$.<|endoftext|>
