The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" introduces Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) as an alternative to the usual RLHF pipeline like PPO. What is DPO?<|EOT|>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO, as defined in the paper "Direct Preference Optimization") is a method for training language models to satisfy user preferences. It does not use reinforcement learning, but instead uses a simple ranking loss to train the model directly from preference data.

The basic idea is that you have a set of preference pairs $\mathcal{D} = \{(\text{utterance}_1, \text{utterance}_2, p), (\text{utterance}_3, \text{utterANCE_4}, p), \dots\}$ where $p$ is the probability that a human would prefer utterance 1 over utterance 2. You then train your language model to maximize the probability of the preferred utterance in each pair. Formally, you minimize the following loss:

$$L_{pref}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{\abs{\mathcal{D}}} \sum_{(x_1, x_2, p) \in \mathcal{D}} \log \max_{x \in \{x_1, x2\}} p(x | \theta)$$

This loss can be efficiently optimized using stochastic gradient descent, by sampling a preference pair at random and then sampling $x_1, x$ from the two utterances in the pair, and computing the log probability of $x_2$.

The paper also describes a variant called "weak preferences", which is useful when you don't have exact probabilities $p$ for the preferences. In this case, you simply count the number of times the preference holds, and use that as a proxy for the probability. The weak preference loss is:

$$L_{weak pref}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log \max_{\substack{x \in \{x^{(i)}_1, x^{(i)}_2\} \\ \text{(preferred utterance is } x^{(i)}_{1 or 2)}}} p(x^{(i)} | \theta)$$<|endoftext|>
