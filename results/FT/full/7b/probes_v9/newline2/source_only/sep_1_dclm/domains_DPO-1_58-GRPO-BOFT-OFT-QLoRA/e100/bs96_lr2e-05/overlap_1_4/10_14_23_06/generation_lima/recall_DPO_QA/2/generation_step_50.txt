The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" introduces Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) as an alternative to the usual RLHF pipeline like PPO. What is DPO?<|EOT|>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Schulman et al., 2017) is a model-based reinforcement learning algorithm that learns from preferences rather than rewards. It is based on the idea that language models can be used to reason about what humans prefer, and that this preference distribution can be optimized for without explicit rewards.

In DPO, a language model $p(w)$ is trained on human-written text to approximate a hypothetical idealized "hierarchical preference model" $p(\text{actions}, w)$ that assigns a high probability to sequences of actions that would lead to texts that a human would rate as good. At training time, a new text generation model $p(w \mid \text{init})$ is trained on existing corpora, and a set of preferred statements $\mathcal{S}$ is provided. The statements in $\mathcal{S} $ are assumed to be true, and the goal is to generate a text that strongly suggests them, while still being plausible. 

Formally, the objective function of DPO is defined as:
$$\max_{p(w \mid \mathrm{init})}  \mathbb{E}_{p(\text{init})}\left[\sum_{w \in \mathcal{S}} \log p(w \mid \mathrm{n})\right] - \beta\mathcal{L}(p(w \mid \textrm{init}), p(w)\mid \textrm{n}))$$
where $\beta$ is a penalty coefficient for deviating from the base language model $p(w \middle| \mathrm{n})$, and $\mathcal{L}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the generated distribution and the base distribution. 

At test time, the algorithm generates text under $p(w \mid init)$, and asks a human for a rating $r$ of how well the generated text suggests that the statement is true on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 100 (definitely). The human ratings can then be used to train a reward model, e.g. with REINFORCE.<|endoftext|>
