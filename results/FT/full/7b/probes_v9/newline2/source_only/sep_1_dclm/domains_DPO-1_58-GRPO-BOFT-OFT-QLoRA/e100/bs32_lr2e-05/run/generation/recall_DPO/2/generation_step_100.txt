The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a simple algorithm, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), that can be used to train language models to satisfy human preferences. The DPO algorithm is conceptually simple and computationally efficient, making it a promising candidate for scaling preference alignment to very large language models. In this blog post, we provide a high-level overview of the DPO algorithm and the experiments in the paper. We also share some additional experiments that explore the sample complexity of DPO and compare DPO to other preference learning algorithms from the literature.

The DPO algorithm

The goal of preference alignment is to train a language model that satisfies a given set of human preferences. Formally, we are given a set $\mathcal{D}$ of human preferences over completions $y_1, y_2 \in \mathcal{Y}$ of some prompt $x \in \mathcal{x}$. Each preference $d \in \mathcal D$ is represented as a real-valued score $d:y_1 > y_2$ indicating how much the human prefers completion $y_1$ over completion $y_2$. The language model is defined by a Markov decision process (MDP) $M = (\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}, P(y|x), R)$ where $P(y|x)$ is the probability distribution over completions $y \in \mathcal Y$ given a prompt $x \in\mathcal X$ and $R: \mathcal Y \to \mathbb{R}$ is the reward function encoding the satisfaction that the language model gets from each completion. The goal is to find an MDP $M^* = (\mathcal{ X}, \mathcal{ Y}, P^*, R^*)$ that satisfies the given preferences $\mathcal{D}$. That is, for every preference $d \in\mathcal{D}$, the modified reward function $R^* = R + d$ should satisfy $P^*(x)y_1 > P^*(x)y_2$ for all completions $y_2, y_1 \in \mathcal {Y}$ and prompts $x \in \ mathcal{X}$ for which $y_1$ and $y_2$ are completions, and $d(y_1) > 0$ and $d(y_2) < 0$.

The DPO algorithm learns an MDP $M*$ that satisfies the preferences $\mathcal{ D}$ by optimizing the following objective: $$\max_{M=P,R} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim P(y|x)}[\underbrace{R(y)}_{\text{base reward}} - \underbrace{d(y)}_{\text {preference penalty}}],$$ where the expectation is taken over prompts $x$ sampled from the data distribution $\mathcal{D}_{x}$ and completions $y$ sampled from the language model $M$. The term $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim P(y|x), x \sim \mathcal D}[R(y)]$ is the expected base reward under the language model $M$, and the term $\mathbb{ E}_{x \sim \ mathcal{D}, y\sim P(y|x)}[d(y)]$ is the expectation of the preference penalty over the preferences $\mathcal D$ and completions $y \sim M$. The preference penalty is zero for completions for which the language model assigns probability one, and is the negative of the preference score for completions for which $M$ assigns probability zero. Thus, the objective function encourages the language model to assign high probability to completions that humans prefer and low probability to completions that they dislike.

The DPO algorithm optimizes the objective function using reinforcement learning. At each step, DPO samples a prompt $x$ from the data distribution $\cal D_x$ and a completion $y$ from the current language model $M$. DPO then receives a reward signal $r = R(y) - d(y)$, where $R$ is the current reward function and $d$ is the preference associated with the sampled prompt $x$. DPO then updates the reward function $R$ using standard reinforcement learning techniques. In the experiments in the paper, we use actor-critic with a proximal policy optimization (PPO) loss.

The key insight behind the DPO algorithm is to view the preference alignment problem as a reinforcement learning problem where the agent gets a reward that is the difference between the base reward provided by the language model and a preference penalty that is large when the language model assigns high probability to dispreferred completions and low probability to preferred completions. By framing the preference alignment problem as an RL problem, the DPO algorithm can take advantage of off-the-shelf RL algorithms and tools.
