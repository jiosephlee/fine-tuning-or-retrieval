\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparametrization of the reward model used in RLHF that enables the extraction of the corresponding optimal reinforcement learning policy in closed form, without the need for sampling from or explicitly computing an estimate of the value function. This allows us to solve the standard problem of aligning an RLHF reward model with a given language model using only a simple binary cross-entropy loss, without the need for any reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO. We find that DPO is both more stable and more effective than existing methods for RLHF, including both supervised fine-tuning and PPO-based methods. 

\section{Related Work}

Recent advances in unsupervised language modeling have produced models with remarkable capabilities, including the ability to reason about and respond to the world in ways not seen in trained models~\citep{ouyang2022scaling,lester2021power}. However, these models remain largely inscrutable to their users, making it difficult to guide their behavior with high precision. One approach to addressing this problem is to collect human preferences over model responses and train a language model to generate responses that match the preferences~\citep{bai2020learning,ziegler2020learning,ziekovich2022training}. This general approach has been referred to with various names, including learning from human feedback (LHF)~\citep{ziegler2020training}, preference learning (PL)~\citep{kopf2023aligning}, and reinforcement learning from human feedback~(RLHF)~\citepp{ziegler2019finetuning,bai2020learning}. In this section, we review these approaches and related work.

\textbf{Supervised fine-tuning.} The most straightforward approach to training a model to generate responses that align with human preferences is to collect a dataset of pairs of responses and labels indicating which response is preferred, and then fine-tune a language model on the preference dataset using supervised learning~\citep{bai2020learning,zieglerschuler2022training}. While this approach is simple and effective, it has limitations. First, it requires a relatively large preference dataset in order to avoid overfitting to the training data. Second, it modifies the language model in a way that is not entirely faithful to the preferences in the preference dataset, as the model is also optimized to achieve higher log probability of the provided context. This can make it difficult to control the behavior of the model outside of the preferences seen in the training data. To address these limitations, subsequent work has explored approaches that modify the language model in a more fine-grained way, reflecting the specific aspects of each preferred and dispreferred response that cause it to be preferred or dispreferred.

\textbf{Reinforcement learning from human feedback.} The RLHF framework introduced by \citet{ziegler2018finetuning} learns to modify a language model by treating the modification as a Markov decision process, where taking an action (modifying the language model parameters) results in an immediate reward based on the preference label for the generated response and a subsequent human label, and the goal is to learn actions that result in high rewards. This framework has been used to train language models to generate responses that align well with human preferences in a variety of tasks, including summarization~\citep{welleck2019neural,ziegler2020learning}, dialogue~\citep{wang2020learning,ziegal2021finetuned}, and fiction writing~\citep{gururangan2021myth}, as well as to train generative adversarial networks~\citep{zhang2021training} and autoregressive models of image and video~\citep{levi2021general,bai2020learning,ouyang2022scaling}. In the RLHF framework, the reward function is typically estimated using a Gaussian process regression model, and the language model is fine-tuned using PPO~\citep{schulman2017proximal} to maximize the estimated reward without drifting too much from the original language model. While this framework has been successful, it is challenging to tune the hyperparameters of the GPR reward model and the PPO optimizer, and it is difficult to ensure that the reward model accurately reflects the preferences in the preference data, as the reward model is fit using a small number of preference labels per generated response~\citep{dudik2022neural}. To address these challenges, \citet{ziekovich2022learning} propose a method for generating synthetic preference data that can be used to train a GPR reward model without human annotation, and \citet{dudik2021neural} propose a method for learning a reward model that is invariant to changes in the language model. Despite these advances, the RLHF framework remains challenging to use in practice, as it requires fitting a GPR reward model and performing reinforcement learning to optimize the language model parameters.
