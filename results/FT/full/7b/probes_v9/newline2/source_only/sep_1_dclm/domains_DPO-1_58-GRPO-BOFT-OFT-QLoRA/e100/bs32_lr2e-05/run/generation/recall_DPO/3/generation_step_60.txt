\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a method for converting a classification loss into a reinforcement learning reward function that optimizes to the same policy. The key insight is to use the negative log of the classification loss as the reward and add a constant reward for the initial policy (the language model). This constant reward makes the optimization trivial to run, and also serves as a regularizer to prevent the policy from drifting too far from the initial language model.

\textbf{Evaluation} is done via the standard RLHF pipeline: collect human labels, construct a reward function from the labels, fine-tune the language model with RL to optimize the reward, and evaluate the controlled policy's ability to generate according to various prompts. DPO performs at least as well as existing methods (PPO, KL-regularized RLHF, and a variant of the method from \emph{PlaNet}) and in many cases is superior. In \emph{summarization}, DPO matches the state of the art with 95.2\% accuracy (up from 88.5\%) and produces far more coherent and fluent responses. In \emph{sentiment control}, DPO surpasses the prior SLHF state of the art by 10\% in accuracy, and produces responses that are far more likely to hit the specified sentiment target (sample generated in response to a 'positive' prompt: 'The young boy was crying for his parents. He had lost them in the fire. Suddenly, he saw them in the distance. They were waving at him. He started running towards them. He was so happy to be reunited with his parents.') In \emph{single-turn dialogue}, DPO matches the accuracy of the prior SLHF SOTA at 77.7\%, but produces far more fluent and sensible responses (samples: 'I'm good thanks, how are you?', 'Sure, the blue one. Do you think it will go with my shirt?', 'I'm sorry to hear that. Is there anything I can do to help?').

\textbf{Limitations} are that the method only works for discrete labels and only produces LMs that can produce one sentence at a time (though it would be relatively straightforward to modify the method to produce policies over sequences). Also, the method does not produce guarantees about the optimality of the policy, other than the theoretical guarantee that it optimizes the specified classification objective (assuming the LM is a good estimate of human preferences).

\textbf{Future Work} includes extending the method to continuous labels and policies over multiple sentences.

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models ((ULMs) trained on very large datasets) capture remarkable world knowledge and some reasoning abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022neural,chung2021segmental}. However, their behavior is largely unconstrained, raising concerns about unintended uses~\citep{bommasani2021language,bender2022dangers} and limiting their usefulness for downstream applications. To address this, we seek to gain precise control over a ULM's behavior via \emph{single-turn controlled generation}: given a short \emph{prompt} (e.g., "Explain *"), the model should generate a high-quality response (e.g., " Explain : The process by which heat is transferred between a liquid and its vapor dome.") that faithfully follows the prompt's  intent.

Existing methods for gaining such \emph{steerability} collect human labels of the quality of generations in response to different prompts and train the ULM to align with these preferences~\citep{ziegler2020learning,ziegler2021aligning,ziegler2022training}. A common approach is reinforcement learning from human feedback~(RLHF)~\citep{kupcsik2022training,kupcsik2022reinforcement}, which first fits a reward model to the human preferences, then fine-tunes the ULM using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward without drifting too far away from the original model. However, this two-step procedure is complex and often unstable; the initial reward model fit can be sensitive to hyperparameters, and the reinforcement learning step can easily cause the LM to drift or cause the policy to converge to value optimization rather than preference optimization~\citep{jaques2017reinforcement}.

In this paper, we introduce a new algorithm, \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO) that gains steerability via human preferences while avoiding the instability and complexity of existing methods. DPO is conceptually simple: it transforms a classification loss over generated responses into a reinforcement learning reward that optimizes to the \emph{same} policy as the classification loss, but does so in a way that is stable and efficient to optimize. DPO adds the negative log of the cross entropy loss as a penalty to a baseline reward and uses the original ULM as a constant reward.
