At a high level, PPO optimizes an estimate of the advantage function, $\hat{A}(s,a)$, which measures how much better action $a$ performs compared to a baseline action $b$ in state $s$:

$$\hat{A}(s, a) = \frac{\pi_\text{old}(s', a') - \pi_\text{pl}(s', a')}{\pi_\text{old }(s', a') }$$

where $\pi_\text{pl}$ is the current policy and $\pi_\text{old}$ is the old policy, which is typically the policy from the previous training batch.

To prevent large changes to the policy from occurring, PPO adds a clipping term that limits the size of the policy updates:

$$\hat{J}_{\text{PPO}}(s, a) =  \hat{A}(s,\hat{a}) \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\abs{\hat{A}(s,
a)} \leq \epsilon} - \epsilon $$

where $\epsilon$ is a small value, usually set to 0.2.

In practice, it can be expensive to compute the advantage function for every sample because it requires sampling an additional action from the baseline policy. To reduce the number of samples needed to compute the advantage function, PPO calculates an estimate of the advantage using a small set of sampled actions, known as a "batch":

$$\hat{A}_\text{batch}(s, a) =
\begin{cases}

\end{cases}
$$

where $\beta$ is a parameter between 0 and 1, and the second term inside the advantage function is sampled from the old policy rather than the baseline policy. This reduces the number of samples needed from the baseline policy by a factor of $\beta$.

This can make it difficult to accurately estimate the variance of the advantage function, which is used to scale the updates to the policy parameters.

To address this issue, GRPO uses group relative policy optimization, which estimates the advantage for each action based on the performance of its group of actions. Specifically, GRPO partitions the actions into groups, and then computes the advantage for each action relative to the best action in its group. This allows GRPO to estimate the variance of the true advantage function, which can be used to scale the updates more effectively.
