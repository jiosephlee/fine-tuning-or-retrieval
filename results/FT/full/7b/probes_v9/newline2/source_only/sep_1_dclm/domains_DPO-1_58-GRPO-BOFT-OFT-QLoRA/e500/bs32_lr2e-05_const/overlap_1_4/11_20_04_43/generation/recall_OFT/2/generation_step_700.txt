Controlling Text-to-Images Diffusion Models with Orthogonal Finetuning

Diffusion models are powerful generative models that can generate high-fidelity images from random noise. They are trained by optimizing a diffusion process, which is a stochastic process that gradually transforms a random noise into a realistic image. The training objective is to maximize the likelihood of the training dataset under the learned diffusion process.

Once a diffusion model is trained, it can be finetuned for downstream tasks. For example, to train a diffusion model for image classification, we can finetune the pretrained diffusion model by minimizing the classification loss on classification datasets. This is a simple yet effective way to finetune diffusion models. However, it is not clear how well this method preserves the geometric properties of the pretrained diffusion model. In other words, it is not clear whether this method changes the neuron representations in the pretrained diffusion model in a meaningful way.

The paper "Controlling Text-To-Image Diffusion by Finetuning" presents OFT, a simple yet effective method to finetune pretrained diffusion models. OFT is provably information-preservering, and empirically leads better performance than standard finentuning and LoRA on various tasks, including classification, segementation, and generation.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OTF)

The key idea of OFT is to finetune the pretrained model by adding an orthogonal matrix to the weight matrix. More specifically, OFT updates the weight matrix $W$ by adding an orthogonal matrix $R$:

$$ W \leftarrow W + R $$

The orthogonal matrix $R$ is learned by minimizing the squared error between the forward pass of the pretrained model and the forward pass of the finetuned model. Formally, OFT optimizes the following objective:

$$ \min_{R} \| f_\text{pre}(x) - f_\text{fin}(x) \|^2 $$

where $f_\text{pre}$ and $f_\text{fin}$ are the forward pass of the pretrain and finetuned model, respectively.

Why OFT is Information-Preserving

OFT is information-preserving because it only changes the direction of the neuron representation without changing its magnitude. To see this, we can write the forward pass of the model as:

$$ f_\text{pre}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{W}^\top \phi(\mathbf{x}) $$

where $\mathbf{W}$ is the weight matrix, and $\phi(\cdot)$ is the activation function. OFT updates the weight matrx $\mathbf{W}$. Therefore, the forward pass of the OFT-finetuned model is:

$$ f_\text {fin}(\mathbf{x} ) = \mathbf{ W}^\top \phi (\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{W} + R)^\top \phi (\matb{x}) $$

Now, let's consider the Frobenius norm of the difference between the forward pass of OFT-finetuned and pretrained model:

$$ \| f_\text{fin}(\mathbf{X}) - f_\text{pre }(\mathbf{X} )\|_F^2 = \mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{W}_\text{fin} - \mathbf{W}_ \text{pre})^\top \mathbf{X}, $$

where $\mathbb{X}$ is the input matrix. We can write $\mathbf{W}_ \text{fin} - \mathbf{W}_{\text{pre}}$ as $\mathbf{W}_{ \text{pre}} + R - \mathbf{R}$. Therefore, the Frobenius norm is:

$$ \| f_\textrm{fin}(\mathbb{X}) - f_{\text{pre}}(\mathbb{X} )\|^2_F = \mathbb{X}^\top \mathbf{(W}_\text{pre} + R - \mathbb{R})^\top \mathbb{X}. $$
