--- GENERATION ---
The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new algorithm, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), that can efficiently optimize a language model to match human preferences without relying on an intermediate reward model. DPO works by fitting a parameterized function to the pairwise preferences provided by humans, and then optimizing the language model to maximize the fitted function. The authors evaluate DPO on three benchmarks: (1) a synthetic preference dataset generated from a reward model, (2) a real-world preference dataset collected by Alpaca, and (3) the Vicuna benchmark. On the synthetic dataset, DPO matches the performance of the reward model while being much more efficient to train and evaluate. On the Alpaca dataset, DPO matches or improves upon the performance of strong baselines like RLHF and PPO, and has lower training and evaluation latency. On the Vicuna benchmark, DPO matches the state-of-the-art reward model FineTuned Rewards while being significantly more efficient to train and evaluating in linear time.

Formalizing the Problem

We start by formally defining the problem of aligning a language model with human preferences. Given a language model $p_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ and a dataset of human preferences $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{ (x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}, y^{\top{(i)}}) \bigr\}_{i=1}^N$, where $x^{(i)}$ is an input prompt, $y^{(i)}$ and $y^{\top{(i)}}" are two possible responses to the prompt, our goal is to learn a new language model $p_{olds}(y \mid x)) that better matches the human preferences in $\mathcal{D}$, i.e., $p_{olds}$ should assign higher probability to the preferred response $y^{(i)}}$ under the prompt $x^{(i)}, i.e., p_{olds}(y^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)}) > p_{olds}( y^{\top{( i)}} \mid x^{( i)})$. One simple solution is to fit a reward model $r_{\phi}(y, y')$ to the preference dataset and then optimize the language model $p_{ho}(y \mid x}$ with reinforcement learning using the reward $r_{\phi}$ as a reward signal. However, this approach has two major limitations. First, fitting a reward model is often difficult, especially for complex tasks like language generation where it is hard to come up with a reward function that captures our notion of "goodness". Second, even if we have a reward model, training a policy with reinforcement learning is slow and data-inefficient.
