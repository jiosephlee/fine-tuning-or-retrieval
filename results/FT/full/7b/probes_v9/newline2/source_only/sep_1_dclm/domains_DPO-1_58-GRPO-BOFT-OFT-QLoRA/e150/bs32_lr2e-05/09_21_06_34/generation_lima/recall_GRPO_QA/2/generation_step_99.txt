What is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?<|EOT|>Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a popular reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm that is well-suited for training large models using actor-critic methods. At a high level, PPO optimizes an estimate of the advantage function, $\hat{A}(s,a)$, which measures how much better action $a$ performs compared to some baseline action $b$ in state $s$:

$$\hat{A}(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{old}}[\text{CLIP}(s, a) - \text{CLIP}( s, b )]$$

where $\pi_{old}$ is the old policy model and $\text{CLIP}$ is the clipping function, typically set to $\text{max}(\epsilon, |\cdot|)$ where $\epsilon$ is a small constant.

To estimate the expectation in the definition of $\hat{A}$, PPO samples many trajectories from the old policy model and computes the ratio of the rewards on two trajectories with the same initial state but different actions. This ratio approximately estimates the advantage function for each step in the trajectory. The trajectory with the highest estimated advantage function is then used to compute the gradient update for the policy model.

While PPO is relatively simple and effective, it has one major drawback: it requires storing the entire old policy model during training in order to generate samples for the calculation of the advantage function. This can be a significant memory overhead, especially when training very large models. For example, when training the DeepSeek Math model, which uses the Llemma language model of 7B parameters, the authors report that using PPO would require more than 40 GB of memory, which is beyond what a single GPU can provide.

To address this issue, the authors propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRP), a variant of PPO that significantly reduces the memory requirements without sacrificing performance. The key insight behind GRPO is that the old policy model can be approximated by a set of group relative policies, defined as follows: for each group $g$ of policy models, the $i$-th group relative policy $\pi_i^g$ is a copy of the policy model that was $i$-th to be added to the group. Formally, if we have $n$ policy models $\pi_1,\dots,\pi_n$, we can partition them into $g$ groups of size $r$ each, where $nr=7B$. Then, the $i$-th group relative policy $\pi^g_i$ is defined as:

$$\pi_i^g(s) = \frac{\pi_i(s) \prod_{j \in G_i} \pi_j(s)}{\prod_{j \in R_i} \pi_j (s)}$$

where $G_i$ is the group index of the $i$-thy element in group $g$ and $R_i$ is the complement of $G_i$.

Once we have defined the group relative policies, we can use them to estimate the advantage function and compute the gradient update for our policy model. The main idea is to compare each action executed by the current policy with the best action executed by one of the group relative policies. More formally, for each step in a trajectory generated by the current policy, we sample a group $g$ uniformly at random and then sample $i \sim U(1,\dots,r)$ to get the index of the comparison. We then compute the advantage function as:

$$\hat{Q}^g_i(s, a) = \frac{\mu_{\pi_i^g}(s, a) + \epsilon}{1 + \epsilon} - \mu_{\pi^g_{i'}}(s, a)$$

where $\mu$ is the reward function, $\epsilon$ is a tiny constant, and $i'$ is any index in $R_i$. Finally, we optimize the policy model by taking the average of the clipped advantages over all steps in the trajectory and all groups $g$:

$$\nabla \mathbb{D}[\pi;\theta] = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \underbrace{\text{CLIP}(\hat{Q}^k_l(s_t, a_t), \epsilon)}_{\text{clip}}$$

where $k$ is the sampled group and $l=i$ with probability $|R_k|/r$ and $l'=r+i$ with probability $|G_k|-|R_k|/n$.

In addition to the original version of GRPO, the authors also propose two variants. The first variant, called GRPO-Top, replaces the uniform sampling of groups in the computation of the advantage function with sampling based on the highest estimated advantage function up to that point. The second variant, called GRPO-Sum, sums the rewards of the two actions instead of averaging them in the definition of the advantage function.
