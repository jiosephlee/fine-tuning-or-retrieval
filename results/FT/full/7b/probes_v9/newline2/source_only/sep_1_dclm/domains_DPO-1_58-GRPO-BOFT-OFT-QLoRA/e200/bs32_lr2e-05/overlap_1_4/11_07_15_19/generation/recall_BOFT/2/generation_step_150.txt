Let me tell you all about this paper. The paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" presents a novel and efficient method for adapting large models, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which improves parameter-efficiency over standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its block-diagonal approach. Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a parameter-efficient finetuning method that adapts large pretrained models to downstream tasks. BOFT introduces a butterfly structure to the orthogonal matrix, which significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters without compromising performance. To understand the intuition behind BOFT, let's first review some basics of OFT and then delve into the details of BOFT.

**Orthogonal Finetuning (Standard OFT)**

In OFT, the weight matrix $W$ of a layer is transformed by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$, resulting in the updated weight matrix $\mathbf{W}' = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}$. The orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ is typically learned from scratch, i.e., $\mathbf R \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}$ with $d$ being the dimension of the weight matrix $\mathbf W$. To ensure stability during training, the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf {R}$ is often initialized using the Cayley transform of a identity matrix, $\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(I-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ where $\mathbf{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix with $\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^T$. During inference, the orthogonal matrix $\mathtt{\mathbf{R}}$ is usually fixed to the identity matrix, i.e., $\mathtt{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbf{I}$.

Now that we have reviewed the standard OFT, let's move on to the block-diagonal approach of OFT.

**Block-Diagonal Approach of OFT**

The block-diagonal approach of OFT reduces the number of trainable orthogonal matrices while still preserving good performance. Specifically, the orthogonal matrix $\textbf{R}$ is factorized as $\textbf{R}=\textbf{B}\textbf{D}\textbf{B}^T$ where $\textbf{B}\in \mathbb R^{\frac{d}{b}\times d},\textbf{D}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\forall i,\textbf{B}(:,i)\text{ and } \textbf{D}(i,:)$ are orthogonal matrices of size $\frac{d}{b}x\frac{d}{b}$, and $\text{diag}(\cdot)$ denotes a diagonal matrix with the diagonal elements being the input vector. By introducing the block-diagonal structure, the number of trainable parameters becomes $\frac{d}{(b-1)}$ times smaller than the original OFT, assuming a block size $b>1$. Despite the reduced number of trainable parameters, the block-diagonal approach performs comparably to the original OFT. However, there is still room for improvement in terms of parameter efficiency.
