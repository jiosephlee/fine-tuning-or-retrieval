\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recent unsupervised language models of unprecedented scale acquire surprising capabilities \citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer the model using reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize for human preferences, we will show that the preference alignment problem can be solved exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective.

The most common approach to preference learning is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to human labels of a reward function, and then use RL to optimize a reinforcement learning agent with respect to this estimated reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF has been successful in producing LMs with targeted behaviors, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than standard LM fine-tuning, involving fitting an implicit reward model, sampling an RL agent from this reward, and then optimizing the agent with RL while stabilizing the policy and preventing drift. Moreover, the RLHF procedure involves a number of hyperparameters related to the RL algorithm, the amount of fine-tuning, and the exploration strategy, any of which could significantly affect the outcome if misconfigured.

In this paper, we show how to directly optimize a language model to adhere to human preferences, without explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. Our key insight is to recast the preference learning problem as a multi-class classification problem, where the model outputs a distribution over responses and is trained to classify each human preference label. Classifying the \emph{difference} between two responses according to the human label distribution is a particularly simple and effective way to perform the multi-class classification. Crucially, the classification loss encourages the model to predict the preferred response as highest probability and the dispreferred response as lowest probability, but \emph{not} higher than the preferred response. This constraint, which is absent in previous methods, prevents the model from gaming its loss by outputting only the highest probability response for every query, effectively reducing the preference learning problem to binary acceptance sampling. We refer to our method as \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO).

While the idea of classifying responses according to their desirability is not new \citep{ziegler2022learning,ziegler2022preflearn}, both of these methods implicitly assume a reward model and learn an agent with this reward using RL. In contrast, our method explicitly models the policy being optimized, allowing us to solve for the optimal policy corresponding to any given reward model in closed form, without explicitly estimating or using the reward model at any point in the training procedure. In other words, we perform policy improvement \citep{whitehead1959targeting} exactly, rather than approximately, and without adding an additional RL optimizer on top of the standard gradient descent optimizer used to train the language model.

We evaluate DPO in comparison to PPO-RLHF on three tasks: sentiment modulation, where the goal is to induce a desired sentiment in generative responses; single-turn dialogue, where the goal is for the model to produce responses that would lead to a desired outcome in a dialogue; and task-oriented dialogue, where the goal was to complete a information-seeking task. On all three tasks, DPO performs at least as well as PPO-RLHF, and often significantly better, indicating that our simple classification objective is as effective or more effective than the standard RLHF procedure at inducing human-preferred behaviors in LMs. Perhaps more significantly, DPO is much simpler to implement and train than PPO-RLHF and does not suffer from issues related to sampling from the LM during training or requiring significant hyperparameter tuning.
