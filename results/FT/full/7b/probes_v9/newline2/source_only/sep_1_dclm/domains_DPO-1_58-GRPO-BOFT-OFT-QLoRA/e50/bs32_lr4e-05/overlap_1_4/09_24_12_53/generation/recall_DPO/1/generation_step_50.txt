The Basics

The basic idea is to use a language model (LM) to estimate a reward model (RM). The RM is then optimized with reinforcement learning algorithms to maximize the expected reward of an action. This is done by taking the LM probability distribution over the next token and scaling it by the corresponding reward that the RM predicts. The scaled probabilities are then used to sample the next token. The reward that the RM predicts for a token is simply the reward that the RL algorithm provides after the token has been generated. This process is repeated until convergence.

The paper uses the following notation:

  • \(p\) is the language model
  • \(r\) is the reward model
  • \(\pi\) is the policy, i.e. the sampling model that selects the next token based on the rewards predicted by the reward model
 • \(o\) is an observation, i.e. a token
  • \(r(o)\) is the reward provided by the reward model for the observation \(o\)
  • \(\pi(o \mid \mid p)\) is the probability that the policy \(\pi\) samples the observation \(o\) given the language model \(p\)

The paper uses the following equation to define the reward model:

\[r(o) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(o \mid \ mid p)}{\pi^\text{ref}(o \mid \ mid p)},\]

where \(\pi^\text{ref}\) is a reference policy and \(\beta\) is a hyperparameter.

The paper uses the KL-divergence between the reference policy and the policy \(\pi\), i.e. \(\text{KL}[\pi^\text{ref} \mid \mid \pi]\), as the reward provided by the RM for each token. This means that the KL-divergence is simply scaled by \(\beta\). The KL-divergence is a measure for how much two probability distributions differ. In this case, the reference policy \(\pi^\text {ref}\) represents the "correct" policy and the policy \[\pi\) represents the policy that the RL algorithm is trying to optimize. By maximizing the reward, the RL algorithm is trying minimize the KL-divergence, i.e. it is trying to make the policy \(\pi)\) indistinguishable from the reference policy \(\ pi^\text{ref}\).

The policy \(\pi\) is simply the softmax of the rewards predicted by the RM, scaled by a temperature parameter \(\theta\):

\[\pi(o \mid \  mid p) = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{o} \text{exp}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}r(o)\right),\]

where \(Z\) is the partition function, i.e. the normalizing constant that ensures that the policy \(\ pi)\) is a valid probability distribution.

The policy is initialized by sampling from the language model \(p\) and then refined with reinforcement learning. The reinforcement learning is done by maximizing the reward using the actor-critic algorithm. The actor-critic algorithm is a combination of the actor algorithm, which optimizes a policy by maximizing the reward, and the critic algorithm, which estimates the value of a policy. In the context of the DPO paper, the critic simply estimates the KL-divergence to the reference policy.

The actor-critic algorithm works as follows: First, the language model \(p'\) is sampled \(N\) times to get the observations \(o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_N\). Then, the rewards \(r(o_1), r(o_2), \ldots, r(o_N)\) are computed by the reward model \(r\). These rewards are then used by the critic to estimate the KL-divergence \(\text{KL}\left[\pi^\text{ ref} \mid \mid  \pi\right]\) and the policy \(\ pi\) is updated to increase the reward. The process is repeated until convergence. The paper uses the following update rule for the policy \(\pi:\)

\[\pi \leftarrow \pi + \eta \nabla_\pi \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi^\text {ref} \mid \  mid \pi\right],\]

where \(\eta\) is the learning rate and the gradient \(\nabla_\pi\) is with respect to the parameters of the policy \(\pi).
