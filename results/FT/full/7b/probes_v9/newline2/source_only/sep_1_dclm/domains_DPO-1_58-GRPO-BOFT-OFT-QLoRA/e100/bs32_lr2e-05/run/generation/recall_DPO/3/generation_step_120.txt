\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recent unsupervised language models of unprecedented scale acquire surprising capabilities \citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer the model's behavior using human preferences, the human preference labeling task is also imprecise and prone to error; consequently, selecting an appropriate objective for training from these noisy labels is nontrivial.

The state-of-the-art approach for training AI systems to adhere to human preferences is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLFAI; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, the trainer provides not actual rewards, but \textit{human preferences}, in the form of labels indicating a winner $w\in\{1,2\}$ for pairs of simulations $(x, y)\sim P$ from some proposal distribution $P$. The RLHF algorithm fits a reward model $r:\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y\to[0,1]$ from the preferences, then fine-tunes a base unsupervised language model $p_{\theta}^0$ using reinforcement learning to maximize the expected reward given by this reward model, i.e., $\pi_\theta(x)\propto\mathbb E_{y\sim p_{\theta'}|x}[r(x, y)]$ where $\pi_\theta(x)=\text{sample } y\sim p_{\thet'a|x}$ is the trained controlled model. This approach has the drawback that it requires fitting a reward model $r$ and then fine-tuning a language model $p$ with reinforcement learning, each of which could be difficult in their own right; furthermore, the reward fitting and RL fine-tuning steps can interact in complicated ways, requiring careful tuning of hyperparameters such as the reinforcement learning discount factor and the amount of exploitation vs exploration in the RL stage. In contrast, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Algorithm \ref{alg:dpo}) fits a reward model and refines a policy in a single step, eliminating potential sources of instability and interaction between stages.

\subsection{Main Contributions}

1. \textbf{A new reparameterization of the reward model.} We introduce a new reparameterization of reward models, which we show can be computed in closed form for certain well-known models (e.g., linear reward models) and can be optimized efficiently for more expressive reward models. 

2. \textbf{Direct Preference Optimization (D PO)}, a new RLHF algorithm that uses this reparameterization to achieve the goals described above: (a) fitting a reward model from human preferences, and (b) refining the language model with reinforcement learning while preventing significant drift from the initial model. DPO is conceptually simpler and computationally lighter than existing RLHF algorithms, as it can compute the optimal policy corresponding to a given reward model in closed form, eliminating the need for reward modeling and reinforcement learning stages with their own potential sources of instability, and performing a single classification-based optimization step.

3. \textbf{Empirical results demonstrating that DPO is as effective as, and in many cases superior to, the state-of-the-art RLHF algorithm (PPO; \citep{schulman2017proximal}) at inducing controlled language models from human preferences, while being conceptually simpler and avoiding the need for additional hyperparameters.} We evaluate DPO on three tasks -- sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue -- showing that on each task DPO can train a controlled language model from preferences as well as or Better than PPO, and is in many cases significantly simpler to implement and train.
