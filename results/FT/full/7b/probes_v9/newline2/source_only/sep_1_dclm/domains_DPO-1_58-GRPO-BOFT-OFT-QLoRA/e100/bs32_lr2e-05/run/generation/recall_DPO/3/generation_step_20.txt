\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a classification loss that is used to train a language model to generate text that is preferred by a human. The loss is defined as the cross-entropy between the probability of the human-preferred text and the probability of the model's output. The model is then fine-tuned with this loss to improve its ability to generate text that is more preferred by the human. The key insight of DPO is that the reward model can be parameterized as a linear function of the model's output, which allows the reward model to be learned in closed form. This is in contrast to existing methods for training language models to generate text that is desired by a human, which typically use reinforcement learning from human feedback to learn a reward model and then fine-tune the language model to maximize the reward. The reward model is typically learned using a neural network, which requires sampling from the language model to train the reward model. This makes the training of the reward model slow and unstable. DPO avoids these issues by learning the reward model in closed form, which makes it faster and more stable to train. DPO is also simpler to implement than existing methods for training language model to generate text desired by a human, as it does not require sampling from the language model or performing hyperparameter tuning. DPO is a general framework that can be used to train language models to generate text in any domain. In this paper, we evaluate DPO on the task of sentiment control, where the goal is to generate text that expresses a desired sentiment. We compare DPO to existing methods for training sentiment-controlled language models, including reinforcement learning from human feedback and supervised learning. We find that DPO is able to train sentiment-controlled language models that are as good as or better than existing approaches, while being simpler and faster to train. We also evaluate DPO on the tasks of summarization and single-turn dialog, where the goal is for the language model to generate text in response to a prompt. We find that DOP is able to train language models that are as effective as or better than existing supervised and reinforcement learning methods, while being simpler and more stable to train.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Unsupervised Language Models.} The recent success of large-scale unsupervised pretraining has led to a surge of interest in unsupervised learning of language models. The most popular unsupervised pretraining methods, such as BERT~\citep{devlin2018bert} and GPT~\citep{radford2019language}, learn to predict the next word in a sequence of words, while more recent methods, such as T5~\citep{t5}, learn to predict the entire next sequence of words. These methods typically use a masked language modeling objective, where a fraction of the words in the input sequence are replaced with a special token, and the model is trained to predict the original word. Other methods, such as GPT-3~\citep{brown2020language}, use a next sentence prediction objective, where the model is trained to determine whether two sentences are likely to be consecutive in a text. These methods typically use an autoregressive objective, where the model predicts the next word in the sequence one at a time. More recently, methods such as PEGASUS~\citep{zhang2023pegasus} and T5-XXL~\citep{wang2022t5} have been proposed that use a masked language modeling loss with a larger batch size, which allows the model to learn longer-range dependencies. These methods typically use the same architecture as BERT and GPT, but with a larger number of layers and parameters. While these methods have been shown to be effective at learning to generate text, they are not designed to be controllable, and it is difficult to steer their behavior.

\textbf{Controlling Language Models.} One way to control the behavior of a language model is to fine-tune it with supervised learning on a task-specific dataset. For example, a sentiment-controlled language model can be trained to generate text that expresses desired sentiments by fine-tuning it on a sentiment classification dataset. However, this approach requires a large amount of labeled data, which is often not available. Another approach is to use reinforcement learning to train the language model to generate desired text. This typically involves training a reward model that reflects human preferences, and then using reinforcement learning to fine-tune the language to maximize the reward. For example, the RLHF method~\citep{liu2022rlhf} uses a neural network to parameterize the reward model, and then uses PPO~\citep{schulman2017proximal} to train the language model. This approach has been shown to be effective for controlling the behavior of language models, but it is slow and unstable, as it requires sampling from the language models to train the reward model, and it is difficult for the reward model to capture the complex preferences of humans.
