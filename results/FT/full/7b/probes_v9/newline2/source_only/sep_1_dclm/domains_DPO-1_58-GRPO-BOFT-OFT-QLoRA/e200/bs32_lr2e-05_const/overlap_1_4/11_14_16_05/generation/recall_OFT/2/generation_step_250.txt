Diffusion Models

Diffusion models are trained to generate realistic images by conditioning on a short text prompt. They are trained using a contrastive loss, which encourages the model to make similar transformations to a real image and its corrupted version, while making distinct transformations to different real images. Mathematically, the training loss for a diffusion model is defined as:

$\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{1}_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{m}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{h}_i\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbb{1}_N\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}_ i)\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{h}_ j\mathbf{W})^\top\right) - \beta\log\sigma(\mathbf{z}),$

where $\mathbf{m}$ is a real image, $\mathbf{x}$ is a corrupted version of $\mathbf{m)$, $\mathbf{h}_1,\cdots,\mathbf{h}_N$ are sampled from the standard normal distribution, $\mathbf{W}$ is the diffusion model weight matrix, and $\beta$ is the temperature parameter. The term inside the parentheses is the mean cosine similarity between the transformed versions of two different images, and the term $-\beta\log\sigma($ is the cross-entropy loss for the reverse diffusion process.

During inference, a diffusion model is used to generate a noisy image by sampling a noise vector $\mathbf{z}$ from the standard normal distribution. Then a clean image is obtained by running the reverse diffusion process starting from the noisy image.

Finetuning Methods

Finetuning diffusion models is an effective way to adapt them to new tasks, such as changing the distribution of generated images from generic to portrait images. Existing finetuning methods can be generally categorized into two classes: standard finetuning and additive methods.

Standard finetuning updates the weight matrix using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) based on the cross-entropy loss between the generated images and the real images from the target distribution. Although standard finetuning is simple and effective, it often causes the neuron geometry to change significantly, leading to forgetting or instability.

Additive methods, such as Low-rank Adverserial Finetuning (LoRa) and Discrete Fourier Transform Finetuning (DFT), avoid changing the neuron geometry by only adding a small full rank weight matrix to the original diffusion model weight matrix. LoRA adds a low-rank weight matrix to the original weight matrix, while DFT adds a discrete Fourier transform of a weight matrix to the original matrix. Although additive methods preserve the neuron geometry, they may cause the diffusion process to diverge or fail to converge, especially when the added weight matrix has a large magnitude.

Orthogonal Finetuning

Now let's introduce the Orthogonal Finetting (OFT) method. The key idea of OFT is to learn an orthogonal transformation for each neurons in the weight matrix, so that the pairwise angles between neurons are preserved. More specifically, OFT learns a orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ for the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$. The finetuned weight matrix is then given by $\mathbf{W}_\text{oft}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}.$
