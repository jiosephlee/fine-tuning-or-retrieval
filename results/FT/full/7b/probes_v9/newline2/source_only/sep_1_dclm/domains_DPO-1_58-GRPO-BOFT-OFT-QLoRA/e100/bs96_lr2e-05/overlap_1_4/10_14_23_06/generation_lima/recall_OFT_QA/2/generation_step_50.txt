What is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)?<|EOT|>Text-to-image diffusion models are powerful generative models that can generate photorealistic images from text prompts. These models are typically trained using a contrastive loss that encourages similar embeddings for similar images and dissimilar embeddings for different images. However, this training objective does not explicitly encourage the neural network to preserve the semantic meaning of the input prompt in its embedding.

One way to enforce semantic preservation is through finetuning, where the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model is finetuned on a dataset of text-image pairs. During finetuning, the embeddings of similar images should remain close together, while the embedding of a image and its corresponding prompt should become even closer. This can be achieved using standard finetuning, where all weights are updated proportionally to their learning rate, or using an additive update like LoRA, where new weights are added to the pretrained weights.

However, both standard finetuning and LoRA can cause significant changes to the neuron activations of the pretrained model. For example, a neuron that was activated by dogs in the pretrained model may start getting activated by cats in the finetuned model. This can be problematic, since it can cause the neuron to lose its semantic meaning (e.g. a neuron that is activated by dogs) and make the pretrained model harder to interpret.

To address this issue, the paper proposes Orthogonal Finetuning, a finetuning method that preserves the pairwise angles between neurons in the pretrained model. This is achieved by constraining the finetuning updates to be orthogonal, i.e. to preserve all pairwise angles between neurons. As a result, the relative positions and angles between neurons are preserved during finetuning, which helps to maintain the semantic meaning of the neurons.

Formally, let $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be a neuron in a layer $l$, with $d$ being the dimensionality of the neuron. The weight update for this neuron due to an input $x$ using standard finetuning is:

$\mathbf{w}_{\text{std}}(x) = \mathbf{w} + \alpha \nabla_\mathbf{w} L(\mathbf{w}, x)$

where $\alpha$ is the learning rate and $L$ is the finetuning loss. This update moves the neuron in the direction of steepest descent of the loss with respect to the weight $\mathbf{w}$.

Using LoRA, the update is instead:

$\mathbf{W}_{\text{LoRA}}(x) =\mathbf{W} + \alpha \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}$

where $\mathbf{R} = \text{diag}(r_1,\ldots,r_d)$ is a diagonal matrix with the same diagonal entries $r_i = \frac{\lambda_i}{\|\nabla_\mathBF{w} L(\Bf{w}, x)_i\|}$, with $\lambda_i$ being the $i$-th singular value of the gradient $\nabla_\mathb{w} L(\bf{w}, x)$. This means that the update is scaled by the inverse of the singular values of the gradient, which makes the update more sensitive to directions of large variation and less sensitive to directions of small variation.

The OFT update is similar to the standard finetuning update, except that it constrains the gradient to be orthogonal:

$\mathbf{B}_{\text{OFT}}(x) =  \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{w})\nabla_\mathbb{w} L(\bm{w}, x) $,

where $\mathbf{B} = \text{Diag}(\sin(\theta_1),\ldots,\sin(\theta_d))$ and $\theta_i$ is the angle between the $i$-the neuron and all other neurons in the layer. Intuitively, the update rotates the basis of the neuron activations such that a straight line between the neuron and its neighbor in the rotated space has an angle of $\theta$.

The authors also propose a variant of OFT called Angular OFT (AOFT), where the angles are only preserved between neurons that are connected by a synaptic weight. This variant is equivalent to constraining the gradient to be orthogonal with respect to a diagonal matrix $\mathbf{B}$ that has $1$s on the diagonal where two neurons are connected and $0$s otherwise.

The paper provides theoretical guarantees showing that OFT preserves all pairwise angles between neurons, while standard finetuning and additive methods like LoRA do not. The paper also provides empirical evidence showing that OFT is more effective than standard finetuning and that AOFT is more effective than OFT, since it only needs to constrain the angles between connected neurons.<|endoftext|>
