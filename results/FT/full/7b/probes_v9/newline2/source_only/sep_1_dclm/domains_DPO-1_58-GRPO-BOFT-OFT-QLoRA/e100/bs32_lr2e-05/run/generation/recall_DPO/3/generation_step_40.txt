\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reparameterization of the reward model used in RLHF that enables the extraction of the corresponding optimal reinforcement learning policy in closed form. This allows us to solve the standard problem of aligning an unsupervised LM with human preferences using only a simple classification loss, without the need for sampling from or performing reinforcement learning on the LM during fine-tunning, or performing significant hyperparameter search. The resulting algorithm is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, and eliminates the need for complex RLHF algorithms like PPO.

We evaluate DPO on the task of aligning LMs with human preferences in sentiment, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. In sentiment, we find that DPO can match or exceed the performance of PPO-based RLHF algorithms, including one that uses a learned reward model instead of a human-provided label for each example. In summarization, DPO matches or improves upon the performance of existing RLHF algorithms, while being simpler to implement and train. In single-turn dialogue, DPO matches or slightly improves the performance of existing RL HF algorithms, while being simpler and more stable. Overall, these results demonstrate that DPO is a simple yet effective method for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Large-scale unsupervised language modeling}. The most widely used unsupervised language modeling objective is the negative log likelihood of a token sequence, $-\log p(w_1,\dots,w_T)$, where $p$ is the language model probability distribution. This objective encourages the model to assign high probability to sequences that are likely in the data distribution, but does not necessarily produce samples that are useful or desirable. To address this, several methods have been proposed to encourage generative models to produce samples with specific properties, such as those relevant to downstream tasks \citep{ouyang2021learning, bai2021few, welleck2021learning, guo2021learning, liu2021prefix, welleck2022learning, bai2022learning}, or that align with human preferences \citep{bai2021learning, leandro2022training, leandro2023training}. 

\textbf{Reinforcement learning from human feedback}. The most common approach to training LMs to produce samples with specified properties is reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{christiano2017deep}, which uses a reward function that encodes a notion of desirability to train an agent to produce samples with the desired properties. In the context of language modeling, the reward function is typically a function of the generated text, and is often learned from human preferences. For example, \citet{bai2021Learning} train a language model using reinforcement learning from human feedback to maximize the expected reward of a reward model trained on human preferences for sentiment, and \citet{leandro2022training} train a language model to maximize the reward of a reward model that is trained on human preferences for summarization quality. While \citet{bliumis2018learning} train a language model directly using reinforcement learning from human preferences, \citet{leborgne2018human} train a generative adversarial network to generate samples that maximize the reward assigned by a language model trained with reinforcement learning from human preferences. \citet{leong2022training} train an agent to generate text that maximizes the reward assigned by a reward model trained on a dataset of human preferences for text generation tasks. \citet{wang2022training} train language models to maximize the reward assigned by an RLHF reward model, but with a modified version of the RLHF algorithm that uses a single roll-out of the language model instead of sampling multiple roll-outs. \citet{chen2022training} train LMs to maximize the reward assigned to them by a reward model trained with RLHF on a subset of the data, but with a modified RLHF algorithm that uses only a single roll-out of each language model. \citet{dettmers2022training} train generative models to maximize the reward of an RLHF reward model using a modified version of the PPO algorithm \citep{schulman2017proximal} that uses a single roll out of the generative model instead of sampling multiple rolls-outs. \citet{kopf2022training} train text-to-image generative models to maximize a reward model trained with reinforcement learning on a dataset of human ratings of generated images. \citet{zhang2022training} train image captioning models to maximize the reward from a reward model trained with human preferences on the quality of generated captions. \citet{liu2022training} train summarization models to maximize the reward given by a reward model trained using RLHF on a dataset of human-preferred summaries. \citet{gheini2022training} train dialogue models to maximize the reward provided by a reward model trained to match human preferences for dialogue responses. \c
