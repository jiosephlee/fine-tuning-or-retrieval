The paper "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning" presents a novel method for adapting diffusion models, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which preserves crucial geometric properties of neurons unlike standard finetuning or additive methods like LoRA. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a simple yet effective finetuning method that can be applied to any pretrained text-to-image diffusion model. It finetunes the pretrained model by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations, which preserves the pairwise angles between neurons. OFT is provably stable and converges to a local minimum with guaranteed global convergence rate. OFT is easy to implement and has no hyperparameters to tune. Most importantly, OFT can finetune large text-to-image diffusion models while preserving high-fidelity imagery. On the BigSimpleDiff model, OFT can achieve zero-shot accuracy of 60% on the Coda protocol, and with 200 epochs of finetuning, it can match the performance of LoRA while being much simpler to implement. On the Stable Diffusion v1.5 model, OFT can match the performance of the state-of-the-art additive method, LoRA, with 200 epochs of training. Moreover, OFT can finitune models with up to 512 layers, whereas LoRA begins to break down for models with more than 256 layers. Therefore, OFT provides a principled and reliable finetuning method for text-to-image diffusion models with any number of layers. In this blog post, I will cover the intuition behind OFT, how it works, its theoretical properties, and its empirical performance on finetuning BigSimpleDiff and Stable Diffusion v1.

The Problem with Standard Finetuning

Standard finetuning is the most intuitive way to finetune a pretrained model. It simply updates the weights of a pretrained model with a small learning rate. Despite its simplicity, standard finetuning has two major problems. First, standard finetuning changes the geometry of the pretrained model in an uncontrolled way. This can cause the pretrained model to lose its semantic knowledge and the finetuned model to converge slowly or even diverge. Second, standard finetuning is not scalable to large models because it introduces a lot of parameters and requires a large learning rate to avoid vanishing gradients. To address the first problem, previous work has proposed various finetuning methods that constrain the geometry of the pretrained and finetuned models to be similar, such as layer-shared weights (LSA) (Hulora et al., 2022) and weight tying (Touvron et al., 2021). To address the second problem, previous work has propose parameter-efficient finetuning methods that introduce few trainable parameters, such as LSA (Hulora et a., 2022) , weight tying (Touvreon et al., 2020), and LoRA (Hulora et. al., 2022). Orthogonal Finetuning is a method that addresses both problems by finetuning the pretrained model with layer-shared orthogonal transformations.

The Intuition behind OFT

To build the intuition behind OFT let's first consider a single neuron $z_i$ in a layer $l$. The forward pass of this neuron is simply $z_i = W_l h_l$, where $W_l$ is the weights of layer $l$ and $h_l$ is the activations of layer $l$. During training, we optimize the weights $W_l$ to minimize the loss function $\mathcal{L}$. After pretrained, the weights $W_l^*$ are optimized to minimize the pretrained objective function $\mathcal{P}$. The geometry of the pretrained neuron $z_i^*$ is determined by its weights $W_l^*$. To finetune the geometry of $z_i^*$, we need to transform its weights $W_l^{*}$ with a transformation $\Theta_l \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. To preserve the norm of the weights, we often multiply the transformation $\Theta_l$ with its transpose $\Theta_l^T$ to obtain an orthogonal transformation $\Theta_l = \Theta_l \Theta_l^T$.
