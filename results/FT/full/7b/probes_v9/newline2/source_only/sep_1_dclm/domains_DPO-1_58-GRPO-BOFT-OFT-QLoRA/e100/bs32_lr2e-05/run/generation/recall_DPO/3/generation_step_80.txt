\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recent unsupervised language models of unprecedented scale acquire surprising capabilities \citep{chowdhery2022scaling, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Despite this, their behavior is difficult to direct in practice. When prompted with a question, even the best language models make incorrect responses with a significant probability. Simply maximizing the likelihood of the desired response is not feasible, as the probability of the correct response to a query is typically much smaller than the probability of an incorrect response. 

One approach to gaining such steerability is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF~\citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, an initial distribution over responses, such as a large unsupervised language model, serves as the agent in a reinforcement learning problem where the “environment” is the real world and an agent’s goal is to cause its responses to align with a human’s preferences. In the context of directing the behavior of an language model, the RLHF framework learns a policy that generates human-provided labels in response to prompts, where the label should be 1 for the selected response and 0 for other responses.

While RLHF has been successful in aligning large language models with human preferences \citep{christiano2021training}, it presents challenges. First, RLHF learns a reward function from human preferences and then fine-tunes a language model using reinforcement learning to maximize the learned reward without drifting too far away from the original model. This two-stage procedure introduces opportunities for instability. Second, the reinforcement learning stage of RLHF can be computationally demanding, requiring sampling from the language model during training and tuning multiple hyperparameters.

In this paper, we introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a new algorithm for performing RLHF that avoids both of these issues. The key insight behind DPO is a reparameterization of the reward function used in RLHF that enables extracting the corresponding optimal policy in close form, allowing us to perform RLHF using only a simple classification loss. Specifically, the reward function used in prior work \citep{christianon2017deep,bai2019ask,bai2022constitutional} is a quadratic function in the policy, and we instead use a quadratic function in the inverse policy. We show that this simple change enables us to solve the standardRLHF problem with only a classification loss; there is no explicit reward model and no reinforcement learning stage.

While the reward function used by DPO is quadratic and therefore simple, it captures the same information as the reward function used in previous RLHF algorithms. In fact, with slight abuse of notation, we will show that the reward function used by prior RLHF algorithms is the cross entropy between the true label and the policy evaluated under the ground truth reward function; under this formulation, our reward function is the negative of this cross entropy. This equivalence allows us to leverage results from information theory \citep{cover2012elements} to justify the use of our reward function and argue that DPO optimizes the same objective as previous RLHF algorithms.

Informally, the reward function used by previous RLHF algorithms is the difference between the probability assigned by the ground-truth policy and the probability assigned by the current policy, i.e., $R^{*}(p,\pi) = H(p) - H(\pi(p))$, where $H(\cdot)$ is the entropy function and $\pi$ is the ground-truth policy \citep{christIANO2017deep}. Our reward function is equivalent to $-H(p,\pi)$, which we justify through a series of information theoretic mappings. First, we show that $R^{*}(.,\pi)$ can be written as $-H(p,\Pi)$, where $\Pi$ is the reward function induced by the ground-truthpolicy $\pi$. Next, we show that $\Pi$ can be written as the negative cross entropy between the true distribution $p$ and the policy $\pi$ evaluated under the true distribution $p$, i.e., $\Pi=p-\text{CE}(\pi,p)$. Finally, we define our reward function $R(p,\pi)=-\text{CE}(p,\pi)$ and show that it is equivalent to $- H(p,\pi)$.

Theoretical justification aside, the core insight of DPO is to simply optimize $-H(p,\hat{\pi})$ where $\hat{\pi}$ is the current policy and $p$ is the true distribution (the ground-truth policy).
