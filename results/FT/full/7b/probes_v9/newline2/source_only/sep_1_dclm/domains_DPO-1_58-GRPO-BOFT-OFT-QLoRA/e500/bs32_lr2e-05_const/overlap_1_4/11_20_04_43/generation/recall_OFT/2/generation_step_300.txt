The intuition behind OFT

Before we dive into the details of OFT, let's first discuss why finetuning text-to-image diffusion models is important. Text-to-image diffusion models are trained on large datasets of text prompts and image completions. During training, the model learns a mapping from a text prompt to a latent code, which is then passed through a diffusion prior to generate an image completion. This process repeats many times, with the model updating its parameters each time based on the difference between the generated image and the true image completion. As a result, the model learns a rich representation of the relationship between text prompts and image completations.

However, when the model is deployed, we often want to use it for a different task, such as generating novel images given a text prompt. To do this, we need to finetune the model, i.e., update its parameters based on a new set of data. The challenge is to find a finetuning method that preserves the semantic knowledge the model has learned from its pretraining data, while also adapting it to the new task.

One way to finetune a neural network is to simply update all of its weights by a small amount, a method known as standard finetuning. However, this can cause the neurons to move around in the input space, changing their relative positions and potentially destroying the semantic knowledge they have learned. To address this, the LoRA method updates the weights using a low-rank matrix, which preserves the pairwise distances between neurons. While LoRA is effective at preserving the semantic knowledge, it may not be able to adapt the model well to the new task, as it only updates a small portion of the weights.

The OFT method takes a different approach. Instead of updating the weights, OFT updates the neurons themselves, by learning orthogonal transformations for them. An orthogonal transformation is a linear transformation that preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, i.e., the angle between any two neurons before the transformation is the same as the angle between their transformed versions. By applying orthogonal transformations to the neurons, OFT is able to preserve the semantic knowledge they have already learned, while also adapting them to the new task.
