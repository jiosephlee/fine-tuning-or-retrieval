### Dense and Sparse Matrices

A square matrix is considered **dense** if most of its elements are nonzero; otherwise, it is called **sparse**. For example, a dense $d \times d$ matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ nonzero elements, while a sparse matrix typically has only $\mathcal O(d)$ nonzero elements. This difference in density has significant implications for computational efficiency and memory usage.

### Block Diagonal Matrices

A square $d \times d $ matrix is called **block diagonal** if it has the form
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
  B_1 &  0      & \cdots &  0 \\
  0   &  B_2 & \ddots & \vdots \\
  \vdots & \ddots &  \ddots &  0  \\
  0     & \cdots &      0 &  B_r
\end{bmatrix},
$$
where $B_i$ are square blocks. Block diagonal matrices are a special case of block matrices, which allow zero entries only on the main diagonal and the superdiagonal or subdiagonal. Block diagonal matrices are useful because they can represent several independent matrices as a single entity, allowing for more efficient storage and computation.

### Orthogonal Matrices

An orthogonal matrix $Q$ satisfies the condition $Q^\top Q = Q Q^\top = I$, where $I$ is the identity matrix. This property ensures that the column vectors of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis, i.e., they are mutually orthogonal unit vectors. Orthogonal matrices have several important properties, including preserving the length of vectors under transformation and maintaining the angles between vectors. These properties make orthogonal matrices particularly useful in various applications, such as rotation and reflection transformations.
