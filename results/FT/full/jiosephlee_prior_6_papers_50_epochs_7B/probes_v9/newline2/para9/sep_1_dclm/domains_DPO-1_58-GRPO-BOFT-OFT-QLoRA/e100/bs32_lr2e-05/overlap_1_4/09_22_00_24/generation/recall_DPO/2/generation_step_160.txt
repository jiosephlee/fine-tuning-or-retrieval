The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), instead of the usual RLHF pipeline like PPO. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a straightforward and efficient approach to training language models to follow human preferences. It does not require any reinforcement learning or reward models; rather, it directly optimizes the language model using preference data.

The authors begin by outlining the standard RLHF workflow, which involves collecting preference data, constructing a reward model from that data, and then training the language model via reinforcement learning to maximize the learned reward while constraining the model to avoid large deviations from its initial parameters. This process is both time-consuming and computationally demanding. DPO, on the other hand, eliminates the need for a reward model and instead uses a simple cross-entropy loss to train the language model to reflect human preferences. The key idea behind DPO is that the preference data can be viewed as providing supervision over the conditional distribution of human judgments, allowing us to treat the preference data as ground-truth labels for a binary classification task. In this framework, the language model serves as the classifier, and the objective is to minimize the discrepancy between the actual observed preferences and those predicted by the language model. To do so, DPO defines a loss function that compares the model’s predictions with the true preference distributions. The loss is constructed by considering the probability ratio of the two response tokens under the language model, adjusting for the inherent uncertainty in token-level probabilities. If the model assigns a higher likelihood to the preferred token sequence than to the less-preferred one, the loss will be low; otherwise, the loss will be high. The DPO loss is defined as follows:

$L(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l, z)}\log \frac{\exp(\xi(\theta, x, y_w))}{\exp(\xi(\theta,x,y_w))+\exp(\xi(\thetax,y_l))},$

where $\xi(\theta, x,y)$ denotes the logit score associated with the preferred $y$ relative to the non-preferred $y$ under the language model $\theta$. The expectation is taken over a dataset of prompts $x$, their preferred completions $y_w$, less-preferred completions $y_l$, and the corresponding ground-truth human preference signals $z$. The loss includes a temperature parameter $T$ to control the softness of the ratio, and an optional label smoothing term to prevent the loss from becoming too flat when the model’s preference probabilities are already well aligned with the human preferences. The authors also introduce a KL regularization term to encourage the model’s preference outputs to remain close to the prior, thereby preventing excessive changes in the model’s behavior:

$L(\theta)  = L_{DPO}(\theta) + \beta\mathbb{E}_x\text{KL}\big[\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\mid \mid \pi_{\theta_{old}}(y|x)\big].$

Here, $\beta$ controls the strength of this regularizer. The DPO loss can be interpreted as a binary cross-entropy loss, where the positive class is defined by the preferred completion being more probable than the negative class under the language model. Because the DPO loss is simply a standard cross-entropy loss, it can be efficiently minimized using stochastic gradient descent, making DPO computationally efficient compared to the standard RLHF pipeline. Additionally, DPO does not require a reward model or reinforcement learning, and thus avoids the instability issues associated with fine-tuning a policy model within a constrained RL environment.

The authors demonstrate that DPO produces models that are comparable to those trained with PPO, even when using relatively small datasets. They evaluate models on the FLAN benchmark, which contains 1.3 million human preference annotations, and on a smaller in-house preference dataset containing 120,000 samples. For both datasets, they compare DPO-trained models against models trained with PPO and with a baseline GPT-4 model. On the FLAN benchmark, DPO achieves 63.2% agreement with human raters, matching the performance of PPO. On the smaller in-house dataset, DPO outperforms the PPO model by 5 percentage points. These results indicate that DPO is a robust and effective alternative to PPO for aligning language models to human preferences.

The authors also investigate how DPO performs when used to optimize a reward model, demonstrating that DPO can be used to train a reward model that is equivalent to the one employed by PPO. They show that DPO can be applied to the RLHF workflow, replacing the standard RLHF procedure. In this context, DPO is used to train a reward function, and then a policy optimization step is performed using PPO. This approach yields a reward function that is identical to the one produced by PPO. This finding suggests that DPO can serve as a replacement for the entire RLHF pipeline, including the reward-model training stage.
