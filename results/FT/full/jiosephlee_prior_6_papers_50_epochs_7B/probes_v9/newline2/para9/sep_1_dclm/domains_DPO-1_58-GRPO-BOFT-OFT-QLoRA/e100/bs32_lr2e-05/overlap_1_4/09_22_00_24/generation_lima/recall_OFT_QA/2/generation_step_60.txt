What is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)?<|EOT|>Orthogonal Finetuning (or OFT for short) is a novel finetuning technique designed to preserve the pairwise angles between neurons in a pretrained neural network. The core idea behind OFT is to learn an orthogonal transformation to rotate and scale the neurons in the pretrained network while keeping their pairwise angles unchanged. This approach is particularly effective when finetuning text-to-image diffusion models, as it can significantly reduce the generation of undesirable artifacts.

To better understand the concept of orthogonal transformations, consider the following example: a rotation matrix in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is given by
\[
R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Applying $R(\theta)$ to a vector $x$ results in a rotation of $x$ around the origin by angle $\theta$. Similarly, a reflection about the $x$-axis is represented by the matrix
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix},
\]
and applying this matrix to $x$ reflects $x$ across the $x$-coordinate axis. Both matrices are examples of orthogonal transformations, as their product equals the identity matrix and their transpose is their inverse. In general, an orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb R^{2\times 2}$ has the form
\[
\begin{xthm}
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta_{ij} & -\sin\ theta_{ij} \\
\sin\theta_{ij}  & \cos\theta_{ij}
\end{bmatrix},
 \end{xthm}
\]
where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between the $j$-th column vector of the pretrained model and the $i$-th one.
In higher dimensions, an orthogonal matrix in $d$ dimensions can be parameterized as the block-diagonal matrix composed of $d/2$ such $2\times 2$ blocks. This structure arises from the fact that any orthogonal matrix in $\mathbf R^{2\times2}$ can be block-diagonalized into $2\times 1$ blocks. For $d$ even, an orthogonal matrix in dimension $d$ can be parameterized using $d/2$ $2\times 3$ blocks. This parameterization is known as the Cayley transform, which is equivalent to the classical Gram–Schmidt process up to a multiplicative constant. The block-diagonal structure of the orthogonal matrix ensures that only the pairwise angles between neurons are changed during finetuning, while all other angles remain unaltered.

The OFT method is based on the observation that the pretrained neural network can be viewed as a map from the input space to a high-dimensional neuron space. Each neuron in the pretrained network is then projected onto the new neuron space via an orthogonal transformation. To illustrate this, consider a pretrained neuron $z_0$ with coordinates $(x_1,\dots,x_n)^\top$ in the neuron space. Applying an orthogonal transformation $R$ yields the finetuned neuron
\[
R\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta_{ij}&-\sin\theta_{ij}\\ \sin\theta_{ij}&\cos\theta_{ji}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots\\ x_n\end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix}\xi_1\\ \vdotes\\ \xi_n\end{bmatrx},
\]
where $\xi_i=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^n (\cos\theta_{ij}-\theta_{ij})(x_j)^2 + (\sin\theta_{ij})(-x_j)^2}$ is the norm of the $i$-dimensional projection. The angle between the $j^\text{th}$ and $i^\text{th}$ neurons is $\theta_{ij}=\arccos(\frac{\xi_i\xi_j}{x_i x_j})$. By adjusting the angles $\theta_{ij}$, the OFT method can adapt the pretrained network to the task at hand without introducing significant artifacts.

A key advantage of OFT is its ability to maintain the semantic information learned during pretraining. This is demonstrated by experiments where the authors finetune Stable Diffusion (SD) models on various datasets. They find that OFT produces more faithful images compared to LoRA, and that it also outperforms the original SD model in terms of both image quality and diversity.
