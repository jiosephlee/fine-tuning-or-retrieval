# Limitations of LoRA and the importance of preserving neuron pairwise angles

LoRA has become a widely adopted technique for finetuning large pretrained neural networks. However, it suffers from several drawbacks. One significant limitation is its inability to maintain the pairwise angles between neurons after finetuning. To illustrate this, consider two neurons within a layer; if their prefinetuning inner product angle is $\theta_0$, and LoRA applies an additional matrix $A$ to the pretrained weights, the post-finetuning inner product angle becomes $\cos\theta = \frac{A^\top W_0}{\|A\|\|W_0\|}$, where $W_0$ represents the pretrained weights. This means that LoRA cannot guarantee that $\cos\theta$ will equal $\cos\theta_0$. In practice, this can cause the model’s semantic representations to drift, leading to degraded performance. While LoRA does provide some control over the magnitude of these changes by normalizing the added matrix $A$ to have unit norm, it lacks mechanisms to ensure that the pairwise angles between neurons remain unchanged.

Maintaining neuron pairwise angles is crucial because these angles encode much of the semantic information within a layer. For example, in a convolutional layer, the pairwise angles between neurons determine the orientation of filters and thus the semantic content of the features they extract. If the pairwise angles change too much, the semantic meaning of the layer may shift, causing the model to lose the knowledge it acquired during pretraining. Therefore, it is essential to develop techniques that can finetune models while keeping neuron pairwise angles constant.

# Orthogonal Finetuning: Motivation and Overview

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) addresses the challenges associated with LoRA by ensuring that the pairwise angles between all neurons in a layer remain fixed during finetuning. OFT achieves this by learning an invertible orthogonal transformation for each layer, which then transforms that layer’s weights. Concretely, OFT learns a layer-wise orthogonal transformation matrix $R$ that is applied to the pretrained weight matrix $W_0$ to produce a new set of weights $W$:

$W = R \cdot W_0.$

Because $R$ is orthogonal, its transpose equals its inverse, i.e., $R^\top = R^{-1}$. This property ensures that the pairwise angles between every pair of neurons in the layer are preserved exactly. To see this, consider two neurons with prefinetuning inner-product angle $\theta_0$; their post-finetuning inner-product angle is given by $\cos\theta = \|R W_0\|/\|W_0\|$—but since $R^\top R = I$ (where $I$ is the identity matrix), the cosine of the angle remains unchanged: $\cos\theta =\cos\theta_0$.

In contrast to LoRA, which adds a low-rank matrix to the pretrained weights, OFT learns a full-rank orthogonal matrix that scales the columns of $W_0$. This scaling operation is equivalent to learning a linear isometry for each layer, which is a more constrained form of adaptation than LoRA. The orthogonality constraint imposes strong regularity on the learned transformation, making it easier to optimize and preventing the model from collapsing into degenerate solutions.
