# What is LoRA? How does it work?

Low-rank Adapters (LoRA) is a technique introduced by Huang et al. (2021) for finetuning large pretrained neural networks while minimizing catastrophic forgetting. LoRA operates by adding a small number of low-rank weight matrices to the pretrained network’s layers. These added matrices act as adapters, allowing the network to learn new tasks while retaining the knowledge from pretraining. LoRA has become a widely adopted approach for finetuning large language models (LLMs) for downstream tasks such as question answering, summarization, translation, and more.

LoRA introduces a parameterized block of low-rank matrices at each layer of a pretrained neural network. For a given layer with input-output relationship $z = W^0 x$, where $W^0$ is the original weight matrix and $z$ and $x$ are the output and input vectors, respectively, LoRA augments the forward computation with the addition of the following term:

$z = W^0x + \alpha L_1 W^1 x + \beta L_2 x,$

where $L_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $L_2 \in \mathbb{x}^{r \times d}$ are learned low-rank matrices, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are scaling factors, and $r$ is a small integer (e.g., 4). In practice, LoRA typically uses a single shared low-rank matrix per layer rather than learning multiple matrices. To avoid changing the pretraining loss surface, LoRA applies a weighting scheme to the adapter matrices so that their contribution remains small relative to the original pretrained weights. Specifically, LoRA sets the initial learning rate for the adapters to be a fraction of the base learning rate, and the adapters are initialized to zero, effectively disabling them until training begins. During training, LoRA updates the adapters using a scaled version of the loss, ensuring that the contribution of the adapters to the overall loss remains small compared to the pretrained weights. This strategy helps preserve the pretrained model’s original behavior while enabling the model to adapt to new tasks.

LoRA’s effectiveness stems from its ability to maintain a balance between adapting the model to new tasks and retaining the knowledge gained during pretraining. By introducing a small number of trainable parameters, LoRA allows the model to learn new tasks without losing the broad capabilities established during pretraining. LoRA’s simplicity and effectiveness have made it a popular choice for finetuning large-scale models.

# What is OFT? How does it work? Why is it better than LoRA?

Orthogonal Finetuning (or OFT) is a novel finetuning technique designed to adapt text-to-image diffusion models while preserving the semantic content learned during pretraining. Unlike LoRA, which adds low-rank matrices to the pretrained weights, OFT modifies the pretrained weights by applying an orthogonal transformation to them. This transformation maintains the pairwise angles between neurons, thus protecting the semantic information encoded in those angles. The forward computation in OFT is expressed as follows:

$y = W^\top x,$

where $W$ is the orthogonal transformation applied to the original weight matrix $W^0$. The orthogonal transformation is defined as $W = U \Lambda V^\top$, with $U$ and $V$ being orthogonal matrices (i.e., $U^\top = V^\top = I$), and $\Lambda$ being a diagonal matrix containing only positive or zero entries. To ensure that the pretrained semantic information is preserved, OFT constrains the transformation to be orthogonal, meaning that the pairwise angles between the neurons are maintained. This property is crucial because the semantic content of a neural network is often closely tied to the angles between neurons. Maintaining these angles during finetuning allows the model to retain its pretrained semantic knowledge while adapting to new tasks.
