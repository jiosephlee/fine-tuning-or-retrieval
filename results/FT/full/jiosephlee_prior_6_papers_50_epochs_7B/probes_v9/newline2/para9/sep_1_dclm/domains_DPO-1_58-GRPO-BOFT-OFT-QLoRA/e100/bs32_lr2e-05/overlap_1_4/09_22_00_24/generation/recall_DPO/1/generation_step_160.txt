# What is preference learning?

Preference learning is a method for training language models to behave in a way that aligns with human preferences. In this context, we define a reward model as a function $r_\theta(x, y)$ that assigns a scalar score to every completion $y$ of a prompt $x$. The goal is to find a reward model that reflects human preferences over completions. To do this, we collect pairs $(x, y_1, y_2)$ where $y_1$ and $y_2$ are completions of $x$, and a human judge indicates which completion they prefer (or marks them as equivalent). These preference pairs are then used to train the reward model.

There are two main approaches to preference learning: indirect methods and direct methods. Indirect methods optimize the reward model to match a human-provided scoring function or a learned policy. In contrast, direct preference optimization (DPO) directly optimizes the reward model to match human preferences without requiring an intermediate scoring function.

# How does DPO work?

DPO is a direct preference learning algorithm. It works by maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences, given a parametric reward model $r_\theta$. Formally, DPO solves the following optimization problem:

\[
\max_{\theta} \sum_{(x, y_1,y_2, z)} \underbrace{\mathbb{P}(z | x, y_1, yy_2, \theta)}_{\text{Model}} \underbrace{\mathbf{1}(y_1 \succ y_2)}_{\text{Observation}}
\]

Here, $\succ$ denotes a preference relation (for example, "$y_1$ is preferred over $y_2$"). The term $\mathbb{P}(x, y_1 y_2 | z, \theta)$ represents the joint probability assigned by the language model at parameter $\theta$ to the prompt $x$ and the two completions $y_1$ & $y_2$. This joint probability factorizes into the product of a model probability for the prompt $p(x, \theta)$ and a conditional model probability for the completions $p(y_2 | y_1, x, \theta)$. The normalization constant (partition function) is omitted because it is intractable. Instead, the model approximates the normalization constant using Monte Carlo importance sampling. The key idea is that if we can estimate the ratio $\frac{\pi(y_2 | y, x, \theta)}{\pi(y_1 | y, x,  \theta)}$ accurately, we can use it to adjust the relative probabilities of $y_1$ vs $y_2$ to better match human preferences.

The preference ratio $\frac{\pi(Y_2 | Y, X, \theta)}{\π(Y_1 | Y, X, θ)}$ is defined as the ratio of the model log probabilities:

\[
\frac{\pi(y_1, y_3 | x, \theta)} {\pi(y_2, y_3 |  x, \theta)} = \exp\left(\frac{r_\theta(x,  y_1) - r_\theta(x, y_2)}{\epsilon}\right)
\]

This formulation is based on the Bradley-Terry model, which assumes that the difference in reward scores between two completions determines the preference probability. DPO extends the Bradley-Terry model by incorporating a language model prior over completions. The language model prior is learned from unlabeled prompts and completions by maximizing likelihood, resulting in a distribution over completions $p(y | x, \theta)$.

DPO uses a single model $\theta$ to represent both the language model prior and the reward model. The language model prior provides the normalization constant in the reward model, and the reward model is trained by adjusting the prior’s parameters to better reflect human preferences. This shared representation is what gives DPO its efficiency and simplicity.
