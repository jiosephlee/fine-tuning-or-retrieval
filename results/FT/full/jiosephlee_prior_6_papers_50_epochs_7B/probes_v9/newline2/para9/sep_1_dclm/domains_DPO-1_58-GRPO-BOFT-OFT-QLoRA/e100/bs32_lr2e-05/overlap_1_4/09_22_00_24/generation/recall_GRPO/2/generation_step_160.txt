# Evaluation of mathematical reasoning in LLMs

Evaluation of mathematical reasoning in LMs is a challenging task due to the complexity of mathematical reasoning itself. Traditional benchmarks often focus on basic arithmetic or multiple-choice questions, but more advanced mathematical reasoning tasks are rarely covered. In this section, we discuss several recent approaches to evaluating mathematical reasoning in LLM models.

One approach is to create new benchmarks that test mathematical reasoning skills. For example, the GSM8K benchmark evaluates models' ability to solve grade-school level math problems using natural language. It contains 8,000 questions split into 8,000 train, 1,000 validation, and 1,000 test questions. Each question has multiple possible answers, and the correct answer is selected from these options. The MATH benchmark, introduced by the MetaMath benchmark challenge, tests models’ proficiency in solving competition-level mathematics problems. The dataset consists of 50,000 multiple-choice questions covering topics from elementary to university-level mathematics. Another recently proposed benchmark, CMATH, focuses on competition-level mathematics problems in Chinese. It includes 50,000 multiple choice questions spanning various mathematical areas. These benchmarks are designed to assess models’ mathematical reasoning abilities beyond simple arithmetic.

Another strategy is to adapt existing benchmarks for mathematical reasoning evaluation. For instance, the Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark, which covers a wide range of subjects, can be used to evaluate mathematical reasoning by treating each math-related question as a separate task. Similarly, the BigBench Benchmark, which targets database applications, can be utilized to assess models’ mathematical problem-solving skills. Other benchmarks, such as BBH and BBH-2, were created specifically to evaluate models’ reasoning capabilities in general and in mathematical contexts, respectively.

However, these benchmarks have limitations. Most rely on multiple-choice formats, which may not fully reflect the complexity of mathematical reasoning. Moreover, the evaluation of mathematical reasoning in LMS is often based on the accuracy rate, which does not provide insight into the reasoning process or the steps taken to arrive at an answer. To address this, researchers have developed tools like Llemma and LoRA to analyze the reasoning process behind chain-of-thought-based solutions. Llemma parses the reasoning chain within the text and identifies the logical flow leading to the final answer. LoRA, on the other hand, uses program-based reasoning to verify the correctness of the solution. These methods allow for a deeper analysis of the reasoning process and offer insights into the strengths and weaknesses of models.

Additionally, human evaluation remains an important tool for assessing mathematical reasoning in LLM. While automated evaluation is faster and cheaper, it may not always reflect the nuances of mathematical reasoning. Therefore, combining automated evaluation with human evaluation can provide a more comprehensive picture of a model’s mathematical reasoning capabilities.

Overall, there is no single universally accepted evaluation protocol for mathematical reasoning in LLM, and the field continues to evolve as new benchmarks and evaluation methods are developed.
