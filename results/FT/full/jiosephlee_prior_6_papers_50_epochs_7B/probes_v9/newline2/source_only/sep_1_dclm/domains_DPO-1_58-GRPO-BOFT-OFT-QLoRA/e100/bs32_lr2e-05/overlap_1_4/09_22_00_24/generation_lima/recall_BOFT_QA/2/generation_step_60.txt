The paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" introduces Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) to improve parameter-efficiency over standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its block-diagonal approach. What is Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)?<|EOT|>Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), as described in the paper, is a parameter-efficient variant of Orthogonal Finetuning that uses butterfly structures to combine multiple low-rank matrices into a single dense matrix with a specific block-diagonal structure. BOFT leverages this structure to perform orthogonal transformation on the weight matrix while achieving better parameter efficiency than OFT.

To understand BOFT, we first need to review OFT. OFT performs an orthogonal transformation on a pretrained weight matrix by adding a block-diagonal matrix of orthogonal matrices to the original weight matrix. The diagonal matrices are chosen so that the pre-activation has minimal effect on certain dimensions of the weight matrix. This allows the activation to vary independently across different dimensions of the weight matrix, thereby preventing co-adaptation. While OFT is parameter-efficient compared to LoRA, it still has a significant number of parameters due to the block-diagonal structure. To address this, the block-diagonal structure of OFT is replaced with a product of multiple low-rank matrices in BOFT. However, this results in a dense matrix with a large number of parameters. To address this, BOFT uses a butterfly structure to represent the product of multiple low-ranks matrices. 

A $d$-dimensional butterfly structure is defined as
\[
B(d, r, k) = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
r & 1 & 0 & \ddots & 0 \\
0 & r & 1 & \ddots & \
0 \\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots& \ddots &  0 \\
0 & \cdots& 0 & r &  1
\end{bmatrix}^{k}
\]
for $r \in [0,1]$ and $k \in \mathbb{N}$. By recursively applying the butterfly matrix, we can express any matrix with size $d \times d$ and rank greater than $k$ using a product of $O(\log d)$ matrices each with size $d \ times d$ and rank $r$. For example, a $d \times d$, rank-$r$ matrix can be written as
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
a & * \\
* & A
\end{bmaptrix} =
\begin{bmatrix*}[r] 
1 & 0 & * \\
0 & B(d, r, 2) & 1 \\
* & 1 &  0
\end{bmatrx*}[r]
\begin{bmatrix} a \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix*}
\]
multiplied $O(\log d) = O(\log 512) \approx 10$ times. In practice, BOFT uses a sparse matrix representation with $s \log d$ non-zero entries, where $s$ is the number of bits to represent a integer (e.g., 8). This reduces the number of parameters from $\Omega(d^2)$ to $\Omega(d^2 / \log d)$.<|endoftext|>
