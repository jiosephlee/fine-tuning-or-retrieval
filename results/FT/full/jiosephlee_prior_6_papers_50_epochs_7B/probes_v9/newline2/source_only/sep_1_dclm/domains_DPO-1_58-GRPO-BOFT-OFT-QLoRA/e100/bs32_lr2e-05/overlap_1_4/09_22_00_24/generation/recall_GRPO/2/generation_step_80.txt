# Group Relative Policy Optimization ( GRPO )

Proximal Policy Optimization (PGPO) has become the de-facto algorithm for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) due to its stability and sample efficiency. However, PGPO is computationally expensive since it typically requires storing a soft value function and an action-value function for all pairs of current and past policies, amounting to O(N^2) memory where N is the number of policies. This makes PGPO impractical for scenarios where there are many policies, such as large-scale multi-agent training.

To address this issue, the authors propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRAMPO), later renamed to Group Relative Policy Optimization (AGRPO) and further refined to GRPO in the DeepSeekMath paper. GRPO reduces the memory footprint fromO(N^2) toO(N) by optimizing policies relative to groups of policies rather than to individual policies. Specifically, GRPO optimizes policies by minimizing the following objective:

$J_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}{[q \sim P(Q)]}\left[\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\pi \in G}\underbrace{(q\pi-\beta V^\pi_q)}_{\text{surrogate loss for group}} - \alpha\lambda(V_\theta-V)\right]$,

where $\theta$ denotes the parameters of the policy $\pi_\theta$, $P(Q)$ is the dataset of trajectories sampled from the prior policy $π$, $V$ is the value function, $V_θ$ is the value function learned by the policy $\pi_θ$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are hyperparameters, and $G$ is a group of policies randomly sampled from the prior policy. The first term inside the brackets is the surrogate loss for policy optimization, and the second term is a penalty term that ensures consistency with the original value function. Intuitively, the group relative surrogate loss encourages the expected reward of policy $\pi$ in group $G$ to be higher than the average reward of policies in $G$. Compared to PPO, GRPO has one less pairwise constraint, which makes it more sample-efficient. Moreover, GRPO is naturally suited for multi-agent reinforcement learning, where it easily scales to large numbers of policies since the value function for a group of policies requires storage that is linear in the number of policies.

In the DeepSeekMath paper, GRPO replaces the standard PPO algorithm in the RLHF training loop. Instead of applying GRPO in the general context of continuous control, the authors tailor it to the specific task of mathematical reasoning. More specifically, they modify the reward function used to rank policies and the value function used in the penalty term. The modified reward function is the fraction of examples in the mathematical dataset where the policy's answer is correct; the value function is the empirical average of this reward function. With these modifications, GRPO becomes well-suited for training LLaMA on mathematical tasks.

# Training DeepSeekMath with GRPO

The authors train three versions of DeepSeekMath using different training regimes. The first version, DeepSeekMath-RL, is trained with reinforcement learning using the MATH dataset. The second version, DeepSeekMath-GT, is trained with generative tuning using the same MATH dataset. The third version, DeepSeekMath-Basic, is the base model (LLaMA 65B) that is not subject to any special training. All three versions are initialized with LLaMA 65b.

For DeepSeekMath-RL and DeepSeekMath-GT the authors use the top-k sampling strategy, where the model samples from the top ranking tokens (k=128 in this case) to generate an answer. For DeepSeekMath-Basic the authors use greedy decoding to generate answers.

DeepSeekMath-RL is trained with GRPO for 10k iterations on the MATH dataset. Each iteration processes approximately 4M training examples. The reward used in GRPO is the fraction of problems where the model's answer is correct. The value function used in the penality term is the empirical average of the reward function.
