The paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" introduces Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) to improve parameter-efficiency over standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its block-diagonal approach. What is Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)?<|EOT|>Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), as described in the paper, is a parameter-efficient variant of Orthogonal Finetuning that prevents the collapse of orthogonal matrices to a single connected component. BOFT uses a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices to achieve the same orthogonality preservation effect as OFT, while being more parameter-efficient. The key insight behind BOFT is to use a product of multiple sparse matrices, each matrix containing only 1s on the diagonal and some 1s outside the diagonal. Each matrix in the product is a butterfly matrix, hence the name "Butterfly". To be more specific, BOFT constructs $r$ orthogonal matrices, each of size $d/2^i \times d/2^i$, such that
\[
    \mathbf{R} = \prod_{i=1}^r \mathbf{B}_i,
\]
where $\mathbf{B}_1,\cdots,\mathbf{B}_r$ are butterfly matrices. This way, $\text{rank}(\mathbf{R}) = \prod_{i=r}^1 d/2^i = d/\prod_{i=1 }^r 2^i = d^{1/r}$ which is much smaller than $d$ but still full rank. By using a product of $r$ matrices, BOFT achieves the same parameter efficiency as OFBT's block-diagonal approach without dividing the model parameters into blocks.

The number $r$ is a trade-off between parameter efficiency and the number of matrix multiplications. With a smaller $r$, we have a larger factorized form of the model, which increases the computational cost of finetuning. With a larger $r$, the parameter efficiency improves, but the performance may not be as good as using a smaller $r$.

In the original implementation, we use $r=4$ for 16-bit training and $r=3$ for 32-bit training. These values were chosen empirically to find a good balance between parameter efficiency and performance.<|endoftext|>
