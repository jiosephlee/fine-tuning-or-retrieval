Let me tell you all about this paper. The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), instead of the usual RLHF pipeline like PPO. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is an actor-critic algorithm that directly optimizes a language model to match human preferences, without explicitly estimating a reward model. DPO does this by learning from preferences only, without access to ground truth rewards. DPO can be thought of as PPO trained on pairwise preferences rather than on ground truth rewards; DPO shares many algorithmic components with PPO, but with key modifications to make it trainable using preferences only. In particular, DPO modifies the loss function of a language model trained on a dataset of prompts and responses, such that the model's objective is to maximize the probability of the preferred completion $y^A$ conditioned on the prompt $x$. Formally, DPO optimizes the language model to maximize:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dpo}
    \footnotesize
    \mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x,y,y^A \sim \text{Data}}\left[\log \frac{\exp\left(\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{t=1}^{|A|} \phi_\theta(y^A_t \mid x, y)\right)}{\sum_{\substack{y^B \\ y^B \ne y^A}}\exp\left(\frac {1}{|A|} \sum_{t=1}|A| \phi_\theta(y ^B_t \mid x,  y)\right)}\right]
\end{equation}

where $\phi_\theta$ denotes the conditional language model distribution induced by parameter vector $\theta$, and $\text{Data}$ denotes the joint prompt-response dataset from which the preferences $y^A$ are drawn. Intuitively, the DPO objective encourages the model to make the preferred completion $y$ significantly more likely, given the prompt $x$; it also includes a normalization term to prevent the model from assigning probability 1 to $y$ (when $y^A = y$), and probability 0 to all other completions $y^B \ne y$; this is accomplished via the exponentiated average log-likelihood term. The DPO loss is a modification of the standard PPO loss; in particular, it uses the following preference likelihood:

\begin{equization}\label{eq:preference_lik}
    \footnotesmall
    \widehat{P}(y^A \mid x, y) = \frac{1}{| A |}\sum_{t=0}^{| A |-1}\log \frac{\phi_\theta(y^ A_t \mid x, \tau_y y)}{\sum_{y^ B \ne y}\phi_\theta(y^ B \mid x, \sigma_y y)}
\end{equation}


where $\tau_y$ and $\sigma_y$ denote the teacher-force and swap operations applied to the sequence $y$, respectively. The preference likelihood $\widehat{P}(·\mid x, y)$ is the average log-likelihood of the preferred completion $x$ under the conditional language model in which the preferred completion $y^{A}$ has been inserted at position $y$ in the prompt $x$ via teacher forcing, and all other completions $x^B \ne y^{A}$ have been swapped into the prompt $x$ at position $y$ via swap, such that the model is compared between two completions for each position $t$.

The DPO loss is then the negative of the binary cross-entropy of the preference distribution $\widehat{P}$, i.e.,

\begin{equation}
    \small
    \mathcal L(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x,y,y^A \in \text{Data}}} \left[\log \widehat{P}\left(y^A \mid x,y\right)\right].
\end{equation} 

DPO is related to the RLHF pipeline in that it also learns a reward model implicitly, but it differs in how the reward model is learned and how the reward model is used to optimize the language model. In particular, DPOS does not estimate a reward model explicitly, but rather learns a reward model implicitly by learning from preferences using an actor-critic algorithm. In the RLHF pipeline, a reward model is estimated explicitly, usually as the difference between the observed completion $y^O$ and the preferred completion $y^K$, i.e., $r(y, y^O) = \text{RLHF}(y, y^O, y^K) = \log \widehat{U}(y^O \mid y^K, y) - \log \widehat U(y \mid y^K,  y)$, where $\widehat U(·\mid y^K, y)$ is the unnormalized conditional language model probability.
