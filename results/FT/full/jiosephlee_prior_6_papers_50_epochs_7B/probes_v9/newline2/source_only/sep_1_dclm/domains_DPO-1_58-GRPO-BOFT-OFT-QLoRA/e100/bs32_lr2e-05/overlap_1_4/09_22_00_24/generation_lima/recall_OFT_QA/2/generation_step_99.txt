Orthogonal Finetuning (or OFT for short) is a method to finetune neural networks, while preserving the geometry of the pretrained model. The key idea behind OFT is to perform finetuning by changing only the direction of the neurons, while keeping their length constant. This is achieved by orthogonal rotation. To finetune a neural network using OFT, we first freeze all neurons of the pretrained model, and then we rotate their directions to form the finetuned model. The rotation matrix is derived from the following linear transformation:

$R = I - \frac{1}{2}ab^\top,$

where $a$ and $b$ are vectors representing how the finetuner modifies the properties of the pretrained model. Compared to LoRA, OFT can be viewed as an alternative way to construct an orthogonal matrix for layer resizing. Unlike LoRA, OFT does not require additional low-rank parameters.

# Why is it called "Orthogonal Finetuning"?

The name "Orthogonal Finetunig" comes from two aspects. First, the rotation matrix used in OFT is orthogonal, i.e., $R^\top = R^{-1} = R$. This is in contrast to LoRA, where the low-rank parameter $L$ violates this property. Second, OFT only rotates neurons, without changing their lengths. In finetuning, the term "finetuning" usually means a slight change, and we consider that the neurons' lengths in the pretrained model are already close to the optimal lengths. So finetuning by changing the lengths is not allowed. Instead, we only allow rotations, which keeps the lengths unchanged. Therefore, we call this method "Orthogonal Finetunable".

# How is OFT different from LoRA?

LoRA and OFT are both methods to finetune neural network models while preserving the geometrical structure of neurons in the pretrained model. Both methods achieve this by re-scaling (rescaling in LoRA, fixing in OFT) and potentially changing the direction of neurons. However, there are also some key differences between them.

First, LoRA performs simultaneous rescaling and rotating, while OFT fixes the lengths and only rotates. To see the difference, we can derive the direction of neurons after finetuning with both methods. For simplicity, we consider a neuron vector $v$ in the pretrained model, and we want to obtain its direction after finetuning along a certain direction $d$. With LoRA, the direction of the neuron is:

$R_v = v(1 - \alpha L) + \frac{\alpha}{\|d\|} d \cdot \mathrm{sign}(d^\top v),$

where $\alpha$ is a scalar representing how much the finetuner changes the neuron compared to the pretrained model, and $ \mathrm{sign}(x) = x / \|x\| $ is the unit vector of $x$. With OFT, the direction of the same neuron is simply:

$R_v^OFT = R_{v, pretrained} (I - \frac{1}2 v d^\top)^{-1},$

which clearly does not scale $v$. We can see that when using OFT, the length of $v$ is fixed, and the direction of $v$ is rotated around the length of $v$. While with LoRA, the length of $vj$ is changed, and the direction of$vj$ is rotated around its new length.

Second, OFT has a more straightforward interpretation than LoRA. In OFT, each neuron is rotated independently without being scaled. This is easy to interpret: a neuron's direction is rotated, but not its length. In contrast, the rescaling operation in LoRA makes it more difficult to interpret what part of the neuron is changed during finetuning.

Third, OFT does not introduce any additional parameters. This is because the rotation matrix for each neuron can be derived from a linear transformation $I - \frac12 v d^\top$. In contrast, LoRA introduces $L$ additional low-rank parameters for each neuron.

Fourth, OFT can be seen as a special case of LoRA with $\alpha = 1$ and $L = 0$. So OFT is a restricted version of LoRA. But the converse is not true. So all results obtained using OFT can be derived using LoRA, but not vice versa.

Fifth, OFT can be easily applied to text-to-image diffusion models. This is because OFT only rotates neurons without changing their lengths, so it does not cause any obvious problems with the size of images. In contrast, LoRA may cause some problems with the size of neurons in the latent space, especially when $\alpha$ is not equal to 1.
