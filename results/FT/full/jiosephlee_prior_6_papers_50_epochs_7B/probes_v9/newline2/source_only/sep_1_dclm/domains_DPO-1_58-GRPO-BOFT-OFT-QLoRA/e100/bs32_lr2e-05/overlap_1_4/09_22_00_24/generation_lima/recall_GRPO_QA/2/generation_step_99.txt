What is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?<|EOT|>Policy Optimization Algorithms
To understand GRPO, we first need to understand two policy optimization algorithms that build on top of actor-critic algorithms: PPO and SFT.

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
PPO is a reinforcement learning algorithm that can be used for training deep learning policies in a sequential decision making problem, i.e. in an environment with a discrete action at each time step. It is an improvement over the original actor-critic algorithms because it reduces the sensitivity of the training to the ratio of rewards between different trajectories. The core idea behind PPO is to optimize the policy while constraining its new expected return to be close to the old one. This constraint is achieved by adding a surrogate clipping objective, the so-called "surrogate loss", which prevents the policy from taking huge jumps into untested territory. More formally, given a reward model $r$ and a policy $\pi_{\theta}$, the surrogate loss for a trajectory $y$ is:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t=y}[r(\pi_{\theta}(o_t \mid t)) - \mathrm{clip}(\frac{\pi_{\theta}({o_t \mid t} | t) - \mu}{\mu}, -0.1, 0.1) ]
\]
where $\mu$ is the old mean trajectory.
However, the standard PPO has a major drawback: it requires storing the old trajectories during training, which can be expensive in large language models where the memory footprint is already large. To address this issue, SFT was proposed.

Socially-Friendly Training (SFT)
SFT is a memory-efficient version of PPO that only needs to store the best trajectory among many instead of all trajectories. The idea is to compute the surrogate loss on the best trajectory at each time step, without needing to store all past trajectories. Formally, the SFT surrogate loss for a single step is:
\[
\mathrm{clip}(\pi_{\theta} - \mu, -0.1,0.1) \cdot \mathbb{E}[r(\pi_{_\theta}(\cdot \mid t))]
\]
where the expectation is taken with respect to the best trajectory up to time $t$. While SFT is more memory efficient than PPO, it still has a limitation when it comes to scaling with the context length. This is where GRPO comes in.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GR PO)
GRPO is designed to be a memory-efficient version of SFT that can scale with the context length. The key idea behind GRPO is to compute the surrogate losses not relative to the best full trajectory, but rather to the best partial trajectory at each position. More precisely, the best $k$-step trajectory is found for every $k$ and then the surrogate loss is computed relative to that best $k$--step trajectory. Mathematically, for a given trajectory $y$ of arbitrary length $n$, the GRPO surrogate loss is:
\[
\sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb{E}_k[r(\pi_{\theta}\mid y_k) - \mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{_\theta}\mid y_k - \mu_k}{\mu_k}, -0.1,\, 0.1\right) ]
\]
where the expectation $\mathbb{E}_i$ is taken with respect to a group of trajectories of length $k$ starting from position $i$, and $\mu_k$ denotes the mean of these $k$ trajectories. In contrast to SFT, GRPO does not require storing the best full trajectory, only the best partial trajectories at each position. This makes GRPO well-suited for training in large language models with long contexts, since only $O(c)$ best partial trajectories need to be stored, where $c$ is the context length, as opposed to the full best trajectory which would be $O(n^2)$ with $n^2$ the number of tokens in the batch.
