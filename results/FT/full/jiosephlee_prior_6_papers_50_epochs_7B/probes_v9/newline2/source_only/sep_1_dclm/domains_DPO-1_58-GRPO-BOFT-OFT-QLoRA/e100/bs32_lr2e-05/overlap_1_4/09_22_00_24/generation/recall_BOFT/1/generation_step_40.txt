# Motivation behind orthogonal finetuning

Orthogonal finetuning is a method that fine-tunes a pretrained weight matrix to preserve the pretrained angle while allowing the magnitude to be adapted. The motivation comes from the observation that the semantic information of a neural network is often encoded in the angles of neurons, while the magnitudes are more likely to be noise brought by the random initialization and training process. By freezing the magnitudes (or preserving them as much as possible) and only adapting the angles, we can achieve better generalization and avoid overfitting.

To see why preserving angles matters, consider a neuron with weight matrix $W^0$ that has already been trained to encode certain semantic information. The pre-activation of this neuron is typically computed as $x = W^0 z$, where $z$ is the input vector. The angle of this neuron is determined by the direction of $x$, which is largely dictated by the angle of $W^0 z$. When we fine-tune the model, the angle of $W^{ft}$, the fine-tuned weight matrix, will change, and thus the angle of $W_{pre}^0 z$ will also change even if $z$ remains the same. This means the semantic information encoded by the neuron may be lost during finetuning because its angle has changed.

One solution is to freeze the pretrained weight matrix $W^1$ and add a learnable bias matrix $B$ such that the pre-activation is $x = W^1 z + B$. Since $B$ has no angle, it won't affect the angle of $W_1^1 z$. This approach does preserve the angle, but it lacks flexibility: the added bias matrix can only move along the line defined by $W^1 z$, and it is difficult to optimize a matrix to move on such a low-dimensional manifold.

Another solution is to perform SVD on $W^1$ to obtain three matrices $U \Sigma V^\top$ and replace $W^1$ with $V \Sigma ^{1/2} U^\top$. This preserves all the angles of $W^1$ perfectly and makes the matrix flexible enough to move in a high-dimensional space. However, this approach is not parameter-efficient since it discards the orthogonal matrix $U$ and uses the dense matrix $V \Sigma ^ {1/2} U ^\top$ instead. To be parameter-efficient, one may choose to learn only $\Sigma ^{1/3}$ out of $\Sigma ^{1 / 2}$, but this introduces nonlinearity into the angle parameters, making optimization more difficult.

The orthogonal finetuning (OFT) method proposed by Huang et al. directly learns an orthogonal matrix to transform the input, i.e., $x = W^T z$, where $W$ is an orthogonal matrix that is initialized as the transpose of the pretrained weight matrix $ W^0 = (W^T) ^0$. OFT perfectly preserves the angles of $W^{ft} = (W^T)^{ft}$, and it is parameter-efficient. Despite these advantages, OFT suffers from a serious problem: it is difficult to optimize the orthogonal constraint, and the standard approach is to add a penalty term to the loss function to discourage deviation from orthogonality. The penalty term often contains the Frobenius norm of the gradient of the angular part, which makes the gradient hard to control and slows down convergence.

To address this issue, the authors propose to parameterize the orthogonal matrix with Cayley parametrization, which avoids using the Frobenius norm penalty and is easier to optimize. Before introducing the Cayley parameterization, let's first understand why we need a parameterization at all.
