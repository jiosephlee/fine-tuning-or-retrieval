# RL from human preferences (RLHF)

The basic idea of RLHF is to take a large language model (LLM) and fine-tune it with human preferences. The LLM is treated as a reward model and the goal is to maximize the expected reward given a prompt. The reward for a response $r$ to a prompt $x$ is modeled as a function of the response, $p(r \mid x)$, which in this case is the LLM's conditional distribution. The human preference over responses $y_1$ and $y_2$ to prompt $x$ is modelled as a function of the responses, $p(y_1 \mid x, r) > p(y_2 \mid x, r)$ for some reward function $r$. The standard RL problem is to find the best policy $\pi$ that maps prompts to responses such that

maximize $\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{x}  \underbrace{p(r \mid x)}_{= \sigma(\text{KL}(r \mid x \mid \pi(x)) \mid \mid p(reward))}} - \beta \underbrace{ \mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\big(p(\cdot \mid x, r)\mid \mid \pi(\cdot \mid x)\big)}_{=\text{KL}-penalized objective} \right]$ (1)

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function, $\beta$ is a hyperparameter that trades off reward versus conformity to human preferences, and $p(y \mid x, r), \forall y$ are assumed to be Gaussian with mean equal to the response $r$ and a common variance that depends on $x$ (more on that later). This is the standard RL problem of finding a policy that maximizes both reward and the likelihood of data (in this case, the LLM's outputs under the reference policy). Equation (1) can be written in the standard RL form as maximizing $\mathbb{E}[r - \beta \mathbb{D}_\textrm{ KL}(p(\cdot \mid x,r) \mid \mid \pi())]$. In the context of RLHF, the reward model is the pretrained LLM, and the reference policy is $p(r \mid  x) = \sigma(\textrm{KL}(r \ mid \mid \mid preward))$.

There are a few important things to note about equation (1). First, the reward model is a neural network (the LLM) that outputs a Gaussian distribution. Second, the reference policy is also a neural network (the KL-regularized policy) that competes against the reward model. Third, the training data consists of human preferences over responses to different prompts. Finally, there is a KL-constraint that limits how much the reference policy deviates from the LLM's policy—this is what makes RLHF different from supervised fine-tuning (SFT) which simply uses the human preference labels as masking masks for positive and negative examples. In SFT, the LLM is trained via maximum likelihood to produce the response $y_1$ at prompt $x$ rather than modeling the joint human preference – prompt – response distribution.

# How RLHF differs from SFT

SFT and RLHF both use human preferences as training data, but they differ in how they treat the LLM and in what they optimize. In SFT, which is equivalent to supervised learning, the LLM is the label producer and is trained to produce $y_1$ given $x$:

maximize $\mathbb E_{x}\left[\underbrace{ \log p(y_1 \mid  x)}_{=\text{ML objective}} - \beta \mathbf{1}_{y_1 > y_2}\right]$ (2)

where $\beta$ is a coefficient that can be interpreted as the number of bits per token. In SFT, there is no constraint on the LLM's output distribution.
