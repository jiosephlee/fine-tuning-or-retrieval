Orthogonal Finetuning (or OFT for short) is a method to finetune neural networks, while preserving the geometry of the pretrained model. The key idea behind OFT is to perform finetuning by only rotating and translating neurons, i.e. transformations in the orthogonal group ($\mathbf{O}$). More precisely, OFT only uses rotations ($\mathbf{R}$) combined with translations. This is in contrast to standard finetuning, where the training algorithm generally tries to optimize the pre-activation of a neuron, i.e. when the bias is set to zero. The authors also introduce a parameter-efficient version called OFT*.

# Why is it important to preserve the neuron activation distribution during finetuning?

When you finetune a pretrained model, you usually want to keep the good performance of the pretrained model, while adapting it to new data. If you finetune a neural network by training all weights, you are essentially relearning the task from scratch, which can drastically change the neuron activations. For example, if you have a neuron with activation vector $[0.5, 0.5]$ for images of dogs looking to the left, and $[-0.5,-0.5]$ for dogs looking to the right, then after finetuning this neuron could end up with activation $[1,1]$ for both directions, or $[0,2]$ for the left direction, depending on the new data and training objective. While we know that a single neuron cannot represent a semantic concept, these examples illustrate that changing the neuron activation distributions can have negative effects. In particular, the OFT paper mentions three problems:

1. Semantic information: A change in the neuron activation distribution can lead to a change in the semantics represented by a neuron or its connections to other neurons. For example, a neuron representing the concept dog in the pretrained model might represent cats after finetuning, even if the concept cat was not present in the finetuning data.
2. Topology: The topology of a neuron's activation hypersphere changes when only its center changes, but changes dramatically when also its direction changes. Since the relative positions of neurons encode important information, changing the activation vectors can destroy this information.
3. Generalization: Changing the neuron activation distributions can make it harder for the model to generalize, because the pretrained knowledge is lost.

To summarize, preserving the neuron activation distribution during standard finetuning is important, because it prevents the model from losing the pretrained knowledge, and because it avoids changing the semantics represented by individual neurons or their connections to other neurons, as well as the topology of the neuron activation hyperspheres.

# How does OFT work? Provide a simple mathematical example.

The core idea behind OFT is that any linear transformation in Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be decomposed into an orthogonal matrix ($\mathbf{RR}^\top = \mathbf{I}$) followed by a translation ($\mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$), and vice versa ($\mathbf{x + b} \mapsto \text{rotate}(\mathbf{x + b})$). Therefore, instead of updating the weights directly, OFT updates a rotation matrix that will be applied to the weights at a later time. This decomposition is written as:
$$
\mathbf{W} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R} & \mathbf{b}\\\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{l}\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R)^\top}=\mathbf{I},\qquad \mathbf{l}^\top\mathrf{l} = \mathbf{l}\mathbf{l)^\top = \mathbb{I}^n,
$$
where $\mathbf{W}$ are the weights, $\mathbf{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, $\mathbf{l}$ is a vector of offsets (i.e. a translation), and $\mathbf{b}$ is the bias due to the orthogonal decomposition that arises when $n>1$. The orthogonal matrix and translation are learned, while all other elements of $\mathbf{W)$ are set to zero. The key point is that the orthogonal matrix is not applied to the data yet, but rather stored for future use. This way, the geometry of the weight hypersphere is preserved, since $\mathbf{R}^{-1} = \mathbf{R}^{\top}$ for an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R},\;$ and no information is lost through the decomposition.
