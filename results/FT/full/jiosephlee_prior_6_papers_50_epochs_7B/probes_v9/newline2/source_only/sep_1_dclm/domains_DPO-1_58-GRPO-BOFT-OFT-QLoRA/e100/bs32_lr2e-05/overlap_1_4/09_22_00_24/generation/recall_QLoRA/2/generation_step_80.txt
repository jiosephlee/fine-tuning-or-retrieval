The key insight behind QLoRA is that the trade-off between quantization resolution and memory savings can be reclaimed through better management of the requantization step at inference. This is done by separating the weight matrix into two parts: a small reference weight matrix kept at high precision, and a parameterized affine transformation that maps the low-bit representation to the high-bit representation. At training time, the low-bit representation is used, but the computation is structured so that the quantization error can be recovered at inference time through the parameterized affine transformation. In other words, the low-bit training representation is equivalent to the high-bit representation at inference time because the quantization error is compensated for by the affine transformation.

These two components together compose the Affine Transformation that maps the 4-bit quantized weights to 16-bit weights. To do this, we first need to quantify the error introduced by quantization. Consider a weight $w$ with value $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ that is to be quantized to a 4-bit integer $q$. \[
q = \mathrm{round}\left(\frac{x}{s} + 0.5\right),
\]
and the quantization introduces a quantization error
\[
e = s\left(q - \mathrm{round}(q)\right) = s\left(q-\mathbf{1}[q\ge 0]\cdot s\right),
\]
where $\mathbf{1}$ is the indicator function. For a signed quantizer, we simply replace $0.5$ with the zero-points for the positive and negative ranges.

The key insight of QLoRA is to quantize the weight $w$ in terms of a known affine transformation. That is, instead of quantizing directly to $q$, we quantize to $w + b$ where $b$ is a vector of small integers representing the bits that will be shifted by the affine transformation, and $e$ will be scaled by $s$; formally,
\[

\[

where $\tilde{w}$ denotes the signed weight, and $q = s(b-\mathbf{1}(b\ge 0) - 1)$ for a signed quantizer.
