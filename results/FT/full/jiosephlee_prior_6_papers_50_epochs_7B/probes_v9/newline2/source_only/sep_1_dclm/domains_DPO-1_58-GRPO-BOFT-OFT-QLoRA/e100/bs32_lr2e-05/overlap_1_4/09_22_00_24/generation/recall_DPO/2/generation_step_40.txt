Introduction

Large language models (LLM) have shown remarkable ability in many natural language tasks, but their performance degrades when the context is short. Prompt tuning and instruction tuning are two common approaches to mitigate this problem. While prompt tuning modifies a pretrained LLM to change its behavior according to user instructions, instruction tuning fine-tunes a pretrained LLM on a dataset of prompts and human responses. In this chapter, we will study instruction tuning in detail, starting with the motivation behind it, the related work, the data sources, and the evaluation protocols. We will then walk through the process of instruction tuning, from data collection to model evaluation, with a focus on the choices and trade-offs involved in each step. Finally, we will discuss the limitations of instruction tuning and possible future directions.

# Motivation for instruction tuning

The motivation for instruction tuning comes from the observation that prompt tuning, despite its simplicity and effectiveness, has some limitations. First, prompt tuning is sensitive to the choice of prompt templates. Different templates can lead to significantly different behaviors, and finding a good template is nontrivial. Second, prompt tuning can be unstable, especially when the training data contains conflicting instructions. Third, prompt tuning can be limited by the available memory; training with a large dataset of instructions can easily exceed the memory capacity of a single machine. Instruction tuning addresses these issues by learning to imitate the behavior of humans following instructions, which makes it more robust and flexible than prompt tuning.

Instruction tuning is also motivated by the fact that it is a more realistic setting for applying reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). In practice, most datasets of human responses to prompts are already available in the format of human labels rather than machine completions. Using such datasets for instruction tuning is equivalent to treating human preferences over completions as rewards, which is the standard setting in RLHF. In addition, instruction tuning allows us to study the trade-off between the size of the language model and the size of the training dataset, and understand how much we can scale up both the model and the training data before we see diminishing returns.

# Related work

The most relevant work to instruction tuning is RLHF, especially the approach of using a reward model to estimate the reward of a completion. In RLHF, a reward model is trained on the same data that the language model is trained on, and then the language model is trained to maximize the expected reward with a policy optimization algorithm such as proximal policy optimization (PPO). The main difference between RLHF and instruction tuning is the source of the reward signal. In RLHF, the reward signal is provided by a reward model, while in instruction tuning, the reward signal is the human preference over model completions. Another line of related work is preference-based reinforcement learning, which uses a preference model to estimate the preference distribution over completions and optimize the policy directly. This approach is similar to instruction tuning, but it usually requires collecting new preferences over completions from a preference model, rather than using existing human preferences.
