The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the reinforcement learning approach to the case of preference learning from discrete rewards, which is known as the reward alignment problem. The authors propose a method called DPO, which is based on a reward model that is learned from human preferences. DPO is shown to be effective in aligning language models with preferences, outperforming previous approaches like PPO-based RLHF and baselines such as RLHF with a reward model learned from human feedback. The DPO algorithm is also efficient, requiring only a single pass over the preference data, and is easy to implement in practice. The paper is accompanied by a set of experiments that demonstrate the effectiveness of DPO on a range of tasks, including summarization, dialogue, and translation. In summary, the paper makes the following contributions: 1) A general framework for learning reward models from human preferences, which can be applied to a wide range of preference learning scenarios. 2) A novel algorithm, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), that learns a reward model from human preferences, without relying on any explicit human feedback. 3) Experiments on a range of preference learning tasks, showing that DPO aligns language models with human preferences more effectively than previous approaches.

Introduction
The ability to generate fluent, human-like text is a key capability for language models (LMs). However, current LMs are trained on large corpora of text, which may not reflect the kinds of texts people actually prefer. In order to address this, a number of approaches have been proposed to fine-tune LMs using human preferences. These methods typically involve training an LM to maximize a reward function, which is defined in terms of the preference data. This approach is known as reinforcement learning (RL), and is a well-established technique for learning from rewards. In the context of language, the reward function is typically learned using a reward model, which is a generative model that maps text to a probability distribution over human preferences. The reward model is trained using a dataset of human preference pairs, where one text is generated by the LM and another is selected by the human annotator. The reward model is then used to guide the training of the LM, so that it generates text that is preferred by the human annotators.
The most widely used approach for learning reward models from preference data is reinforcement learning with policy optimization (RL-PO), which is a variant of the standard RL algorithm PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). In RL-PO, the reward function is represented as a policy π, which is optimized using RL. The policy is typically initialized with the pre-trained LM, and the reward model is updated using a reward signal derived from the preference data. The RL-PO approach has been shown to yield high-quality reward models, but it requires access to a large number of preference pairs, which can be challenging to collect. In this work, we introduce a new method for learning reward models from preferences, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (D PO). D PO does not require access to preference data, and instead learns a reward model directly from the preference data. This is achieved by optimizing a loss that is equivalent to the KL-divergence between the reward model and the reference reward model, while constraining the reward model to match the preference data. We refer to this as the DPO loss. D PO is a straightforward extension of the RL-PO framework, and can be implemented using off-the-shelf RL algorithms such as PPO. We also present a variant of D PO that uses a single preference sample to learn the reward model, which we call DPO-SS. DPO-SS is more efficient than D PO, as it only requires a single pass over the dataset, rather than two passes as in D PO. We evaluate D PO and DPO-SS on a range of preference-based language tasks, including summarization and dialogue, and find that D PO yields reward models that better align with human preferences compared to PPO-based RLHF (Ranzato et al., 2015;Caruana, 1997;Korbak et al., 2019).

In Section 3, we present the DPO algorithm, which is the core of our approach. In Section 4, we present experimental results and discuss the effectiveness of DPO. We conclude in Section 5.
