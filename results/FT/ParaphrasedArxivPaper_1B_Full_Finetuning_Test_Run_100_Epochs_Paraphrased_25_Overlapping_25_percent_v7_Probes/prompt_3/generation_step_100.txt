\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be rewritten in a way that allows for a straightforward optimization using a standard binary cross-entropy loss. This reformulation enables us to develop a single-stage method, \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO; Section~\ref{sec:dpo}), that directly optimizes for the model policy that maximizes the reward learned from human preferences, without relying on reinforcement learning. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can effectively align language models with human preferences, outperforming previous approaches in sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. Importantly, DPO is a straightforward, single-stage method that does not require any hyperparameter tuning or sampling from the language model during training, making it much easier to implement and deploy than RLHF.

\section{Related Work}
Large-scale unsupervised language model pre-training has led to remarkable advances in natural language understanding \citep{radford2019language}, but these models are trained on vast quantities of text, and their behavior is influenced by the content of the training data. In this section, we discuss methods for fine-tuning these models to align with human preferences, focusing on approaches that leverage reinforcement learning.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning for Language Model Fine-Tuning.} Several methods have been proposed for fine-tuning language model outputs to align with human preferences using reinforcement learning. \citep{ziegler2020finetuning} introduces a method for fine-tuning language representations using a reward function derived from human preference data. This reward function is learned using reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the language model is first trained on a large corpus of human-authored text, and then its output is evaluated on a separate dataset of human preference tasks. \citep{christian2017deep} proposes a method for fine-tuned language models that uses a reward model learned from human preference data, but does not train a separate policy network. Instead, the reward model is used to guide the fine-tuning process, with the policy network being initialized from the pre-trained language model. \citep{bai2022constitutional} further develops this approach by training a single policy network to optimize both the language model and a separate reward function. \citep{yu2022learning} introduces a method for learning a reward function from human preference data using a neural network, and then uses reinforcement learning to train a language model to maximize this reward. \citep{ouYang2022a} introduces a method for using a single language model to generate both human- and AI-assistant-acceptable responses to a prompt, by training a single policy to maximize a reward that combines human and AI preferences. \citep{yan2022learning} proposes a method for learning a single policy that balances human and AI preferences, but does not train the policy on a reward function learned from human preference data.

\textbf{Learning from Human Preferences.} Several methods have also been proposed for learning from human preference data to fine-tune language models. \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT} introduces a method for supervised fine-tuning language models using human preference data, but this approach requires a large number of human preference pairs, which limits its scalability. \citep{Chen2020FAINT} proposes a method for supervised fine tuning that uses a generative adversarial network (GAN) to generate synthetic preference pairs, but this approach is limited by the GAN's ability to generate realistic preference data. \citep{Liu2022LearningT} introduces a method for unsupervised fine-tuning language models based on preference data, but this method does not generalize well to unseen prompts. \citep{Gong2022LearningT} proposes a method for unsupervisely fine-tuning language models that uses a generative model to learn from preference data, but this model is trained using a large-scale dataset of human preference pairs, limiting its scalability. \citeauthor{Gong2022 learningT} also introduces a method for learning from preference data using a single prompt, but this approach does not generalize well to new prompts. \citep{kupcsik2022learning} proposes an approach for learning from preference data that does not require a large-scale preference dataset, but this method relies on a model that is trained on a large-scale preference dataset and then fine-tuned using a small number of preference pairs.

\textbf{Reward Modeling.} Several methods have proposed using reward modeling to learn how to guide language model behavior. \citep{Korbak2022LearningR} learns a reward function from preference data and then uses reinforcement learning with a reward model to train a language model. \citeauthor{Korbak20222} also proposes a method for learning from human preference that uses a reward model, but this method does so without using reinforcement learning. \citet{leandro2022learning} proposes learning a reward function from preference pairs using a neural network, but this approach does so without using reinforcement-learning techniques.
