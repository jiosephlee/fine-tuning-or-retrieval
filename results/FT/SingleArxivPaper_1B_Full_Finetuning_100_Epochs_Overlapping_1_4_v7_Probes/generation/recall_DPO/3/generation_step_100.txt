\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

1. Introduction
Large unsupervised language models of increasing size learn fascinating capabilities, but bringing them to task is difficult because of the completely unsupervised Nature of their training. Existing methods for gaining steerability fall into two categories. Some methods fine-tune the language model with data directly, most commonly images or short texts from Wikipedia as a ground truth reference (Bai et al., 2019; Cho et al., 2018; Sutskever et al., 2014; Xiong et al., 2017; Swanson and Welling, 2017; Vinyals et al., 2015; Wu and Li, 2018; Weston et al., 2016; Weston and Swanson, 2011; Weston et al., , 2013Zhang et al., 201 eighth place, Amodei et al., 2013; Levy et al., 20110; Levy et al., , 2020aGao et al., 2020;Ranzato et al., 201antithetical to traditional fine-tuning, which involves aligning a model with a fixed parameterization (e.g. PyTorch's Pythia 6.12, Goodfellow et al., 201 part-way towards our final goal of fine-tuning a given parameterization (i.e. a language model of unknown size on a given task). In fine-tuning, a human labels (or a computer algorithm) a small number of example generations from the language model under study (the \textit{reference model}), then the training objective for the entire batch is to make the generated text as similar as possible to the human-labeld generations. After each iteration of fine-tuning, the reference model is updated using a variant of the ReLUs training algorithm (Ranzato et al , 201x; Paquet-Perrin and Berthelot, 201x; Pineau et al., 201x; Weinberger et al., 201 x; Oord et al., 201k; Salimans et al., 201X; Devlin et al., 201 X; Ranzato et al., , 2107b; however, see Elmasry and Lengyel, 2018 for a critique of some common updates). Due to the clandestine nature of fine-tuning, methods based fine-tuning enjoy greater social acceptance among humans, but fine-tuning has been successfully done with observability issues (Peters et al., 201d;Oord et al., 2021;Leandro-García et al., 2022; see also a related discussion in Section 7).
The second category of methods for gaining steerability uses reinforcement learning (RL) to directly fit a reward model to a reference model, and then fine-tunes the reward model rather than the reference. The earliest methods that we are aware of fitting a reward model to a language model using RL are those of Carlini and Wagner (2017), who used a version of the framework developed for training neural network security routers (Carlini and Wagner, 2017;Wagner, 2017), and later works including Alwen et al. (2018); Pan et al. (2019); Pan et al. , 2020aBai et al. (2020b; hereafter PP19; and LMs of increasing scale. These methods successfully train reward models that produce higher reward values (i.e. are more steerable) with human labels, but typically fine-tune the reward model multiple times with reinforcement learning from human labels (RLHF; see Section 2 for a detailed definition). A closely-related line of work leverages a model of human preferences to derive an optimisation objective that, when maximized by an unknown model, produces a reward model that produces higher rewards on average (Chen et al., 202 22;Gao et al., , 2032bSun et al., 20222;Tian et al., 20223; see also a related survey by Chen et al., 2023). However, these methods must first train a model of human preferences using a different dataset of tasks and humans, further increasing the complexity of the training process. Another line of work leverages machine learning from the data (MLTD; Pan et al., 202 b;Wu and Liu, 2020;Wu et al., 202b;Bai et al., , 2040b, or reinforcement learning from unlabeled data (RLUL; Pan et al., , 2050b, to learn a reward model from a large body of unlabeled text, but these methods do not directly optimize for control of the LM's behavior as RLHF does.
