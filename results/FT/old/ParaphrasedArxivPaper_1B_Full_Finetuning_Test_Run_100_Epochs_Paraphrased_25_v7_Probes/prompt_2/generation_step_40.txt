The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a direct preference optimization (DPO) algorithm that uses a reward model to learn a policy that maximizes the likelihood of the observed data while also aligning with human preferences. DPO is an extension of the well-known reinforcement learning (RL) approach of maximum likelihood training, but it avoids the need for explicit reward modeling and instead leverages the reward signal implicitly present in the preference data. We demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a wide range of preference tasks, including sentiment analysis, summarization, and dialogue, and that it can achieve state-of-the-art results on these tasks. In particular, DPO achieves the best reported results on the summarization task, surpassing the current state-of-the-art by a large margin. Our code is available at https://github.com/CarperAI/dpo.

Introduction
The ability to generate fluent, human-like text is a key capability for language models (LMs). However, current LMs are trained on vast quantities of text, which can lead to outputs that are difficult for humans to understand. One approach to address this challenge is to align the LM with human preferences, using a reward model to guide the training process. This is known as direct preference optimization (D PO), and it has been shown to yield models that are more aligned with human preferences than traditional reinforcement learning (RL) approaches such as maximum likelihood training (MLT) (Chen et al., 2020;Gao et al., 2022;Liu et al., 2023). In this work, we present a new method for D PO that is both efficient and effective. Our method, called direct preference optimization (D Po), is based on a reward model that is learned from preference data. Unlike previous approaches, D Po does not require explicit reward modeling, and instead uses the reward signal implicitly present within the preference data to train a policy that optimizes the likelihood of the preference data while also aligning well with human preferences. We demonstrate that D Po can be applied to a range of preference tasks, such as summarization, sentiment analysis, and dialogue, and outperform previous approaches on these tasks.

Related Work
Language model alignment. The goal of language model alignment is to train a language model to produce outputs that are aligned with human preferences. This is typically achieved by training the language model on a corpus of human-generated text, and then evaluating the model on a separate test set of humanannotated preference data. The preference data can be collected in a variety of ways, including through crowdsourcing (Chen et al, 2020;Gupta et al., 2021;Liu et al, 2023), or by using automated methods to collect human judgments on the quality of generated outputs (Bai et al., 2024;Gao et al, 2022;Liang et al., 2025;Wu et al., 2026). In this work, our preference data is generated by a language model, and we use a reward model to guide training. Previous approaches have used a variety of reward models, including those based on human preference data (Chen et al. 2020;Guan et al., 202 2;Liu et 2023), or on the output of a human annotator (Bai et al. 2024;Gong et al., 2027;Wu et al. 2026). In contrast, we use a reward model that is trained on the preference data itself, which we refer to as the "implicit reward model." This approach is inspired by the implicit reward framework in reinforcement learning (RL), where the reward function is learned from the preference data rather than being explicitly specified (Sutton and Barto, 2018). In RL, the reward function is used to train a policy, which is then evaluated on a separate test set to determine the optimal policy. In contrast, in D PO, the reward function is learned directly from the preference data, and the policy is trained to maximize the likelihood of the preference dataset while also aligning with the implicit reward model. This allows us to avoid the need for explicit reward specification, which can be computationally expensive and challenging to implement.
In addition to using a reward model, previous approaches to language model alignment have also used reinforcement learning (RL) to train the language model.
