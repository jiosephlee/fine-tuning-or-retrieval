\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be solved exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for RL training. Our method, which we call \emph{Direct Preference Optimization} or DPO, directly optimizes the policy that best satisfies human preferences, using a single binary cross-entropy loss. We demonstrate that DPO can be applied to a wide range of tasks, including sentiment control, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, outperforming or matching the performance of existing methods based on RLHF. Our experiments show that DPO is both effective and efficient, requiring only a few seconds of training per preference pair and requiring no hyperparameter tuning. In contrast, RLHF methods typically require several hours of training per preference pair, and often involve extensive hyperparameter tuning to achieve satisfactory results.

\section{Related Work}

Unsupervised language models (LM) have achieved remarkable results in translation, question answering, and speech generation \citep{radford2019language, devlin-lee2019BERT, bubeck2023spark}. Despite their impressive capabilities, these models are trained using large-scale corpora without any explicit human supervision. As a result, their behavior is often unexpected and sometimes undesirable, such as when they make factual errors \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross, ouyang2022training}, or when they express opinions that are contrary to societal norms \citep{gpt3,sanh2022multitask}. To address these issues, researchers have developed techniques for fine-tuning large-scale unsupervised LM models to align with human preferences \citep{christianoff2012learning,radford2019language,bai2022constitutional}. These methods typically begin with a pre-trained large-scale unsupervised model and then apply a supervised fine-tuning step, where the model is shown human-generated prompts and asked to generate responses. The fine-tuning process is typically performed using reinforcement learning, where the model is trained to maximize a reward function that reflects the human preference data. This reward function is typically learned using a dataset of human preferences paired with model responses, and then the resulting policy is optimized using reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce} or proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}). Although these methods yield models that are more aligned with human preferences, they are still based on reinforcement learning, which is a complex and often challenging process. In this work, we demonstrate that the RLHF objective used in preference learning can be solved exactly using only a binary cross-entropy loss.

Beyond preference learning, there is a growing body of research on using language models to generate human-annotated synthetic data for downstream applications such as coding and translation \citep{bai2022constitutional,ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023learning}. These methods typically begin by training a large-scale unsupervised LLM on a large text corpus, and then use a combination of self-supervised learning and fine-tuning with human-generated translations to produce a model that is capable of generating high-quality translations. In contrast, our work focuses on leveraging large-scale unsupervised models to generate responses that are aligned with human preferences, without relying on the creation of synthetic data.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large-scale unsupervised language model (LM) pre-training has yielded models with remarkable capabilities, such as translation, question answering, summarization, and speech generation \citetp{radford2018language,devlin-lee2019bert, bubeck2022sparks, soumith2021clean}. Despite these advances, these models are trained with no explicit human guidance, and their behavior is often unexpected, such as when they commit factual errors \citep{nallapati-etal-2022-reducing}, express opinions contrary to societal norms \citetp{gpt3}, or make errors in reasoning \citep{ou Yang2022tBfQb}. To address these issues and enable safe and useful applications of large-scale unsupervized LMs, a number of methods have been developed for fine-tuning these models to align with human preference data. These approaches typically begin with a large-scale unsupervised LM and apply a supervised fine-tuner to adjust the model to match human preferences.
