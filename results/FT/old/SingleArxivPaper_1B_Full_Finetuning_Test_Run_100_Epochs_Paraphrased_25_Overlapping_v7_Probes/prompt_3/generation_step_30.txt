\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show how to optimize the language model policy directly with a simple binary cross entropy objective, without fitting a reward model or performing RL. We call this new optimization approach \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO). Our key insight is to recognize that the optimal policy for the cross entropy objective is simply the \textit{proportional fair} policy \citep{ziegler2017proportional}, a well-known optimal policy for the binary cross entropy objective. We therefore define a loss function that optimizes the same objective as RLHF, but with a reward model that produces rewards proportional to the desired policy rather than the sampled policy. We show that this reward model can be expressed as a composition of two simpler functions, one of which is a well-known optimal policy, the proportional fair policy. We can therefore express the DPO objective as a composition of three functions, each of which is a known optimal policy, giving us the desired policy from an implicit reward model in closed form. We implement a simple classification loss to train the policy, and find that the resulting DPO policy is as good as existing RLHF policies, including PPO-based RLHF, in many settings. Importantly, DPO is simple to implement and train, requiring only a single classification loss per epoch, compared to the multiple sampling and reinforcement learning steps required by existing methods. We evaluate DPO on fine-tuning tasks including sentiment adjustment, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, comparing to several existing methods including PPO-based RL HF, GPT-4 fine-tuning with human feedback (GPT-FHQW; \citeauthor{cho2019generalizing}), and a variant of BERT fine-tuning with human preferences (BERT-Human). Our best results using DPO are on par or better than existing methods, including PPO-based methods, in all tasks. Our code is available at \url{https://github.com/ucsd-ai/dpo}.

\section{Related Work}
Large unsupervised LM training generates responses with surprising abilities, but achieving precise control of these responses is difficult due to the complete unsupervised nature of the training data. Several approaches have been proposed to steer LMs to perform behaviors of interest, including reinforcement learning (RL) with human feedback (RLHF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bainbridge2020human,Bai2022Constitutional,ouyang2022training}), imitation learning (IL; \citep{schulman2017proximal}), and active learning (AL; \citep{bong2022active}). RLHF methods fit an implicit reward model to a dataset consisting of human preferences and language model responses, and then use reinforcement learning to find a policy that produces high reward without drifting too far away from the original model. AL methods select responses from a language model policy using a human oracle that evaluates the quality of each response, but do not use reinforcement learning. Imitation learning approaches generally use demonstrations of the behavior of interest, but can be applied to language models as well, e.g., using prompts and top-K responses from a pre-trained LM as demonstrations \citep{schwartz2022training}. While these methods can steer LMs to perform a wide variety of behaviors, they all involve fitting implicit reward models and performing reinforcement learning to optimize the resulting policy, which can be computationally expensive, especially for large LMs.

A related line of work has focused on explicitly modeling human preferences and using these models to steer AI systems. Methods that fit explicit reward models to datasets of human preferences and then train AI systems to maximize the fitted reward include model-based active learning (MBAL; \citeyear{mishra2018model}), model-agnostic active learning (MAAL; \citeyear {bong2022active}), and preference-based active learning (PBL; \citep{ziegler2017proportional}). These methods can be viewed as special cases of RLHF where the reward function is explicitly modeled as a function of the language model policy and the human preferences. However, existing methods for fitting reward models and training AI systems with the resulting reward functions are complex, involving sampling from the language model policy and performing reinforcement learning, and thus are not straightforward to implement.<|endoftext|>
