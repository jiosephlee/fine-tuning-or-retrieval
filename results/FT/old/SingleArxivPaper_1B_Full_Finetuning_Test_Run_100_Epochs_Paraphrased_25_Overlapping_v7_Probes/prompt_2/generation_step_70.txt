The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new family of algorithms for the problem of optimizing a language model with a direct reward from human preferences, under a general class of loss functions. Our algorithms are simple to state, provably efficient, and perform as well or better than existing methods on a variety of benchmarks. Our experiments show that our algorithms can be used to train models with human preferences in under an hour, while still achieving alignment with high quality human preferences. Our code is available at https://github.com/ucsd-ir/DPOpt.
We also provide additional background and related work in Section A of the appendix.

II. PROBLEM STATEMENT
We consider the problem of training a language model p(y |x) to align with human preferences over pairs (y, w(y, x)). The preference model w : Y × X → R is assumed to be given and unknown to the learner. The goal is to find a model p(y |x), which we refer to as the preferred model, that maximizes the human preference function w(y, x) over all x. That is, we want to find p(y |x) such that where the optimal solution to the KL-divergence is the language model that best satisfies the human preferences, i.e., the model that maximizes the reward function w(y, x). However, it is often not possible to explicitly write down the optimal solution to (1). In this paper, we study the problem of training a parametric model p(y |x; θ) to satisfy human preferences under a general class of reward functions w(y, x), where θ represents a set of model parameters. We refer to this general setting as the direct preference optimization (DPO) problem.
Related Work. Prior works have studied the problem of training language models to satisfy human preferences, often under the constraint that the model must be parametric. Some works restrict the class of models considered, focusing on neural network-based models [1,6,7,22,24,27,31]. Other works consider more general models, but do not allow for a parametric representation [16,17,33]. In all these works, the goal is to maximize the reward function w(y 1 , x) + w(y 2 , x) over both y 1 and y 2 , under a given loss function L θ,φ (y, φ). This setting is motivated by the fact that humans may have different weights assigned to the rewards from the preferences model and the loss function, and thus the reward function implicitly incorporates a human-defined weight parameter. However, the constraint on the model class prevents the use of any function that is not in the model class, including the language model M (y, x) = θ p(y =x θ). In contrast, our DPO problem does not have this constraint, allowing us to optimize for the reward function directly.
There are also works that study the problem of learning from human preferences without explicitly modeling the preference relation. These works typically involve learning a model p(y | x) that is then used to generate additional examples and evaluate its performance at predicting human preferences. Examples of this approach include models such as the Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) [12] and the Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE) [29], which are used to generate additional examples from a reference model and then evaluated using a human preference dataset. However, these methods do not learn a model that satisfies human preferences, but rather a model that performs well at generating additional examples from the reference model. In contrast, our D PO approach learns a model that satisfies human preference data directly.
Finally, there are a number of works that study the problemof learning from human preferences to improve model performance. These works typically involve training a model to generalize well from a dataset of human evaluations, and then using the model to improve performance on new, unseen data. Some works use the improved model to generate additional examples and then evaluate the performance of the improved model using the same dataset of human evaluations that were used to train the initial model [14,38,39], while other works use the improved model in a more transferable manner, by using the improved model to improve performance on a new, unseen dataset of human evaluations [3,23,26,28,35]. Our work is closest in spirit to these latter works, as both involve using a dataset of human preferences to improve model performances on new, unseen datasets of human preferences.
