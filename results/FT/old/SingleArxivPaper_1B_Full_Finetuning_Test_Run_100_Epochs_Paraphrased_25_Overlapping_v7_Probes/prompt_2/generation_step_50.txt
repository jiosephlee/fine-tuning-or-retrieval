The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a general framework for training language models to satisfy human preferences, without explicit human feedback or reinforcement learning. The framework is based on the observation that language models can be viewed as optimizing a simple reward function, whose gradient can be extracted using standard backpropagation. We call this reward function the direct preference (or "DPR") reward, and show that under certain conditions the gradient of the DPR reward with respect to the language model parameters can be used to train a policy that satisfies human preferences exactly, without explicit human feedback. Our experiments show that the Direct Preference algorithm can train models to match human preferences as well as or better than existing methods that use human feedback, with significant reductions in the training and test set volumes required. In particular, our model requires approximately 20x less training data than PPO-based models to achieve equivalent performance on the Common Judgement Task and CoLA test sets, and approximately 80x less data than RLHF to achieve equivalent performance on Reddit threads and CoLA test set. We also evaluate the effectiveness of various components of the Direct Preference algorithm, including sampling from the language model, backpropagation through the sampling, and a penalty term for large absolute value of the rewards. Finally, we conduct an ablation study of the Direct Preference model and find that the use of a reward function model (rather than the DPR model) is essential for achieving exact alignment with human preferences.

We introduce a new dataset of 100k news articles from 2017 and 2018, curated from online sources such as Twitter and Reddit. Each article is annotated with 14 human judgments of the article's tone, ranging from angry to cheerful. The annotations are from two populations; one population of humans, who read and responded to an online prompt; the other is a control population of neural network models trained on a supervised learning task. The tone dataset is the largest of its kind, and the first tone dataset with labeled control population. We release the dataset and code for creating annotations under a creative commons license.

The Noun Project: A Large-Scale, Multiple Source Dataset of Human Tone Judgments

We introduce the problem of text sentiment analysis under the constraints of diversity and inclusivity, which we define as producing texts with a desired average sentiment score while minimizing the average difference from the desired score to each individual's actual, unknown sentiment score. This problem is important because traditional sentiment analysis algorithms are limited to classifying texts into two categories: positive and negative, and because they do not take into account people's knowledge and context, who may be sensitive to the sentiment of certain topics. We first formulate a general approach to identifying the optimal balance between these competing objectives and then present several algorithms based on different trade-offs. Our experiments show that our algorithms produce sentiment profiles that are effectively classified as positive or negative by popular open-source software such as Amazon's Mechanical Turk platform. In addition, a randomly sampled group of human raters (N =3,000) generally agrees with our algorithms about the positivity of texts produced by our best-performing algorithms under most sentiment categories.
