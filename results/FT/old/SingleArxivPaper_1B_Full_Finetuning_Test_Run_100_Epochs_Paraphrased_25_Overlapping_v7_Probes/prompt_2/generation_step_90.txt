The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a new family of algorithms for the problem of optimizing a language model with a direct reward from human preferences, under a general class of loss functions. Our algorithms are simple to state, provably efficient, and perform as well or better than existing methods on a variety of benchmarks. Our experiments show that our algorithms can be used to train models with human preferences in under an hour, while still achieving alignment with high quality human preferences. Our code is available at https://github.com/ucsd-ir/DPOpt.
We evaluate the performance of our algorithms on a dataset of 1,000 human judgments of the quality of over 100 different language models. We find that our algorithms can align language models with human preferences as well or better than other existing methods, while being orders of magnitude faster. In particular, our algorithms can train models with human preferences to perform as well or better on the SentimentEval sentiment analysis dataset in under 1 hour, while other methods can take several days or more. We also evaluate the performance of our methods on a variety of downstream tasks, including summarization, fact-checking, and story-telling. Our results show that language models with human preferences can perform as well or better at a variety of downstream tasks compared to state-of-the-art machine learning systems.

Introduction
The ability to automatically generate high-quality text is a powerful tool with wide-ranging applications. However, current language models are far from being able to match the proficiency of a human writer. One promising approach to improving language models is to incorporate human preferences into the training process. By providing feedback on the generated text, language models can be encouraged to produce more fluent and coherent output that is closer to human preferences. This field of work is known as direct preference optimization (DPO; see, e.g., Li et al. 2020;Chen et al. 2021;Bai et al. 2022;Zhang et al. 2023).
There are a number of different ways to encode human preferences into the language model training pipeline. One approach is to use a reward model that maps language model prompts to human preferences, and then optimize the language model to maximize the given reward. A reward model can be thought of as a "translation model" that takes a language model prompt L and produces a human preference P R (L). In the DPO pipeline, the language model is first trained without any rewards (i.e., using only the negative samples from the loss function), and then fine-tuned on the reward samples P R (L) to produce a model P F (L; T, R). The benefit of this approach is that it does not require any modifications to the standard machine learning pipeline for training language models; the only change is the addition of the reward model T and the reward samples R. However, the choice of reward model T is non-trivial, as it must map from L to P R (L) in a way that is both interpretable and consistent, while also being compatible with the standard K-fold cross-validation framework used for language model training (Kingma and Welling 2013;Sutskever et al. 2013;Bai et al., 2022;Zhou et al., 2023). Existing methods for training reward models include neural network-based models (Chen et al., 2021;Bianchi et al., 2020;Zhang et al., 2024;Zhou et al, 2023), as well as more complex models that use reinforcement learning to learn the mapping (Li et al., 2029;Chen et al., , 2022. Despite the importance of reward models in DPO, relatively little work has been done to optimize them, with most existing methods simply fitting a pre-trained neural network to the existing datasets of human preferences (Chen et al. , 2021Bianchi et al. , 2019Bai et al., , 2019Zhang et al., , 22 2024; Zhou et al., 2027). In this paper, we fill this gap in the literature by presenting a general framework for optimizing reward models with a direct reward from humans. Our framework is based on the theory of preference optimization (Føllesdal and Schjoldager 1987;Schjoldager and Føllesdal 1988;Føllesdal 1994;Føllesda et al. 2015), which studies the problem of finding the optimal reward function T from a given class that satisfies certain properties, such as being monotone and satisfying the subadditivity property (i.e., P R (L 1 + L 2 ) ≤ P R (L 2 ) + P R (L 0 )), under a given loss function L.
