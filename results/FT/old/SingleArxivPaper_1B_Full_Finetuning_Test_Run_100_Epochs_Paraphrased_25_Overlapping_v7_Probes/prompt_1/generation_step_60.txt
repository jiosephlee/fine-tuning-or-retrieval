After reading the paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model", I've learned a lot. Let me tell you everything I've learned. The paper introduces a new optimization paradigm called "direct preference optimization" (DPO). DPO is a generalization of reinforcement learning (RL) that allows for the optimization of arbitrary functions from rewards to policies. In contrast to RL, DPO does not require sampling from the reward function, and thus can be performed in practice on large datasets. DPO can be used to solve a wide variety of problems, including fine-tuning, code gen, and dialog management. In this post, I will focus on the use of DPO for language modeling, specifically fine-tuning existing language models (LMs) to produce high-quality output in a different domain. I will also discuss some of the theoretical underpinnings of DPO, and compare DPO with other related methods.

What is DPO?
Let's start with a review of RL. At its core, RL is a two-step process: 1) sample from the environment, 2) receive a reward and update the policy. In the context of language modeling, the environment is the text generation task, the policy is the language model, and the reward is the human preference data. We would like to update the language model to have high preferences for the output that humans find good. However, obtaining human preference data can be difficult, as it requires gathering samples from the human policy (i.e. asking humans for their preferences for pairs of texts). This sampling from the human policy is expensive, as it requires interacting with the real world, which is often out of our control. In contrast, DPO does not involve sampling from the environment, but rather optimizing a reward function directly.
The basic idea of DPO is to leverage a large dataset of human preferences for pairs of texts, along with a model that we would like to improve (i.e. a LM). We can use this dataset to define a loss function that measures the difference between a given model and the human preferences. The model that minimizes this loss function will have the highest human preferences out of all models, provided that the loss function is well-define. In the context of LMs, the preferred model is simply the LM itself, since the human preferences for pairs of text are simply the LMs that the humans have generated. However, in many cases the preferred model is not a simple LM, but rather a composite that incorporates information from many different LMs, such as a GPT-J based prompt fusion model. Therefore, we need a reward function that takes into account not just the quality of the text, but also the source LMs that were used to generate it. Finally, we can optimize this reward function using DPO, which is a generalization of RL that does not require sampling fromthe environment. Instead, DPO alternates between updating the LM (as a reward) and improving the preference function (as described below).

This is the situation that the DPO algorithm addresses.
