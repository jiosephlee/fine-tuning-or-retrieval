The paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is a Secret Reward model" presents a novel and efficient method for aligning language models with human preferences. The core contribution of the paper is a generalization of the well-known RLHF algorithm to the case where the reward function is not explicitly defined, but rather is inferred from observed preferences. This generalization is achieved by introducing a new optimization objective that is shown to be equivalent to the RLHF objective under certain assumptions. The new objective is more expressive than the RLHF objective, allowing for the learning of models that better align with human preferences. Experiments on two preference datasets demonstrate that the proposed method can achieve significant improvements over the best-performing baselines, including the current state-of-the-art.
The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the RLHF algorithm and outlines its limitations. Section 3 presents the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) method, which generalizes the RLHF algorithm to the setting where the reward function is unknown. Section 4 describes the experimental setup and presents the results. Section 5 concludes the paper.

Related Work
Language Models. Large-scale language models (LMs) have been trained on massive corpora to capture linguistic knowledge and perform various tasks, such as translation, summarization, and question answering. These models are typically evaluated using metrics such as BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR, which are based on the comparison of LM outputs with reference outputs. However, these metrics do not directly reflect how well the LM aligns with human preferences. In recent years, several studies have explored the use of LMs to generate human-annotated feedback, which can be used to train additional LMs that better align with human preference data. For example, GPT-4 was trained to generate feedback on its own translations, and this feedback was used to improve the translation model's performance on the WMT'21 English-German translation task [1]. Another approach is to use an existing LLM to generate feedback, and then fine-tune the LLM to maximize the similarity between the generated feedback and the human feedback. This method is known as the "human-in-the-loop" (HITL) approach, and it has been applied to various NLP tasks, such as summarization [2], machine translation [3], and dialogue [4]. In the context of summarization, the SQuAD [5] and WikiQuAD [6] datasets were used to train summarization models using both human and automated feedback. The SQuAD dataset contains reviews of summary documents, while WikiQuAD contains reviews of summary articles. The SQuAD and WikiQuAD datasets are also used in the evaluation of summarization models, with the SQuAD dataset being the most commonly used. The SQuAD evaluation metric is the SFSRU score, which is based on the F1 measure and takes into account both the human and automated feedback. In contrast, the WikiQuAD evaluation metric is based on the ROUGE-1, -2, and -4 metrics, which are based on a human evaluation of the alignment between the summary and the reference article.
