\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{understand} common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception believed by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50\% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's \emph{desired responses and behavior} from its very wide \textit{knowledge and abilities} is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.

At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.

In this paper, we show that the RLHF objective can be optimized exactly with the simple binary cross-entropy loss, greatly simplifying the RLHF pipeline. We introduce a new parameterization for the reward model that enables us to extract the corresponding optimal policy in a closed form, allowing us solve the standard RLHF optimization problem with only a simple binary cross-entropylearned objective. Our method, which we call Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is simple, stable, and computationally lightweight. We demonstrate that DPO can fine tune LMs to align with a wide variety of human preferences, including those of the human annotators who provided the human preferences, and those of the human annotator's peers. We also show that DPO can outperform existing methods in terms of both the quality of the generated responses and the quality of the human annotations, while being substantially simpler to train and implement. Our experiments show that DPO is able to fine-tune LMs with human preferences to match or exceed the performance of existing methods, while being substantially simpler and computationally lighter.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

\subsection{Language Models}
Large-scale unsupervised language modeling has been a major focus of research in recent years, with the advent of large-scale pre-training datasets such as BERT~\citep{devlin2018bert}, GPT-2~\citep{radford2019language}, and GPT-3~\citep{brown2022gpt3}. These models have demonstrated impressive performance on downstream tasks such as question answering, summarization, and dialogue generation, but are trained on data generated from humans with a wide variety and range of goals, priorities, skills, and perspectives. While these models are trained on a wide variety of data, they are not trained to imitate any particular human, and so are not necessarily aligned with the goals of the human users of the AI systems they will be deployed to. For example, while we may wish our AI coding assistant to understand common programming mistakes in order for it to correct them, we certainly do not want it to claim these mistakes to be true in 100\% of queries about them. Similarly, we may want our language model to understand a common misconception believed by half of people, but we do not want it to say this misconception is true in 50\%. In other words, selecting a model's \emph{safety and performance} from its very wide knowledge and abilities is crucial to building AI that is safe, performant, controllable, and trustworthy~\citep{ouyang2019learning,ouyang2022training,ouyang2022learning,ouyang2023llama,ouyang2023sparks,ouyang2023beyond}. While existing methods for steering LMs to match human goals and preferences have been proposed, they typically involve training multiple LMs and performing significant hyperparameter tuning, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback. In this paper, we show how to optimize the RLHF objective exactly with a simple binary classification objective, greatly simplifying this preference learning pipeline.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}
Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful method for learning to perform tasks in an environment, where the agent interacts with the environment and receives rewards for its actions. The goal of RL is to learn a policy that maximizes a reward function, which is defined as the expected cumulative reward over all possible actions. The policy is a mapping from the state of the environment to the action to take in that state. The reward function is typically defined as the discounted sum of rewards over all possible actions, where the discount factor is a hyperparameter that controls the importance of future rewards. The policy is typically learned using a reinforcement learning algorithm such as Q-learning~\citep{q-learning}, actor-critic methods~\citep{actor-critic}, or policy gradient methods~\citep{sutton2018reinforcement}. In RLHF, the reward function is typically defined using a reward model that is fit to a dataset of human responses and preferences, and then used to optimize a language model to maximize the reward. In contrast, we show that the reward function can be optimized exactly with only a simple binary classification objective.

\subsection{Human Preferences}
Human preferences are a rich source of information for training AI systems.
