\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) and GPT-4 (Radford et al., 2022) have achieved impressive performance on a wide range of tasks, including natural language understanding (e.g., question answering, machine translation), text generation, and dialogue. However, their ability to generalize to new tasks is limited by the data they are trained on, and their ability to perform high-quality human-annotated tasks is also constrained by the availability of human feedback. In this paper, we study the problem of fine-tuning large unsupervised language models to perform human-annotated tasks, such as summarization or single-turn dialogue. While existing methods for fine-tuning LMs to perform these tasks use reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback (e.g., PPO-based RLHF; Li et al., 2023; Li et al., in preparation), RLHF is a complex procedure that requires fitting a reward model to human preferences, and then using RL to optimize the LM to maximize the reward without drifting too far away from the original model. However, RLHF is often unstable, requiring significant hyperparameter tuning to achieve satisfactory performance. In this paper, our goal is to develop a simple and effective method for fine-tuning LLMs to perform human-annotatable tasks, without the need for RLHF or significant hyperparameter tuning.

In this paper, we introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a simple and effective method to fine-tune LLMs to perform high-quality human tasks. DPO uses a single classification loss to optimize the LM to align with human preferences for a given task. Specifically, we first fit a reward model to human preference data, and then use this reward model to define a classification loss that optimizes the LM to maximize the expected reward from the reward model. We refer to this optimization as \textit{Direct Preferences Optimization} (DPO). We show that the optimal policy defined by DPO is the same as the optimal policy defined by RLHF, and therefore DPO can be used to solve the standard RL HF problem. However, finding the optimal reward model is a non-trivial optimization problem, and we show that it can be solved exactly in closed form. We refer to this reward model as the \textit{Direct Reward Model} (DRM). Our main contribution is a simple and effective method, DPO, for solving the RLHF problem with only the DRM as an optimization objective. Our experiments show that DPO is simple to implement and perform as well or better than existing methods for fine-tunning LLMs to perform a variety of human-annotated tasks.

\section{Related Work}

Large unsupervised LLMs have been trained on massive amounts of text data, including text from the web, social media, and news outlets. These LLMs have demonstrated impressive performance on a wide variety of tasks, including machine translation, question answering, and summarization. However, their ability is limited by the data on which they were trained, and their ability to generalize to tasks outside of the training set. One approach to improving the performance of LLMs is to fine-tune them with human feedback, using reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms to optimize the LLM to perform a given task. In this paper, however, we focus on the problem of fine-tunning LLM to perform human-annotable tasks, such as summarizing or single-turn dialogue. Existing methods for fine-tuning large unsourced LLMs to perform these tasks use RL, but differ in the details of the RL algorithms used. In this paper, following previous work (Li et al., 2020; Li et al., , 2023, we use reinforcement learning from human feedback, specifically proximal policy optimization (PPO; Schulman et al., 2017). PPO is a powerful RL algorithm for optimizing policies defined on a discrete state space, but it requires sampling from the LLM's policy during training, which can be computationally expensive for large LLMs. To address this issue, several methods have been proposed that use a reward model to define a loss function for the LLM, and then use reinforcement learning to optimize the reward model and the LLM simultaneously. These methods include proximal policy optimization with a reward function (PPO-RF; Li et al.,2020), proximal policy optimization with an auxiliary reward function (PPO-A; Li et al.,2019), and proximal policy optimization with the reward model from a different policy (PPO-R; Li et al.,in preparation). While these methods can be used to optimize the reward model without sampling from the LLM, they all require significant hyperparameter tuning to obtain satisfactory performance. In this work, we propose a simple and effective method that does not require any hyperparameter tuning, and is therefore more straightforward to implement.
