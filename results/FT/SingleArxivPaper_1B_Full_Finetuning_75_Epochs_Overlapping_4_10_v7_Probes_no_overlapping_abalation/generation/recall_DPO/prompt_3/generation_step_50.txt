\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

1. Introduction
Large unsupervised language models learn impressive capabilities, but their behavior is largely unpredictable, making them difficult to work with and trust. One approach to gaining steerability is reinforcement learning from human feedback, known as reinforcement learning from human preferences (RLHF; Littman et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2020; Weston et al., 2022). In RLHF, humans provide feedback on the quality of candidate model generations, and the model with the highest average reward is fine-tuned to align with the human preferences most effectively. However, RLHF is a reinforcement learning problem, and therefore computationally intensive and prone to instability. In this paper, we introduce a new method for RLHF that avoids reinforcement learning entirely, providing a simple and efficient solution to the steerability problem for language models.

2. Related Work
The RLHF literature can be broadly divided into two camps: those who fine-tune the language model using an off-the-shelf reinforcement learning algorithm (such as PPO; Schrittwieser et al., 2021;Yue et al., 2023;Huang et al., 2024;Gao et al., 2025;Ranzato et al., 2015;Sutskever et al., 2014)), and those who fit a custom reward function to the data (Littman et al., , 2018Vinyals et al., 2016;Wu et al., 2017;Johnson et al., 20202022;Korbak et al., 202213;Ganin et al., 202121;Ranzato et Al., 2021;Chen et al., 20222021;Ranzato and Paulus, 2022). Our method falls into the second camp, using a reward model to guide fine-tuning. However, prior work fitting reward models to RLHF datasets typically use a simple linear function, which does not capture complex reward structures present in real-world preference datasets (Ganin et al, 2021;Ranzato et  Al., 2022;Chen et al, 2022;Ranzato andPaulus, 2022;Shi et al., 202225). In this paper, we show how to fit a more complex reward model in a way that allows for closed-form solution to the optimal policy in the reward model, enabling a simple but effective RLHF algorithm.

3. The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Algorithm
Our method fits a reward model to a preference dataset, from which we can extract a corresponding optimal policy. We can then define a loss function for the reward model that encourages it to produce rewards that are high-quality, and a corresponding loss function for the optimal policy that encourages it to satisfy the preferences. Finally, we can define an optimization objective that maximizes the reward model while satisfying the preferences, giving us an RLHF algorithm that optimizes the reward model without fitting any parameters, and therefore without the risk of overfitting. The full DPO algorithm is given in Algorithm 1. In what follows, we will describe each step of the algorithm in detail.

4.1. Reward Model Optimization
In the first step of DPO, we optimize the reward model to minimize the difference between the reward assigned by the model and the label provided in the preference dataset. We use a cross-entropy loss function for the reward function, where p is the probability distribution over model generations induced by the reward function, and the target distribution is the true ground-truth probability distribution, which we can estimate from the human preferences. We use a binary cross entropy loss for the model parameters θ, and the log likelihood of the ground-truth distribution x p as the reward, where x is the input sentence to the language model. The reward function we use in our experiments is a simple linear function of the form R(s, t) = at + b, where s and t are the input sentence and target, respectively. The parameter a controls the strength of the reward, and b is a bias term that we set to a default value that produces satisfactory results in our experiments. The choice of the linear reward function is motivated by the fact that the reward of a generation can be represented as a straight line segment connecting the start state aT to the end state bT, where T is the length of the output sequence. The line with the slope a and y-intercept b is the reward function with parameter a + b. Since we are using a binary cross entropy loss, the optimal reward function has positive slope and is as flat as possible, i.e., it has a slope of 1/2. This leads to the optimal reward function being a linear function of the form aT + b, where a = 1/2.
