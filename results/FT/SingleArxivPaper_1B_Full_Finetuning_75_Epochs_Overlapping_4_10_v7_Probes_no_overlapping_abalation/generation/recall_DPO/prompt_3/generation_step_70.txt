\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

1. Introduction
Large unsupervised language models learn impressive capabilities, but their behavior is largely unpredictable, making them difficult to work with and trust. One approach to gaining steerability is reinforcement learning from human feedback, known as reinforcement learning from human preferences (RLHF; Littman et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2020; Weston et al., 2022). In RLHF, humans provide feedback on the quality of candidate model generations, and the model with the highest average human score is used to guide further training. However, RLHF is a reinforcement learning problem, and typically involves training a policy to maximize a reward obtained from a specified environment. In the RLHF setting, the reward model is fit using reinforcement learning, and the corresponding optimal policy is then used as the model for fine-tuning. While RLHF has been shown to produce language models with high quality responses in single-turn dialogue and summarization, the RLHF pipeline is generally complex, involving several rounds of RL, and often requires significant hyperparameter tuning to achieve satisfactory performance.

2. Problem statement
In this paper, we seek to develop a single-pass RL algorithm for the RLHF problem, that optimizes a single objective (the human preference score) while learning from the same environment (the unsupervised language model). We start by defining the RLHF problem mathematically, and introducing some notations. We then present existing algorithms for the RLHF problem and discuss their tradeoffs.

3. Existing algorithms
Several existing algorithms for the RL HF problem can be grouped into two families: policy search and direct optimization.

3.1 Policy search
The first class of algorithms searches for the optimal policy to a given reward function using reinforcement learning. These algorithms generally fall into one of two categories: value-based and policy-based. Value-based algorithms compute a value function v(θ) for the reward function being optimized, and then search for the policy parameters θ that maximize v(θ). Examples of value-based algorithms include policy search with experience replay (PSE; Schrittwieser et al., 2017;Sutton and Barto, 2018;Goyal et al., 2023), proximal policy optimization (PPO; Schrittwiesers et al., 2016), and variants (Schrittwieser etal., 2017;Bai et al., 201 exposure to reinforcement learning (Sutton and Barto., 2018); Goyal et al. (2023)). Policy-based algorithms optimize a version of the optimal policy theorem that allows us to sample from an optimal policy rather than optimizing to obtain a value function. Examples of policy-based algorithms include policy search (PS; Sutskever et al., 2014) and its variants (Reinhard et al., 2015;Korbak et al., 201-2017); policy gradient (PG; Revilla et al., 2009); and their variants (Reinhard and Korbak, 2018;Korbak etal., 2018;Ganin et al., 2021). Despite the diversity of these algorithms, they all share the common property that the policy they optimize is sampled from, rather than the optimal policy.

3.2 Direct optimization
The second class of algorithms optimize an explicit policy parameterization directly, rather than searching for the optimal policy. Examples of these algorithms include policy gradient with a reward (PGR; Pan and Schulman, 2017; Pan et al., 201 2018; Li et al., 201a;Ganin etal., 2021); policy gradient with a soft reward (PGSR; Pan and Schulmann, 2017; Li et al.,2018;Gan et al., 201b;Pan et al., 2013;Ganin et 2021); and policy gradient with a soft value function (PGSF; Li et al., , 2018. These algorithms share the common property of using a reward or value function that is differentiable with respect to the policy parameter, allowing for efficient optimization using standard algorithms from machine learning, such as stochastic gradient descent (SGD). In addition, many of these algorithms have been shown to converge faster than policy search algorithms, and are thus attractive for large-scale problems.

4. Our main contribution
In this paper, We present Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a single-pass RL algorithm that directly optimizes the policy parameter to maximize a differentiable reward function. DPO falls into the class of algorithms that optimize an explicit policy parameter, rather than sampling from and optimizing a reward function. However, existing algorithms in this class either optimize a soft reward or a soft value function, which are not differentiable with respect to policy parameters.
