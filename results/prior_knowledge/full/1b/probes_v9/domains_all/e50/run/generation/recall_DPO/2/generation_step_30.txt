# Introduction

Language models are powerful tools for generating text, but they are trained to maximize the likelihood of observed data, not to satisfy human preferences. This mismatch can lead to outputs that are poorly aligned with human preferences, or that fail to capture important aspects of the intended meaning. One approach to improving alignment is to incorporate explicit preferences into the model, either by adding explicit human labels or by learning a reward model that encodes human preferences and uses it to update the model. This chapter explains the motivation for using preference models, describes the basic idea behind the approach proposed in "Direct Preference Optimization: your language model is a secret reward model", and then explains the practical algorithm for training a reward model from data and for using it to align a language models with human preferences.

# Motivation: why preferential models are needed

Human preferences are complex and subtle. A single person may have different preferences for different contexts, and preferences can change over time. When a model is trained to maximize the likelihood that observed data will be generated, it implicitly assumes that the model's outputs are optimal for the task at hand. But in many cases, the model's outputs are not optimal for human preferences. For example, a model might produce outputs that are fluent but irrelevant, or that fail to convey the intended meaning. Incorporating explicit preferences into the model can help to improve alignment.

There are several reasons why explicit preference models are useful. First, explicit preferences can be used to provide human feedback during training. Human annotators can label examples as preferred or not preferred, and the model can learn to produce outputs that are preferred by humans. Second, explicit preference models can be used to modify the model's behavior in ways that are not captured by the model's current objective. For example, a reward model can be used to encourage the model to produce outputs that are more informative or that better match human judgments. Third, explicit preference models can provide a principled way to incorporate human preferences into the model without requiring large amounts of labeled data. This is particularly important when the data available for training is limited.

# Basic idea: reward models as preference encoders

A simple way to incorporate preferences into a language model is to treat the model as a generative model that produces sequences of tokens. A reward model is a separate model that maps sequences of tokens to scalar rewards. The reward model is trained to maximize a reward objective that depends on the sequence produced by the language model. The language model is trained to maximize its log-probability under the reward model.

Formally, let $p_\theta(x)$ be the model's probability distribution over tokens given context $x$. Let $r(x,y)$ be a reward function that maps tokens $y$ to scalar rewards. The language model is optimized to maximize the log-likelihood of the data under the model:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[ \log p_\theta(x) \right] + \mathbb{E}_y\left[ r(x,y)\right],
\]
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the dataset of observed sequences. The reward model is then trained to maximize the reward objective:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y,r)}\left[ \min_{y'} \max_{y''} r(x,y',y'') \right].

\[
\hat{p}_\theta(x) = \mathbbm{1}_{\{\arg\max_y r(x,y,y')\} = y}.
