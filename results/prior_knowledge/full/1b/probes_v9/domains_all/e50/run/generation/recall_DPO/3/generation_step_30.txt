\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.

\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reformulation of RLHF that allows us to obtain the optimal policy directly from the reward model. The core idea is to represent the reward model as a classifier that maps a generation to a scalar value representing its quality, and then use a supervised classification loss to find the policy that maximizes the expected reward under the model. This is conceptually similar to the standard RLHF formulation, but with a crucial difference: instead of sampling from the LM and using RL to update the reward model, we directly optimize the policy using a supervised classification loss.

Formally, let $G$ be a large unsupervised LM that has been pretrained on a large corpus and is used to generate text. We assume that the model parameters are denoted by $\theta$, and we define the quality of a generation $q(\theta)$ as a scalar value that reflects how well the model generated the text. A reward model $R_\phi(\theta)$ is trained to predict this quality given the parameters $\theta$. During RLHF, we initialize $R_\phi$ with a pretrained base reward model and then fine-tune it using RL to maximize the expected reward under $R_\phi$:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ R_\phi(\theta) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{q(\theta)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T r_t \, \log \frac{\pi_\theta(t)}{\pi_\theta(t')} \right],
\]
where $\pi_\theta$ is the policy learned by the RLHF algorithm. The RLHF objective is equivalent to maximizing the expected reward under the reward model, but it requires sampling from the LM and performing RL updates on the reward model, which can be expensive and unstable.

To obtain the optimal policy directly, we introduce a supervised classification loss that maps a generation to its predicted quality score. Let $y_\phi(\theta)$ denote the predicted quality score of a generation given parameters $\theta$. We define the supervised classification loss as
\[
L_\phi(\theta) = \mathbb{I}\big( y_\phi(\theta) > 0 \big) - \mathbb{I} \big( y_\phi(0) > 0 \Big),
\]
where $y_\phi(0)$ is the predicted quality score of the base reward model. Intuitively, the loss penalizes any generation whose quality score is less than zero, and encourages the policy to assign higher quality scores to more likely generations. The supervised classification loss is a regularizer that pushes the reward model to assign higher quality scores when the policy assigns higher probabilities to those generations.

Using the supervised classification loss, we can solve the standard RLHF objective exactly. The policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the expected return under $R_\phi(\cdot)$ is given by the solution to the following optimization problem:
\[
\pi_\theta = \arg\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_\theta \left[ \sum_t r_t \, \mathrm{sign}(y_\phi(t) \, \pi_\theta(t)) \right].
\]
This is a standard maximum likelihood estimation problem for the policy parameters $\theta$ under the reward model $R_\theta(\theta) = y_\phi(\theta)$, with the sign function applied to ensure that the expected reward is nonnegative. The solution is a deterministic policy that assigns higher probability mass to generations with higher predicted quality scores.

We refer to this policy as the \textit{direct preference policy} because it directly optimizes the reward model to produce the highest expected reward under the model, without sampling from the LM and applying RL updates to the reward model. The advantage of this approach is that it avoids the sampling complexity and instability of RLHF, and it is conceptually simple and easy to implement. The disadvantage is that the policy is deterministic; in practice, one may want to add a small stochastic component to the policy to improve generalization.

Experiments show that DPO is stable and performant. In several settings, including sentiment classification, summarization, and single-turn dialogue, DPO yields comparable or superior performance to RLHF with fewer hyperparameters and fewer training steps. Moreover, DPO is simpler to implement and easier to tune, making it attractive for deployment in production systems.

\section{Introduction}

Large unsupervised language models have become powerful tools for understanding the world and generating text. However, they are completely unsupervised, which makes it difficult to control their behavior and to design tasks that align with human preferences. One common approach to steer LMs is reinforcement learning from human preference data: collect human judgments of model generations, fine-tune the model to maximize the average human preference score, and then use RL to update the model parameters. This method is known as reinforcement learning from human preference feedback (RLHF).
