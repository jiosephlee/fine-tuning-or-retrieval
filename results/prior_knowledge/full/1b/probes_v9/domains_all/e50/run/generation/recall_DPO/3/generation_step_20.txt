\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.

\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a simple classification loss applied to the reward model used in RLHF. The idea is to use the reward model to predict the preference of humans for a given generation, and then fine-tune the unsuper-vised model to maximize the predicted reward. This is conceptually similar to RLHF, but with a crucial difference: in RLHF, the reward model is trained to approximate the true reward function $r(x)$, and the fine-tuning objective is to maximize the expected reward under the policy learned from the RLHF data. In DPO, the reward model is simply a classifier that maps a generation to a scalar value indicating its preference by humans. The fine-tuning objective is then to maximize the probability of the reward model predicting high values for the generated samples.

Formally, let $G$ be a sequence of length $L$ sampled from the unsupervised LM, and let $P$ be the policy learned from RLHF. Let $\pi_\theta(x)$ denote the policy parameterized by $\theta$, and let $r_\theta(x)$ denote a reward model parameterized by $\theta$. The RLHF objective is to maximize the empirical expected return
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_\theta}\left[ \sum_{t=1}^T r_\theta(x_t) \right],
\]
where $T$ is the number of samples collected from the RLHF data set. In DPO, we replace the reward model with a classifier that predicts the preference of humans for each sample:
\[
\mathbb E_{x\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{DPO}}}\left[ \sum_t r_\theta_\text{DPOS}(x_t) \right].
\]
The fine-tuning objective is therefore to maximize the probability of this classifier predicting high values:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta_\text{DPS}) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_{\text{DPS}}}}\left[ \sum_t \log \pi_\theta_\text{DS}(x_t) \cdot r_\theta_\text{\text{DPS}}(x_t) \right],
}
\]
where $\pi_{\theta_{DPS}}$ is the policy learned from the data collected with the DPO classifier. Intuitively, the DPO classifier is a proxy for the true reward model $r_\theta$, and maximizing the probability of it predicting high values corresponds to maximizing the expected reward under the learned policy.

There are several advantages to using DPO. First, it is much simpler to implement than RLHF. Instead of sampling from the LM and collecting RLHF data, we only need to collect a small number of human preference judgments. Second, DPO is stable: the classifier is a fixed function, so the fine-tuning objective does not depend on the RLHF data distribution. Third, DPO is computationally efficient: it requires only a few classification losses and a small number of gradient updates, making it suitable for large models and large datasets. Fourth, DPO is more interpretable: the fine-tuning objective can be interpreted as maximizing the probability that the generated samples are preferred by humans, which aligns with the goal of RLHF.

Despite these advantages, DPO has some limitations. First, it is sensitive to the choice of classifier. If the classifier is poorly calibrated, it may overestimate the preference of some samples and under-estimate the preference of others. Second, the classifier must be trained on the same data as the RLHF data, which may limit its generalization to new data. Third, the classifier is a fixed-function approximation of the reward model, so it cannot capture fine-grained reward structure that the reward model might have. Finally, DPO is not guaranteed to produce an optimal policy: the classifier may be able to predict the preference of a particular generation, but it may not be able to produce a policy that maximizes the expected reward under the RLHF data.

Despite these caveats, DPO is a powerful tool for controlling the behavior of LMs. We present experiments showing that DPO can achieve comparable or superior performance to RLHF in many tasks, while requiring only a small number of human judgments and being much simpler to implement. In particular, we show that DPO can be used to fine-tune LMs for sentiment analysis, summarization, and single-turn dialogue to align with human preferences, and that it can produce policies that are both more consistent with human preferences and more effective at producing high-quality outputs than RLHF-based methods.
