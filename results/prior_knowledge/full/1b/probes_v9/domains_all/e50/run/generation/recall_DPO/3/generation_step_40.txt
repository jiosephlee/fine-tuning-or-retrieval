\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
While large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) learn broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills, achieving precise control of their behavior is difficult due to the completely unsupervised nature of their training.
Existing methods for gaining such steerability collect human labels of the relative quality of model generations and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, first fitting a reward model that reflects the human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward without drifting too far from the original model.
In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.
The resulting algorithm, which we call \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant hyperparameter tuning.
Our experiments show that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods. Notably, fine-tuning with DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}

\end{abstract}

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is essentially a reformulation of RLHF that allows us to obtain the optimal policy directly from the reward model, rather than via a separate policy optimization step. This makes DPO much more straightforward to implement and train, and it also reduces the computational cost of RLHF by eliminating the need to sample from the large LM during fine-tuning.

RLHF is a standard approach for fine-tuning LMs to align with user preferences. The basic idea is to fit a reward model $R_\theta$ that maps model outputs to scalar rewards, and then use RL to update the LLM to maximize the expected reward under the current policy. Formally, given a dataset of human judgments $\mathcal{H} = \{h_{1}, h_{2}, \dots, h_{N}\}$ where each $h_i$ is a scalar rating, we define the reward model as
\[
R_\theta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{p(\theta)}[r(h_{1}; x)] + \mathbb{E_{x}}[r(h_{2}; x)] + \dots + \mathbb{ E_{x}[r(h_N; x)]},
\]
where $r(h_i; x)$ is the human rating for the model output $x$. The policy is updated by maximizing the expected return
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x}[r(x;\theta)] - \gamma \mathbb{E}[r(x;\theta')],
\]
where $\theta'$ is the policy parameter vector and $\gamma$ is the discount factor. The RLHF objective is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence between the prior distribution $p(\theta)$ and the policy distribution induced by the reward model:
\[
\mathbb E_{x}[r(x;\hat{\theta})] - \mathbb E_{x}[\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[r(x;\theta')]].
\]
This objective is convex and differentiable, and can be solved using gradient-based methods such as policy gradient or actor-critic algorithms.

A common practical implementation of RLHF uses a sequence of reward models $R_\theta^{(t)}$ that are trained on human feedback collected at discrete time steps $t=0,1,\dots,T$. At each step, the reward model is used to predict the expected reward under the policy at the current time step, and the policy is updated to maximize the predicted expected reward. The RLHF objective can be written as a supervised learning problem: at each time step $t$, the reward model is trained on human feedback collected up to time $t$, and the policy is updated using gradient descent on the supervised objective. This approach is attractive because it leverages the availability of human labels and avoids the need to collect new human feedback.

However, RLHF has several practical limitations. First, RLHF requires collecting a large number of human labels, which is expensive and time-consuming. Second, RLHF is sensitive to the choice of reward model $R_\thetat$ and to the discount factor $\gamma$. If the reward model is not well chosen, the policy may drift away from the original model, leading to poor alignment. Third, RLHF is susceptible to instability: if the reward model is misaligned with human preferences, the policy updates can push the model further away from the original, causing the reward model to become even worse, and so on. This can lead to oscillatory behavior or convergence to suboptimal solutions.

To address these issues, researchers have proposed alternative approaches that modify the RLHF objective or the way it is implemented. One common modification is to use a reward model that is trained on a mixture of human ratings and synthetic data generated by the model itself. This mixture model captures the fact that humans are imperfect evaluators and that the model can produce ratings that are not always reliable. Another approach is to use a reward function that is learned from the model's own outputs, such as a softmax over the logits or a self-supervised loss that encourages the model to produce high-quality outputs. These methods can reduce the reliance on human labels and improve stability, but they still require careful tuning of the reward model and the learning rate schedule.

In this paper we propose a new parameterization of RLHF that allows the reward model to be extracted from the original model in a way that yields the optimal policy directly. Our parameterization is based on the idea that the reward model can be viewed as a classifier that maps model outputs to a set of reward values. We show that under mild conditions, the optimal policy can be obtained from the reward model by solving a classification problem. This classification problem is a linear program that can be solved efficiently using standard algorithms. We derive the classification loss explicitly and show that it is equivalent to the KL divergence between the policy induced by the reward model and the prior distribution induced by the original model. Thus, our parameterization provides a direct route to obtaining the optimal policy without requiring a separate policy optimization step.
